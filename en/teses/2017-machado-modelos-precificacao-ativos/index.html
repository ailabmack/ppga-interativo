The market and the dollar best explained Brazilian stocks

The thesis compared 15 models (1 to 8 factors) explaining the monthly ups and downs of 259 stocks, 1996–2016. The leanest model that did the job won: market return plus the dollar. The old CAPM, market alone, beat the classic models with more factors. In 11 screens.

02 The question

Eight candidates to explain why stocks go up and down

An asset pricing model is like a recipe: it combines a few ingredients (the factors) to explain a stock’s return. The thesis tested eight, from three families. Tap a jar.

Market Type of company (Fama and French) Economy (APT) Dollar (the author’s addition)

All factors enter as the month’s surprises, standardized (mean zero, standard deviation one), as APT theory requires (section 5 of the thesis).

03 The models

The thesis tested 15 recipes, from 1 to 8 ingredients

Three recipes are classics: the CAPM (the market alone), the Fama and French 3 factors and the APT with four economic factors. The others mix ingredients, up to the recipe with all eight.

Each recipe was estimated with and without a constant (a fixed return of each portfolio’s own), for three periods. Figure 14 of the thesis.

04 How it looked

05 How to compare

The yardstick rewards fewer errors and charges a fine for each extra ingredient

Adding one more ingredient to a recipe never worsens its fit to the past, even if the gain is just chance. That is why the thesis uses the BIC criterion: the score adds the model’s error and a fine for each estimated number. Lower score is better.

score = error + fine
each extra factor = 13 new numbers (one per portfolio) × 5.53 points

We split each model’s error and fine from the thesis’s scores (Figures 15 and 16): fine = estimated numbers × ln(252 months). Our calculation.

06 The result · 1996–2016

Market and dollar came 1st; the CAPM came ahead of the classics with more factors

Over the 21 years, the two-ingredient recipe (market + dollar) had the lowest score. Behind it, practically tied, market + size and the CAPM. The Fama and French 3 factors and the APTs came later.

08 The unexpected

The size factor tied with the CAPM by 0.03 points

Adding company size to the recipe reduced the error by almost exactly the fine it cost. On the milder yardstick (AIC), which fines less, size pulls clearly ahead.

09 Sector by sector · 1996–2016

When the dollar rose, mining and paper rose with it; finance, metals, energy and construction fell

In the winning model, each portfolio has two weights: how much it moves with the market and how much it moves with the dollar. The market pulls all of them the same way; the dollar splits the sectors.

10 The other end

Without the market return, the recipes went to the back of the line

The classic APT, with only output, interest, credit and inflation, came last. Over the 21 years, only 3 of the 52 links between these factors and the portfolios had p below 0.10.

11 So what? Limits and credits

The CAPM is still useful, and the dollar deserves a place in the Brazilian recipe

For finance students and teachers

  • The market return is indispensable: without it, every model goes to the back of the line. (author)
  • More factors does not mean a better model; the yardstick must charge for excess. (author)

For analysts and companies

  • The CAPM remains a practical tool for expected returns, and that use is where improvement is worth seeking. (author)
  • In Brazil, looking at how much each sector moves with the dollar can complete the risk picture (screen 09). (our reading)

Next steps

  • Test other factors: household consumption, oil prices, unemployment, BNDES (development bank) lending. (author)
  • Split electricity from oil, and banks from insurers, into their own portfolios. (author)
Not investment advice. The thesis explains past ups and downs (1996 to 2016) of sector portfolios; it does not forecast returns or simulate portfolios or trading costs.

What this research does not say

  • Explaining is not forecasting. The models describe how portfolios moved with the factors in the same month; they do not give tomorrow’s return. · our note
  • A ranking, not a test of difference. The yardstick orders the models; gaps of a few points, like the tie on screen 08, do not set one model apart from another. · our note
  • Sector portfolios, not individual stocks. The results apply to the 13 average portfolios of each sector. · our note
  • One version of the CAPM. The conditional and intertemporal CAPM, the Fama and French 5-factor model and stocks outside the São Paulo exchange were left out. · according to the author

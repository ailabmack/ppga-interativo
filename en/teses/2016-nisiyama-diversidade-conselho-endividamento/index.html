Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

Companies with more varied boards used a little more debt

In 100 companies among the most traded on the Brazilian stock exchange, from 2010 to 2014, the thesis gave each board of directors a diversity score (structure, personal profile, education and experience). Higher scores came with more debt the following year, on all four measures used, by a small amount. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem · who decides on debt

The board watches over management, and debt is one of the tools of that oversight

Every listed company in Brazil must have a board of directors. It sits between the owners of the money (the shareholders) and those who use it day to day (the executives), and major financing decisions usually go through it. The thesis asked whether a board made up of more varied people changes that decision. See the three roles:

Sections 1.1, 2.2, 2.3 and 2.7.5. Mandatory board in listed companies: Brazilian Corporations Law 6,404/76, art. 138. The idea of debt as discipline comes from Jensen (1986), cited by the thesis. The thesis’s hypothesis: “there is a positive relationship between overall board diversity and the firm’s level of debt”.

03 How the research looked

100 large listed companies, five years of boards read one by one

The author took the 100 companies with the highest trading volume on BM&FBovespa (now B3) in 2013 and 2014, excluding banks and other financial firms. Accounting figures came from the Economatica database. The data on each board member (age, education, career, other boards) were collected by hand from the Reference Forms filed with the CVM, Brazil’s securities regulator, and completed from company websites and biography databases. 05 How the research looked · the yardstick

Diversity became a score of up to 37 points, adding up ten items in three families

For each item, the boards in the sample were split into four bands (quartiles), from least to most varied, worth 1 to 4 points. The exception is the CEO: 1 point if the CEO does not chair the board. 06 What it found · the change

Boards became slightly more varied, pulled by separating the chair from the CEO

The average score rose from 22.2 in 2010 to 23.2 in 2014. The structural part and the education-and-experience part grew; the social part (age, gender, nationality) barely moved from 2011 on. The biggest change was taking the CEO out of the board chair. Pick a line:

Appendix C (yearly percentages) and section 4.1 (average score 22.2 → 23.2, Figure 2). Vertical axis from 0 to 100% for every line, so as not to exaggerate small changes.

Each measure answers a different question about how much the company relies on other people’s money. The bar shows the average company in the sample. Switch the measure:

08 What it found · the answer

A higher diversity score came with more debt the following year

Comparing each company with itself over the years (and accounting for profit, size, growth and other factors), the board’s score in one year moved with the following year’s debt, always upwards. The hypothesis was confirmed. But the size is modest. Move the score and watch the average company change:

09 What it found · the parts

Broken into parts, the one that shows up most is the variety of education and experience

The author also tested the three families separately and redid everything in two ways to see whether extreme cases changed the result. The overall score held up almost every time. Among the parts, the occupational one (education and experience) lights up most; the structural one (independent members, CEO out of the chair) never lit up. Switch the version and tap a cell:

11 The turning point · the explanation

For the author, a varied board watches more closely and pushes executives out of their comfort zone

Executives who are not closely watched tend to avoid debt: it forces them to pay interest and takes money out of the cash they could use as they please. The thesis reads the result this way: people with different perspectives on the board watch more closely, and the company ends up using more debt. See the three readings the thesis offers:

Sections 2.7.5, 4.2.5 and 5. The readings are the author’s and those of the studies he cites (Jensen, 1986; Berger, Ofek and Yermack, 1997; Hillman and Dalziel, 2003); the thesis measures the association, not the mechanism. The drawing is our illustration, not to scale.

12 So what? · limits and credits

Who sits on the board also matters for how the company finances itself

For companies and shareholders

When putting a board together, look beyond gender: different ages, nationalities, education and experience. For the author, a varied board can mean closer oversight and more confident, even more aggressive, investment and financing decisions. (author)

For lenders and analysts

Board composition can help explain why similar companies use different levels of debt. The average effect is small: a much more varied board (3.3 points higher, one standard deviation of the score) comes with 1 to 3 percentage points more debt. (our calculation from Tables 1 and 2)

For research

The ten-item yardstick in three families is, according to the author, the thesis’s first contribution. Next steps: methods that better handle cause and effect (dynamic panels, simultaneous equations), each company’s context and links to the psychology of groups. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Moving together does not prove cause. Board and debt influence each other; using the previous year’s score reduces this problem but does not remove it. · according to the author
  • It holds for large listed companies. The sample was chosen by trading volume, not at random, and does not extend to privately held companies. · according to the author
  • Data declared by the companies. Errors in filling in the Reference Forms can distort the score. · according to the author
  • A relative score. Points depend on this sample’s bands: a “4” board here is among the most varied of these 100, not by a fixed standard. With year controls, the effect on debt/EBITDA is no longer significant. · our note (Appendix B and section 4.2.2)
  • The mechanism was not measured. Closer oversight is the proposed explanation, backed by the literature; the thesis does not measure oversight. · our note

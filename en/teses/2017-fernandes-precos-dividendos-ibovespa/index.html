Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2017

Over 30 years of the Ibovespa, prices and dividends moved together, but not as in a rational market

If a share’s price is today’s value of the dividends it will pay, the two follow precise rules. The thesis tested those rules on the Ibovespa, Brazil’s main stock index, from 1986 to 2016, in two ways. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The question · the present value model

A share is worth what it will pay, brought back to today

Think of a hen that lays one egg per quarter, forever. What is she worth? It depends on how much you require to earn per quarter for waiting for the eggs. Shares work the same way: the eggs are the dividends, and the price should be today’s value of all of them.

This is Campbell and Shiller’s (1987) present value model, the yardstick of the thesis. If investors set prices this way, with rational expectations, prices and dividends stay tied to each other by rules that can be tested.

The hen is our analogy. The calculation (value = dividend ÷ rate) is the one the thesis uses to link the multiplier to the discount rate (section 3.2.1).

03 The concept · efficient market hypothesis

In an efficient market, the price already tells all that is known, and only new information moves it

If no one has an information edge, there is no easy profit left: the price stays close to fair value and surprises are unpredictable. The literature distinguishes three versions, depending on the information the price already carries.

Malkiel and Fama (1970) and Fama (1991), as reviewed in the thesis. According to the thesis, empirical work usually tests the semi-strong form.

04 The problem · studies in other markets

In other markets, the choice of test changes the answer

In its review, the thesis brings together two studies asking whether prices move like a “random walk” (no pattern to predict). In Asia, three tests find a pattern in all 14 countries, and a fourth (ADF) finds none in any of them.

That is why the thesis does not rely on a single path: it tests the present value model with the classic method and with a more recent alternative test.

05 How the research looked · data

The author rebuilt 30 years of the Ibovespa, share by share, to separate prices and dividends

The Ibovespa is the portfolio of the most traded shares on the exchange. Using the daily returns of every share that was ever in it, with and without payouts, the author isolated how much came from dividends and how much from price, and grouped it all into quarters, net of inflation.

06 What it found · classic test, step 1

Prices and dividends wander, but like an owner and a dog on a leash: they never drift far apart

Each series on its own has no level it returns to (it has a “unit root”). But a combination of the two always comes back near its average: this is cointegration, the model’s first requirement. It passed.

07 What it found · classic test, step 2

The price is worth 80 to 130 quarterly dividends, depending on the calculation

This multiplier says how many “eggs” fit into the price and, turned upside down, which discount rate the market uses. The thesis tried three ways to estimate it; the third, from the Ibovespa’s average return, did not form a stable combination and was dropped.

Tables 7, 8, 9 and 10; correlations in section 3.2.1. Years of dividends = multiplier ÷ 4, our calculation.

08 What it found · who anticipates whom

The price should anticipate dividends, and only that; in all three versions, it went both ways

If the price is a forecast of dividends, the price gap should help predict the dividends to come. The reverse should not happen. The Granger test found both arrows, so the thesis calls the result inconclusive.

Tables 13 and 19. In the interest-rate version, the thesis uses the dividend/price ratio and real interest rates (Selic) minus dividend growth; it notes that this test is a weaker implication of the model.

09 The finding · Johansen and Swensen (1998) test

In the alternative test, the rational price model is rejected by a wide margin, for any discount rate

Instead of looking at one arrow at a time, this test checks all the model’s rules together. It had not yet been applied to Brazilian data. The calculation yields a number; the larger it is, the farther the data are from the model.

With discount factors from 0.95 to 0.99 per quarter, the number ranged from 122 to 144. If the model held, going above 15.5 would already be rare (5 times in 100).

Table 20 (chi-square with 8 degrees of freedom, p-value 0.0000 in all cases). The 15.51 threshold (5%) and the curve are our calculation from the same distribution.

10 The unexpected · same exchange, a different conclusion

With the same exchange and more than twice the time, the conclusion of a 2001 study was reversed

Anchite and Issler (2001) used the Ibovespa from 1986 to 1998 and concluded that the data favored rational prices. This thesis followed the same script with 30 years of data and reached the opposite, as Campbell and Shiller (1987) did in the United States.

11 So what? · limits · credits

Prices and dividends are linked, but stock prices carry more than the dividend math

Investors

In the long run, prices and dividends do not let go of each other; along the way, the price drifts from what dividends would justify. That does not mean easy profit: the thesis tests no strategies and no costs. · our reading

Market watchers

Rule changes move the data: after 1996, with interest on equity, companies began paying more to shareholders. Comparing periods requires taking this into account. · our reading

Researchers

The author suggests modeling the 1996 shock, testing the cointegrated VAR of the original series and looking at behavioral finance, with investors who follow fundamentals and others who follow charts.

Teachers and students

A good example that the answer depends on method and period: the same model, on the same exchange, passed in one study and was rejected in another. · our reading

What this research does not say

  • Market or model? As the thesis itself notes, a rejection may mean the market is not rational or that the equilibrium model is wrong; the test cannot tell the two apart. And a statistical rejection may carry little economic weight. · according to the author
  • The 1996 shock. The model does not handle the tax change that made payouts to shareholders grow; the author points to this as the main limitation, alongside not having explored other methods. · according to the author
  • One index, quarterly averages. The test is for the Ibovespa portfolio as a whole, not for each share, and the interest-rate version assumes that only the Selic rate makes the required return vary. · our note
  • How many links? One of the Johansen tables (Table 5) also rejects “at most one link”, which does not fit two wandering series; the page relies on the Engle-Granger test and on the single link estimated in Table 7. · our note

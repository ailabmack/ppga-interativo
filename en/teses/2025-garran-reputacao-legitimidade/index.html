02 The starting point

Two sides of the same coin?

In everyday life, we call a school, a hospital or a company "respected" without separating two very different judgments.

Reputation answers "is this institution good?" It is a comparison: its past performance set side by side with that of its competitors.

Legitimacy answers "is this institution right?" It compares no one: it asks whether the institution acts in line with the norms, values and beliefs of its context.

They usually go together, but they are not the same thing. And the scientific literature itself often mixes them up.

Definition proposed in the thesis. Reputation is the collective perception built by stakeholders on the basis of an organization's past actions, evaluated in terms of its performance and position relative to competitors, reflecting its corporate identity.
Definition proposed in the thesis. Legitimacy is the generalized acceptance of an organization by stakeholders, based on the conformity of its actions with social norms, values and beliefs, perceived as appropriate and desirable in the specific social context in which it operates.

11 The central finding

Legitimacy comes first

The top-weighted criterion on each list tells the story:

17.1%Reputation: faculty quality, a comparative, performance-based criterion
18.3%Legitimacy: integrity and ethics, a moral criterion about consistency

In regulated sectors such as higher education, the thesis calls the relationship hierarchical complementarity: legitimacy is the foundation that lets an institution be recognized as a valid player in its field. Only then does reputation start to work as a differentiator.

With the base in place, reputation has something to stand on.

12 A counterintuitive result

What gets talked about most came last

Inclusion and social responsibility takes up more and more space in sustainability reports and brand campaigns. Even so, of the seven legitimacy criteria, it received the lowest weight (11.5%).

This does not mean the topic is irrelevant. For the professors, it contributes indirectly, through other criteria. The risk the thesis points to is investing in outward-facing social initiatives before consolidating integrity and support for faculty work: the image gained outside does not turn into legitimacy at home.

13 Why this matters

What changes in practice

For institutional leaders

Before investing in rankings, marketing and visibility, consolidate integrity, transparency and consistency between what you say and what you do. Reputation without legitimacy creates an image the faculty themselves do not recognize, and that turns into disengagement.

For professors

The results show that faculty judge legitimacy in categorical terms: a practice is either acceptable or problematic, with no middle ground. A voice in decisions, research support and academic freedom all count here.

For students and families

Rankings and prestige tell the story of reputation. To judge legitimacy, it is worth asking other questions: does the institution do what it promises? Is it transparent? Does it treat its teachers well?

For other organizations

The literature reviewed points to the same logic in other regulated sectors, such as banks and hospitals: being "the best" only counts for those already accepted as right. In new sectors, the order may be reversed.

14 Limits and credits

What this study does not say

  • The professors surveyed work at private institutions in São Paulo and were recruited through academic networks and professional contacts. Generalizing to other contexts calls for caution.
  • The study heard from a single group, the faculty. Students, regulators or the market may weigh the criteria differently.
  • The literature review used only the Web of Science database.

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2021

Rules nobody writes down keep company leadership white

More than half of Brazil is Black, yet almost every executive at large companies is white. To understand why, the thesis interviewed 14 leaders, 9 white and 5 Black, who hire and promote other leaders. It found racial norms that nobody writes down and a white privilege few people see, both upheld by the discourse of meritocracy. Explained in 12 screens.

06 Meritocracy and colour-blindness

Two discourses make inequality look normal

Meritocracy says that whoever works hard gets there. Colour-blindness says that colour does not matter and the problem is only social class. The thesis found meritocracy in the speech of most white leaders. Classify these sentences, said in the interviews:

A race with different starting lines

For the thesis, meritocracy ignores that starting points differ (Young, 2003), and colour-blindness takes race out of the conversation, so inequality is left without a cause or an owner. Among the five Black leaders, the author found no colour-blindness.

10 Diversity and inclusion programmes

Racial diversity programmes are few, and those that exist move slowly

12 To take away

Structural racism does not depend on any one person, but overcoming it depends on leaders who act

For the author, white leaders who want to fight racism should do it where they lead, with objective rules and without waiting for the long run.

For those who lead

  • Admitting the problem is the first step; awareness without action keeps everything as it is.
  • Measure diversity programmes by what they actually change in leadership, not by the campaign.

For HR and people teams

  • Clear, objective rules for hiring, evaluation, promotion and career plans, leaving less room for “affinity”.
  • Review requirements the job does not need, such as fluent English or an elite university. (what some leaders reported)

For society

  • The thesis argues for affirmative action in companies too, and for laws that punish discrimination firmly.
  • The findings can support public policies against racial inequality at work.

What this research does not say

  • How often this happens across the country. These are 14 leaders, all interviewed in the state of São Paulo; the thesis is qualitative and does not generalise. The author suggests a scale to measure racial norms. · according to the author
  • Who was heard. People came through the author’s network and through referrals by the interviewees themselves; six people who were suggested declined or dropped out. · our note
  • An interpretive reading. The names of the points on the line of awareness and the reading of omission or gradualism are the author’s, through critical discourse analysis. The author is a white executive and says so in the thesis. · our note
  • The effect of the measures. The changes some leaders described (CVs without photo or address, new trainee criteria) were not measured. · our note

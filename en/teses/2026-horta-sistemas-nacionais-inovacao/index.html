03 The history of the concept

From 1841 to 2023, the concept only kept growing

The term "national innovation system" appeared in 1987, but its roots go further back. Later it gained neighbors: helices, regional systems, biomes. Since then it has appeared in more than 1,300 academic articles. Drag the slider or click the dots.

04 The problem

It grew so much it lost its shape

The thesis starts from a diagnosis: the concept has undergone reification. The word is hard, but the idea is simple: people began to use it as if it were a ready-made, well-defined thing, while each study understood it differently.

National, regional, sectoral, technological, triple helix, biome… About 43% of studies link the national system to other kinds of system. Without a common base, it is hard to compare, measure or improve these systems.

The thesis does not discard the concept. It reorganizes it so that it becomes useful again.

Our visual metaphor. The layers are the framings cited in the thesis (Section 2.2); the common core is what the review found consistently (Section 2.7).

05 Study 1 · Literature review

2,225 articles found, 126 read in depth

The researcher searched two major scientific databases, Web of Science and Scopus, for everything published on the topic between 1990 and 2024. Hover over each layer of the funnel.

The method follows the PRISMA protocol: every exclusion step is recorded, so that someone else can retrace the path.

07 Study 1 · The actors

Everyone talks about government; hardly anyone about money

The review counted how many of the 126 articles mention each actor. Government and universities dominate the conversation. Civil society and the financial sector barely appear, even though the literature itself acknowledges that they matter.

For the thesis, this imbalance signals that research tends to look at only part of the system and leaves out many of its interdependencies.

An article can mention several actors, so the total exceeds 100%. Source: Appendix E.

08 Study 1 · The critical analysis

Consensus, dispute or surprise?

The researcher cross-checked the findings of the 126 articles and sorted each one into three groups:

Consensus (compatible findings, in the thesis): comes up often and is confirmed from study to study.

Dispute (contradictory findings): authors disagree and the debate does not build up cumulatively.

Surprise (counterintuitive findings): runs against what theory expected.

Do the same sorting alongside. For surprises, the card flips to show what was expected and what the literature found.

12 Study 3 · The scale

A scale made of 31 pieces

The scale has 8 dimensions: the seven actors plus the system itself. Each dimension is assessed at each stage of the cycle (input, sharing and collaboration, output, and impact), and each combination becomes a factor with a statement that experts rate. Click a block.

The hollow block is the impact of the "system" dimension, which does not appear in the thesis’s table. That is why there are 31 factors, not 32. Source: Table 4.2 and Appendix F.

13 Study 3 · How it is calculated

From "I agree" to a maturity level

Innovation experts (academics, researchers, entrepreneurs and public managers) say how much they agree with each statement, on a scale from 1 to 7. The answer becomes a level from 0 to 3. Click an answer.

Expert’s answer

Experts also rank the dimensions from most to least important. With that, the Fuzzy AHP method can give each dimension a different weight. Source: Table 4.1 and Section 4.3.3.

Example from the thesis · one dimension with 5 factors

A dimension’s maturity is the sum of its levels divided by the maximum possible (3 points per factor). The system’s sophistication is the average of the dimensions. In the thesis’s example, three dimensions at 90%, 70% and 60% give 73.3%.

15 Why this matters

From ranking to diagnosis

For the thesis, improving a country’s innovation is not just about investing more or producing more patents. It is about increasing the system’s capacity to coordinate, learn and adapt.

For policymakers

Instead of only knowing a position in a ranking, see where the system is strong or weak and decide where to allocate resources. It is the shift from comparison to diagnosis and strategy.

Thesis conclusions

For emerging countries

Economies with structural constraints can innovate through external links and adaptive strategies. A maturity scale can capture that movement instead of ignoring it.

Thesis conclusions

For each actor in the system

Companies, banks, universities and social organizations can find themselves in their own row of the scale and ask: at which stage of the cycle are we weak? Who do we need to connect with?

Our reading

For researchers

Next steps: apply the scale across several countries, validate it with statistical techniques, and track over time how systems respond to policies and technological change.

16 Limitations and credits

What this research does not say

  • The scale has not yet been applied to countries. Its content was validated by experts (in two rounds), but empirical and statistical validation is still missing.
  • The ratings are perceptions. Expert answers carry a degree of subjectivity.
  • The model simplifies. Eight dimensions cannot capture every country’s particularities, and the integrated framework has not yet been tested empirically.
  • The review has boundaries. It was limited to the 11 leading journals with the most articles on the topic and left out literature not published in journals.

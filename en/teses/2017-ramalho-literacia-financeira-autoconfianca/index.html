Knowing finance helps, if you see yourself clearly

With 1,487 Brazilians from the OECD survey, the thesis shows that people who know more about finance, and so feel more confident, handle money better. But the rule did not hold for those who think they are better or worse than they are. Explained in 11 screens.

03 The problem

Knowing, thinking you know, thinking ahead and doing: four different things

“Financial literacy” has no single definition. Many studies measure it only by right answers. The thesis treats it as a set of links that explain what people do with money. Tap a measure:

    Table 4 (means of the 1,487 people) and section 6 (Brazil and the average of the 30 countries in the OECD survey, 2016). Self-confidence: the question “how would you rate your knowledge of finance?”, from very weak (1) to very good (5). For the OECD, the desirable level is 5 in knowledge, above 3 in attitude and 6 in behaviour.

    04 How the research looked

    A 30-country survey, with 1,487 Brazilians

    The OECD interviewed adults in 30 countries with the same questionnaire. In Brazil, the data were collected by the Central Bank, Serasa Experian and IBOPE, and two Central Bank researchers shared them with the author. 515 people who did not say whether they had saved money, one part of the habits score, were left out.

    07 The unexpected

    Those who say they are good at finance got fewer right than those who say “average”

    Before you look: among those who rated themselves weak, average or good, who got more questions right, on average?

    Section 6. Mean right answers from 0 to 7; columns start at zero. For the OECD, this suggests that some of those who think they are above average are overconfident; according to the author, the survey only saw the same in three other countries (Poland, South Africa and Thailand).

    09 The finding

    When confidence keeps pace with knowledge, knowledge weighs more on habits

    The author put the 355 people who rate themselves better or worse than they are into one group and compared them with the other 1,132. Switch the group:

    Tables 10, 20, 21 and 22. Height = β without its sign; striped top = negative link; hollow column = no clear effect. Our reading: the groups were split by the very knowledge and self-rating answers, so the first column (knowing → confidence) comes largely from the split itself.

    10 What did not show up

    Saying you think ahead did not show up in habits

    Attitude was measured by three statements; those who disagree with them think more about the long run (mean 3.11, from 1 to 5):

    • “I prefer to live for today and let tomorrow take care of itself.”
    • “I usually find it more satisfying to spend money now than to save it for the long term.”
    • “Money is there to be spent.”

    Overall, attitude was linked neither to knowledge nor to habits. For the author, a person may think saving matters and still not manage to: self-control between intention and action is missing from the model. The measure was also weak (reliability 0.57, below the usual 0.7).

    Attitude → habits, β by group

    Tables 9, 12, 14, 16, 18 and 20. Solid bar = clear effect (p below 0.05); hollow = no clear effect. In the two groups with a solid bar, those who say they think more about the future had slightly worse habits, the opposite of the bet; the author cites a study in India with a similar result.

    11 So what?

    Teaching finance is not enough: how people see themselves matters

    For the author, financial education programmes that only teach technical content may not help people who over- or underrate themselves, and may waste resources that are already scarce.

    For policymakers

    • Brazil is below average in knowledge and in habits: there is room to invest in financial education. (author)
    • Consider nudges, which change how choices are presented without banning anything. (author)

    For finance educators

    • Diagnose each person’s profile (how much they know and how much they think they know) before the course. (author)
    • Our reading: a test like the one on screen 2 already shows who overrates themselves.

    For each person

    • Our reading: comparing your test score with your own self-rating helps spot over- or underconfidence.
    • Wanting to save does not guarantee saving; the author points to self-control as the missing piece. (author)

    What this research does not say

    • Not all of Brazil. With 515 people excluded, the sample is not representative and the results do not generalise. · according to the author
    • Link is not cause. An interview done once shows what goes together; knowledge, attitude and habits may influence each other both ways. · according to the author
    • Small effects. The model explains 12.1% of the differences in habits; each link has an effect just above “small”. · according to the author
    • Short measures. Self-confidence is a single question and attitude three statements about time; the author suggests longer scales. · according to the author
    • Smaller groups. With 157 to 297 people per profile, small effects are harder to detect. · our note

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2019

R&D and patents show up in a company’s value, but not on every yardstick

With 1,342 firms from 57 countries and three decades of data, the thesis measured innovation in three ways (research and development spending, or R&D, patents and patent citations) and tested whether it explains how much the stock market pays for a firm and how much its shares return. R&D and patents showed up in some of the tests; citations, in none.

Author: Fernando Henrique Taques Advisor: Prof. Dr. Leonardo Fernando Cruz Basso Co-advisor: Prof. Dr. Nelson Areal (University of Minho) Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 How to measure · chapter 2

Innovating is a chain, and each yardstick measures one link

A firm spends on research, research becomes a patent, the patent becomes a product and the product becomes sales. The first chapter gathered the measures researchers use and showed that none of them captures the whole chain. Tap each link.

04 What is being explained · chapter 3

The target is how much the market pays for each dollar the firm owns

Tobin’s Q divides the firm’s market value by the value of its assets. Above 1, the market sees something beyond the balance sheet, such as what the firm is still expected to earn. Move the market value of a firm with 100 million in assets.

Marks on the scale: 10%, half and 75% of sample A observations (Table 3.5): 0.78, 1.45 and 2.44. The firm in the middle was worth 1.45 times its assets. In the models, Q entered in logarithm.

05 Two ways of counting · chapter 3

Does last year’s patent count, or everything the firm has learned so far?

The thesis measured each yardstick in two ways. As a flow, only what came the previous year. As a stock, everything accumulated, losing 15% of its value each year, like an aging machine. Both were divided by assets, so the giants are not favored. Pick a story.

07 Result · quantile regression

Looking band by band, the patent almost disappears

The second method, the thesis’s novelty, does not look only at the average: it estimates the effect separately for firms with low, middle and high Q (the 10%, 25%, half, 75% and 99% bands). In sample A, patents had a clear effect in one band only, and the downward weight of assets was more than three times larger in the 75% band than in the 10% band. Tap the bands.

08 Scoreboard · all of chapter 3

In 56 tests, innovation lit up 12 times

Putting together the three samples, the two methods and the two ways of counting, each yardstick had its chance. R&D lit up in the average effects and in two bands. Patents, only in sample A. Citations, never. Whenever it lit up, the effect was always upward. Filter by yardstick.

09 Another question · chapter 4

Spending on research is one thing; turning it into sales is another

The last chapter follows a method by Cohen, Diether and Malloy (2013): for each firm, it measures how much R&D from 1 to 5 years earlier tracked sales growth. That is the “ability to innovate.” The author did the same with patents, which was new. Move the ability of a made-up firm.

Each firm’s ability, using 8 years of data at a time, from 1992 to 2018.
Stock portfolios: low or high ability (bottom 30%, top 30%) crossed with low, medium or high R&D, rebuilt every year.
Excess return of each portfolio: what it earned above the risk-free rate, also net of what size, price-to-book, recent trend, profitability and investment already explain (3-, 4- and 5-factor models).

10 Result · stock returns

Shares of firms with more R&D or more patents returned more

The author split firms into five steps, from lowest to highest R&D (or number of patents), and measured each step’s monthly excess return. The bottom two fell below the risk-free rate; the top ones, above. Switch the yardstick.

Not investment advice. Past average returns of portfolios do not guarantee any stock’s future.

11 The unexpected · chapter 4

Being skilled at turning R&D into sales barely changed returns

The bet, from the American study, was that the market is slow to notice who knows how to innovate, and that those shares return more. Among high-R&D firms, the skilled ones returned only slightly more, with no clear difference. For patents, the difference was clear because low-skill firms with many patents did badly.

12 In practice · limits · credits

Measure innovation with more than one yardstick

Managers

R&D and patents went with higher value and higher returns in part of the tests. For the author, tracking what is invested in innovation helps decide where to direct resources, without expecting a guaranteed effect.

Investors

The author reads that the market does not fully take in public information on innovation. The results come from past average portfolios and are not investment advice.

Governments

The author sees in the findings support for policies that encourage and fund innovation in firms, and for checking whether it is a long-term strategy or just a reaction to one-off incentives.

Researchers

Use input, intermediate and output yardsticks together, separate manufacturing and services and test by bands, not just on average. The author also suggests adding US patent office data and other databases.

What this research does not say

  • Few yardsticks. Of the chapter 2 measures, the databases only had R&D, patents and citations; output measures were left out, the author acknowledges.
  • Moving together is not causing. The models show association, and explain little of market value (about 4% to 7% of the differences in Q). · our observation
  • Different rules in each country. Accounting differs across countries and over time, and European firms are not required to disclose R&D. Citations at the European office are few, because the examiner adds them.
  • Simplified Q and few controls. Q does not use the replacement cost of assets, and debt, capital intensity and market share are missing, the author points out.
  • Tables to double-check. Table 4.2 repeats columns; the patent difference in Table 4.7 (0.3849) was redone by us as 0.0384; and the unit of the returns is not stated. · our observation

Strategy, technology and performance in city halls

A city hall can innovate, defend what it already does well or just react. It can spend time and paper on every reply. And it can have more or less technology. With 131 civil servants from city halls in inland Paraná, Brazil, the thesis asks which of these goes hand in hand with better public service. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem

Answering the city council once took up to 30 days of back and forth

In one of the study’s interviews, a civil servant describes how it used to work: each request from the city council arrived on paper, was copied for each department and went back and forth with the mail driver. Finding the information, chasing whoever had not replied, checking: theory calls these transaction costs, costs that do not show up in the budget. Compare:

03 The thesis’s ideas

Miles and Snow describe four ways of dealing with change

In 1978, Miles and Snow proposed four strategic postures: the prospector seeks the new, the defender protects what it already does well, the analyzer balances the two and the reactor only moves when pushed. Tap a posture to see how the thesis measured it and how interviewees described themselves:

09 The finding

The defender posture reaches performance through technology

Those who value keeping services running and protecting citizens also rate IT infrastructure and capabilities higher, and it is through them that the defender posture reaches performance. The prospector does not. Together with the reactor, the model explains 61% of the differences in perceived performance. Switch the view:

12 So what?

Invest in technology and start measuring

For the author, the study can serve as a basis for administrative modernisation policies in inland cities. The interviews and the limits the author points out show the missing step: indicators to measure one’s own performance.

For city halls

  • IT infrastructure and capabilities were the largest links to perceived performance (0.382 and 0.328).
  • Training staff and having robust systems are, for the author, critical to these policies; civil servants agree IT requires investing in both (4.42 and 4.41).

For public managers

  • “Improve a performance indicator? We don’t have that kind of tracking.” (Interview 02)
  • Our reading: without indicators, performance can only be measured as people’s impression, as in this study.

For strategy researchers

  • In the public sector, postures blend: prospector and defender scores barely separate, as Boyne and Walker, cited in the thesis, had argued.
  • The author suggests studies in other regions and over time.

What this study does not say

  • Small convenience sample. 131 civil servants from inland Paraná; other regions may differ. · according to the author
  • Few indicators in city halls. The lack of clear indicators made it harder to assess the impact of technology. · according to the author
  • Perception, not measurement. Performance is what the civil servant perceives over the last three years, and the postures are how important each attitude seems to them, not the city hall’s type. · our note
  • A single moment. Everything was asked in the same questionnaire: it shows what goes together, not what causes what. · our note
  • Two postures that do not separate. Prospector and defender were above the similarity limit; the author kept them apart to preserve the typology. · Table 5

What from a management course reaches the job?

With 424 students and recent graduates of specialization and MBA courses in São Paulo, the thesis shows that those who most say they use what they learned took a course that taught them how to apply it and made them rethink ideas. The company weighed little, and autonomy at work made no difference. Explained in 12 screens.

03 The problem · Table 11

The final project hardly ever goes back to the company

Companies and people invest in courses but rarely know whether what was learned turns into practice. The literature calls this the transfer problem. One sign appears in the participants’ final projects.

11,3%

The final-project questions were optional: 291 of the 424 people answered. For the author, the numbers suggest the project is seen as a formality; schools also do not require it to deal with the student’s company.

04 The idea · Yelon, Ford and Bhatia (2014)

Using what you learned goes beyond doing your own task

Instead of asking “did the course pay off?”, the thesis asks “is the person using what they learned?”. And there are five ways to use it, from one’s own work to changes across the whole company. Tap each one.

06 The method · sections 4.3 to 4.5

First a new yardstick, then 424 questionnaires

There was no yardstick for the five uses. The author listened to students, wrote statements, tested and cut. Then the author visited classes at five schools in São Paulo between March and June 2019.

Tables 9 and 10 and section 4.5. Schools were contacted by convenience; 29% of the printed questionnaires came back completed. Individual micro-entrepreneurs, people with many blank answers or with more than 80% identical answers were removed.

09 The finding · section 5.5.4

The course weighs more than the company

The model explains 43.6% of the differences in use between people. By the author’s account, almost all of it comes from what the course sparked: rethinking and learning how to apply. Company culture adds little. Switch off each part and watch the tower drop.

Shares calculated by the author as coefficient × correlation with use (section 5.5.4; correlations from Table 19). The rest, 4.5 points, is our calculation (43.6 − 39.1) and goes to cynicism, autonomy, position and age. With links measured in a single questionnaire, “explain” means going together, not causing.

12 So what?

Using what you learn starts in the classroom

For the author, combining classes that show how to apply with methods that lead to reflection answers part of the criticism of MBAs. And the five-use yardstick can serve schools and companies.

For schools and coordinators

  • Plan exercises, examples and activities that show how to use the content at work, and methods that make students rethink ideas. (author)
  • Use the five-use yardstick alongside satisfaction and learning assessments, in open or in-company courses. (author)

For companies and HR

  • The yardstick can be answered by managers and colleagues, not only by the student, and linked to indicators such as sales and profit. (author)
  • Even when the student pays for the course (74%), the uses point to a short-term gain for the company. (author)

For those taking the course

  • Our reading: choosing a final project with data from your own company is a simple way to bring the course back to work; only 4 in 10 of those who answered did so.
  • In the interviews, those who applied what they learned said applying also taught them, and one use led to another. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Who answered. 424 people from five well-regarded schools in São Paulo, chosen by convenience; other schools and other students may give a different result. · according to the author
  • A snapshot, not a film. The questionnaire was answered once; it shows what goes together, not what causes what. · according to the author
  • Use, not results. The yardstick measures whether people say they use what they learned; using is necessary but not enough to say the course paid off. · according to the author
  • Almost all still in the course. 92% were finishing the course; use may change over time. · our note
  • A short yardstick for culture. Company culture was measured with 7 statements, one per aspect; the full version might capture more. · according to the author

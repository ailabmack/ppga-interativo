Does a single-business company owe more or less than a diversified one? It depends on the yardstick

The thesis sorted 237 companies on the Brazilian stock exchange, from 2012 to 2016, by how much of their revenue came from the main business, and compared how much each group owed. Measured against the balance sheet, the single-business company shows more debt; measured against the stock-market value of its shares, less. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The question

The theory’s bet: the more diversified the company, the more debt it takes on

“Don’t put all your eggs in one basket” applies to companies too: a firm with several businesses depends less on any one of them. US studies (Rumelt, 1974; Barton and Gordon, 1988) found that single-business firms avoid risk and owe less, while the most diversified owe more.

The thesis tested these bets in Brazil, with Wrigley’s (1970) three categories. Place yours before going on: the answer comes on screen 08.

03 The method

The diversification yardstick is the main business’s share of revenue

05 What is measured

The same debt can be measured against the balance sheet or against the value of the shares

The balance-sheet yardstick divides debt by everything the company owns (its assets). The market yardstick divides it by the value of its shares on the exchange at year end. If the share price falls, the company looks more indebted on that yardstick without borrowing a single extra real.

06 Result · balance-sheet yardstick

On the balance sheet, single-business companies owed the most

Each model compares the groups with the group left out of it, holding profit, size, growth and the other factors equal. In the three models with both diversified categories, single-business companies came out on top: 2 to 9 points of assets higher.

07 Result · market yardstick

Against the value of the shares, single-business companies owed the least

Here debt is measured in “times the value of the shares”: 1 means debt equal to the company’s value on the exchange. In six of the seven comparisons, the single-business company came out below the other groups; the seventh did not pass the test.

Models 7 to 11 use fixed effects: they compare each company with itself over the years. Switch the model.

08 The finding

Switching the yardstick flips the answer

On the balance sheet, the single-business company sits at the top for debt. Against share value, it drops to the bottom and the diversified ones rise, as the US theory predicted. Between dominant and diversified companies, the order changes from one model to another.

11 So what?

Before saying who owes more, ask on which yardstick

For those who analyse companies

  • In the sample, the order of the groups flipped between the balance-sheet and market yardsticks. (screen 08)
  • Looking at both yardsticks avoids conclusions that depend only on the share price. (our reading)

For finance managers

  • Diversifying did not come with more balance-sheet debt: diversified companies owed less than single-business ones. (screen 06)
  • The thesis aims to prompt reflection on which business strategies Brazilian companies have been adopting. (thesis)

For researchers

  • Test other measures, other calculations and other diversification classifications. (thesis)
  • Use the same classification to study the profitability of Brazilian companies. (thesis)

What this research does not say

  • Still a fragile result. The thesis itself says the results are not solid enough to establish a model for future research. · according to the thesis
  • Many models, few shown. Of 120 models run, 11 were assessed; choosing among many can highlight chance results. · our observation
  • Fixed effects. In models 7 to 11, the category only counts for companies that changed category from one year to another. · our observation
  • Model errors. The tests in Tables 5 and 6 point to autocorrelation; the thesis does not say whether standard errors were corrected. · our observation
  • Association, not cause. The models do not say whether diversification leads to debt or the other way round. · our observation
  • Scope. Five years, companies listed on the B3, without banks and funds; related and unrelated companies merged because there were few. · according to the thesis

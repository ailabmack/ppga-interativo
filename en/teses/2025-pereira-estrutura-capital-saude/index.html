What makes a health plan operator take on debt?

Health plan operators collect monthly fees and pay for consultations, tests and hospital stays. To finance everything else, each one chooses between using its own money and borrowing. The thesis followed about 200 operators in Brazil from 2018 to 2023 to see what weighs on that choice. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The idea

Of every R$ 100 in monthly fees, about R$ 75 goes back out in consultations, tests and hospital stays

This is the claims ratio (in Brazil, sinistralidade): the share of what the operator receives from its plans that goes to the health expenses of its beneficiaries. What is left has to pay for everything else.

In the thesis sample, the average claims ratio was 75%. But some operators spent more than they took in. Pick a case.

03 The context

More than 80 million plan memberships, and an agency watching the books

Brazil's private health plan market has more than 1,200 companies of eight types. All answer to the National Supplementary Health Agency (ANS), which monitors their figures and requires technical reserves: money set aside to honor the care already promised.

An operator has to satisfy ANS, which wants solvency, and investors, who want returns. Tap a type.

Operators that differ widely

09 The finding

Those who earn more owe less; those with more buildings and equipment owe more

Both theories show up together. Profit follows the queue (pecking order): operators that earn more use their own money and owe less. Fixed assets follow the balance (trade-off): they serve as collateral and come with more debt.

10 The sector factors

Claims ratio and technical reserves: it depends on the yardstick

The two factors specific to operators did not give a single sign. On the total debt yardstick, neither had an effect. On the other two, the claims ratio came with more debt, and technical reserves switched sign.

12 In practice, limits and credits

Debt, profit and regulation go together in private health care

Operator managers

The author recommends close management of the claims ratio and technical reserves, balancing capital structure with financial sustainability.

ANS and regulators

For the author, reserve rules ensure continuity of care but weigh more on smaller operators; she suggests rules that take differences between operators into account.

Investors

Our reading: operating profit and fixed assets are the steadiest signals of an operator's debt: they had the same sign in all six models.

Researchers

The author suggests interviews with managers and regulators, longer periods, comparisons by size and the effect of technologies such as telemedicine.

What this research does not say

  • Only balance sheet figures: secondary data from ANS, without the managers' reasons behind their decisions. · according to the author
  • Six years (2018–2023), with the pandemic in the middle: too short to see long-term effects. · according to the author
  • Left out were operators with assets below R$ 1 million and those with negative equity, which may borrow differently. · according to the author
  • Association, not causation, and much of the variation in debt (73% to 89%) is left unexplained by the models. · our note

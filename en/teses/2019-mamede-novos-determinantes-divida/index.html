Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2019

In U.S. companies, debt tracked profit and growth; the new factors left only faint signals

The thesis followed 1,675 U.S. companies from 2010 to 2017 and tested 20 factors at once: 7 classic company factors, 10 about the CEO and 3 about the state where the company is (corruption, immigration and refugees). Explained in 12 screens.

02 The question

Sixty years of theory still do not say how much debt a company should carry

Every company is financed by a mix of borrowed money (debt) and owners’ money. That mix is its capital structure. Three well-known theories try to explain it, but each predicts something different, and the author calls this a puzzle still to be solved.

06 Result 1 · classic factors

More profitable companies and those with more growth opportunities owed less, in every model

Four of the seven classic factors came with less debt. That is the opposite of hypothesis H1 (which expected “more”) and fits the pecking-order theory: companies that earn money use their own cash before borrowing. Tap a factor.

Source: Table 4 (8,617 firm-years). ▼ = less debt, ▲ = more debt; stars = confidence (* 10%, ** 5%, *** 1%), recalculated by us (coefficient ÷ standard error), because some printed stars do not match the numbers. We show direction only: the printed scales of the variables do not allow translating the coefficients into everyday values.

07 Result 2 · CEO profile

Almost nothing in the CEO’s profile tracked debt

Of the ten traits, seven never showed up. Three (older, better-paid, female CEO) came with less debt in the simplest model, but almost all of it disappears when the model compares each company with itself over the years. Hypothesis H2 expected “more”.

Source: Table 4 (stars recalculated by us) and Table 1 (average profile). A female CEO appears in about 5% of firm-years, so the signal for this trait rests on few cases.

08 Result 3 · corruption in the state

Sand or grease? State corruption left a faint signal of more debt

The literature cited in the thesis has two views: corruption as sand in companies’ gears, or as grease that eases contracts and credit. The author bet on grease (more debt). The measure is a state index, from 1 to 262, combining convicted and expected corruption cases.

Source: Tables 4, 8, 9, 16, 17 and 18; stars recalculated by us. Each row is a combination of factors tested by the author. In the last one, the models use an instrumental variable (how concentrated the state’s population is around its capital) to deal with two-way causation between corruption and debt.

09 Result 4 · refugees and immigration in the state

Immigration did not show up in any model; the refugee signal switched sides

Both measures are yearly state counts: how many immigrants and how many refugees the state received. The thesis does not know whether each company hired anyone from these groups. The author expected less debt with immigrants and more with refugees. Pick a factor.

Source: Tables 4, 8, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17 and 18; stars recalculated by us. Means per firm-year (Table 1): about 3,300 refugees and 16,300 immigrants per year in the state.

11 Read with care

The easy-credit story is the author’s explanation, not a tested result

In the abstract and conclusion, the author proposes a chain: companies that join state refugee-hosting programs would get cheaper credit and, over time, more government contracts, which would open room for corruption. Compare it with what the thesis’s data measure.

What the thesis’s data measure

  • Each company’s debt and its accounting figures.
  • The profile of each company’s CEO.
  • State-level counts: corruption cases, immigrants and refugees received in the year.

What they do not measure

  • Whether the company joined any program or hired refugees.
  • The interest rate each company paid.
  • Government contracts or corruption cases involving the company itself.
Association is not causation. The thesis shows what moved together in these companies and states. A faint signal that appears in only some models is not enough to say that refugees or corruption make companies borrow.
12 So what?

What remains, for whom, and what this research does not say

For finance managers

  • Profit, growth opportunities and fixed assets remain the best guide to debt. (screen 06)
  • Consider the state’s corruption environment and migration flows. (author)

For policymakers

  • More oversight of company–government deals can add transparency and reduce corruption. (author)
  • The effect of immigrants on company finances needs more study. (author)

For boards

  • The CEO’s profile barely explained debt in this sample. (screen 07)
  • The author suggests studying the quality of that influence further. (author)

For researchers

  • Use the Institute for Corruption Studies index with other topics: lobbying, bankruptcy, taxes, leniency agreements. (author)
  • Measure each company’s link to state programs and contracts. (our reading)

Limits

  • State-level factors. Corruption, immigration and refugees are state figures, the same for every company there in a given year. · our note
  • Faint, unstable signals. With 20 factors and many combinations, isolated 10% signals can appear by chance; the refugee signal flips with the instrumental variable. · our note
  • Printed numbers that do not add up. Some stars do not match coefficient ÷ standard error (we redid all of them) and the scales in the descriptive table do not match the coefficients; so we show no effect sizes. · our note
  • U.S. companies only, 2010 to 2017. Behavior was studied only through the CEO, and the period ends in 2017. · per the author

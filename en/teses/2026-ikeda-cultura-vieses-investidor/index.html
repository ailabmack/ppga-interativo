Do investors have an accent?

Trusting yourself too much, holding on to the stock that keeps falling, following the herd: investors all over the world make the same mistakes. With daily data from 64 stock exchanges, from 2010 to 2024, the thesis shows that each country's culture changes the size of these biases, and that the pandemic even reversed the direction of some of them. 02 The problem

Three mistakes almost every investor has made

Classical theory assumes a cold investor who uses all available information. Behavioral finance shows otherwise: emotions and mental shortcuts lead to predictable mistakes, called biases. The thesis studies three of them.

Are these mistakes the same size everywhere? The thesis bets they are not: each country has a national culture, a shared way of thinking and feeling passed down through generations, which can amplify or curb each bias.

Do investors worldwide exhibit the same biases, or are these biases shaped and amplified by each country's culture?

04 Chapter 3 · 60 articles

A young field that has doubled in size since 2017

Before going to the data, the author combed Web of Science and Scopus for everything published on national culture and investor behavior in well-ranked finance journals. 60 articles remained, from 2009 to 2024. 06 Chapters 5 to 7 · how the research looked

How to measure a bias without asking anyone anything

Instead of questionnaires, the thesis reads biases in the footprints they leave in the market: trading volume and price movements, day by day, sector by sector.

64stock exchanges
15years of trading, 2010 to 2024
> 0.5 bntransactions
Overconfidence: trading in falling sectors, compared with each sector's normal volume. High volume on a bad day suggests too much confidence.
Disposition effect: volume in rising sectors divided by volume in falling sectors. If the first is much larger, people sell what gains and hold what loses.
Herding: how far sectors move away from the market average on days of big moves. See the idea on the right.

Each market's days become three levels (low, medium or high bias, by quartiles), related to the six dimensions by multinomial logistic regression; a second model (GAM) checks for curved relationships. Data: Capital IQ. Source: sections 5.2, 6.3 and 7.3.

The idea behind the herding measure · illustrative example

Made-up sectors and returns, only to show the logic. The thesis's measure follows Chang, Cheng and Khorana (2000): if, on days of big moves, the average distance of sectors from the market grows less than expected, there is herding.

07 Chapter 5 · overconfidence

During the pandemic, three cultural traits switched sides

Before COVID-19, more individualistic, more competitive (masculinity) and more uncertainty-avoiding countries had more days of overconfidence. During the pandemic, all three began to curb the bias, and stayed that way afterward. For the author, facing a risk nobody controls, people began to act more as a group.

08 Chapter 6 · disposition effect · 2010 to 2024

Rich, long-term-minded countries hold on to losses less

The higher the income per person, the lower the chance of days with a high disposition effect. More individualistic and long-term countries also show less of the bias. Contrary to earlier studies, masculinity and indulgence are also linked to less bias.

09 Chapter 7 · herding · 64 exchanges

In the pandemic, markets stopped going against the flow

Before COVID-19, 26 exchanges showed the opposite of herding: investors each acting on their own. During the pandemic, only 9 stayed that way; most became neutral and herding appeared in 14. Afterward, anti-herding returned (25) and herding fell to 7, almost all in emerging markets such as Morocco, Tunisia and Vietnam.

10 Chapter 7 · culture, income and herding

After the crisis, hierarchy weighed much more

Income per person protects against herding in all three periods. Among cultural traits, power distance (accepting hierarchy) always pushed toward herding, and its weight nearly quintupled after the pandemic. Long-term orientation always curbed the bias. 12 So what?

Educate, regulate and invest with culture on the radar

Financial education

Programs and risk warnings work better when adapted to the cultural profile of investors, instead of a single recipe for everyone.

Sections 6.5 and 8

Regulators

Special attention to low- and middle-income markets, where rising income reduces herding most, and to hierarchical countries as crises end. Crisis communication that reduces ambiguity.

Section 7.5

Companies

Consider the target market's culture when negotiating, entering a country and launching products: where uncertainty avoidance is high, detailed contracts and products that convey safety.

Sections 4.5 and 8

Investors

Knowing that the cultural setting weighs on decisions, and that in a crisis this weight can change direction, helps you be wary of your own impulses. Our reading.

Based on chapter 8

What this research does not say

  • It does not prove cause. It shows associations between culture, income and biases; other country factors may weigh on both. · according to the author
  • One score per country. Hofstede assumes a homogeneous culture and misses regions, subcultures and immigrants. · according to the author
  • Biases measured by market footprints, not by tests with people; liquidity and trading robots enter the volume. · according to the author
  • A single crisis. The pandemic was a health shock; a financial crisis or a war may have another effect, and the period dates are a choice. · according to the author
  • Modest explanatory power. The models have pseudo R² from 0.02 to 0.24, depending on the bias: culture is one piece, not the whole explanation. · our note

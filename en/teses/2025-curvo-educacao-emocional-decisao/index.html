The emotions that go into managers’ decisions

Laying someone off, hiring, cutting costs: do managers decide with their head or their heart? The thesis talked to nine managers in Campo Grande, Brazil, and found emotion in the decisions they described, including the ones they called rational. The author concludes that emotion helps when managers learn to deal with it, which is why she proposes emotional education. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Luciana Auxiliadora Guimarães Fonseca Curvo Advisor: Prof. Dr. Almir Martins Vieira Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

11 The finding · theme 7 and the thesis’s contribution

The thesis adds emotional education to the model

The managers pointed to emotional education as a way forward, and one recalled what nobody teaches: We don’t learn at school, or in life in general, to name and tell apart emotions and feelings (G5). Hence the thesis’s central sentence: emotions contribute to the decisions of managers who are emotionally educated. Compare the two models:

12 Takeaways

Handling emotion is part of learning to decide

The managers care about their teams’ well-being but, in the author’s words, are still in a process of emotional learning. For her, emotional education is a form of prevention.

For managers

  • Name what you feel before deciding: breathe, wait and only then reflect, as G5 described.
  • Seek balance with practices that work for you (exercise, therapy, meditation, conversation).

For companies

  • Emotional education programmes designed around managers’ routines, according to the author.
  • Pay close attention to illness and addiction, discrimination by age, gender and race, and how generations work together.

For schools and courses

  • The author argues for emotional education from the first years of school.
  • And also in undergraduate Business Administration, where future managers are trained.

What this research does not say

  • Other cities and more managers. The research stayed in Campo Grande and heard nine people. · according to the author
  • Whether emotional education works. The thesis proposes programmes but did not run or evaluate any. · our observation
  • How the decisions actually went. Everything comes from the managers’ own accounts, not from observing decisions or teams. · our observation
  • How many said each thing. Apart from family (nine of nine), the thesis does not count quotes by theme; counts here are ours. · our observation
  • A similar group. Four of the nine work in education; the author, also a manager, says the research changed how she decides. · our observation

Buying medicines at the lowest price does not make Brazilian labs innovate

The thesis heard 14 people from government and industry: ordinary tenders only look at price and leave no room for dialogue, while partnerships in which a private lab teaches a public factory to make a medicine (PDPs) pushed Brazilian firms to modernise. Explained in 13 screens.

02 The setting · industry data gathered in the thesis

The government is a giant buyer of medicines

In 2019, Brazil’s pharmaceutical market sold R$ 102.8 billion, the 7th largest in the world. The fastest-growing part was the institutional market (government, clinics and hospitals): +57.5% since 2015, driven by innovative medicines for cancer, degenerative and rare diseases. According to the thesis’s documents and interviews, Brazil’s public health system (SUS) accounts for about one third of the country’s medicine consumption.

03 The setting · who sells

Brazilian labs sell two thirds of the boxes but less than half of the value

Of Brazil’s 249 pharmaceutical labs, 148 are Brazilian-owned. They specialise in generics and “similars”, copies of off-patent medicines that require less research investment. They sell many boxes at low prices; foreign firms hold the innovative medicines. The country depends on imported ingredients and medicines.

05 The method · grounded theory

Each conversation brought less that was new

In grounded theory, the theory emerges from the data. The researcher reads each interview line by line, names (codes) what comes up and only then moves to the next one. Interviewing stops when conversations no longer bring new ideas: saturation. The first interview yielded 24 codes; the last ones, 2 to 4.

Our count from Tables 10 to 22 (new codes, 119 in all) and Appendix E (18 themes marked 0 or 1 per interview). The text says the 12th interview brought nothing new; the appendix table marks one new theme in the 12th and two in the 11th.

From 119 codes to one sentence

07 What it found · Model 1

In an ordinary tender, price rules and nobody talks

Three forces shape public purchasing of medicines: the laws, the SUS division of tasks between federal, state and municipal governments, and the slow process of accepting new medicines into the SUS. 08 What it found · proposition P3

Selling to the government pays off, but not with new medicines

Despite the obstacles, labs want to sell to the SUS: they set up tender departments, subscribe to tender portals and use distributors. What they do not do is innovate for it. Switch the question and see which way the scale tips:

09 What it found · Model 2

In a PDP, the government buys the medicine and the know-how to make it

Productive Development Partnerships (PDPs) combine public purchasing and technology transfer. The Ministry of Health buys a strategic medicine from a public lab without a tender. During the contract, the partner private lab makes the medicine and teaches the public lab how to produce it; at the end, the public lab takes over production. Turn each flow on and off:

10 The finding · the thesis’s theory

Three ingredients link public purchasing to innovation, and only one is decisive

For government purchasing to help Brazilian labs innovate, it must reduce transaction costs, create business opportunities and promote interaction between government and firms. The first two are essential but not enough; interaction is the decisive one. Compare the two ways of buying:

The substantive theory (Figure 20) and the author’s comparison of tenders and PDPs (sections 5.6.1 to 5.6.3 and conclusions). Illustrative heights. PDPs also have problems: political interference, frequent staff turnover at the Ministry, the lack of a law for public labs and uncertainty about what happens after the transfer.

11 What PDPs changed in firms

The biosimilar is the first step

PDPs did not create brand-new medicines: they transferred the manufacturing of existing ones. For the author, innovation happened inside the firms, which modernised plants, certified lines and trained teams (process innovation), and it also came in products: biosimilars, copies of biological medicines that Brazilian labs did not know how to make. 13 So what? · limits and credits

For public purchasing to spur innovation, government and firms need to talk

For public managers

Rethink the weight of lowest price in tenders, make room for dialogue and feedback to suppliers and steer part of the demand to innovative medicines and biosimilars. Combine purchasing with other policies, such as technology transfer. (author)

For Brazilian labs

Understanding public purchasing rules can help firms see the state as a possible buyer of innovation, not only of generics. PDPs showed that the training requirements lead to new plants and teams. (author)

For lawmakers

Interviewees call for a clear legal instrument for public purchasing of innovation, instead of ordinances hanging off the Procurement Law, and state rules, not government rules, for public labs. (interviewees)

What this research does not say

  • It does not measure the effect. The thesis explains how the policy works from participants’ views; it does not compare firms’ innovation figures before and after PDPs. The author suggests quantitative studies to test the models. · our note and the author’s
  • It does not apply to every sector or country. It is qualitative research based on the views of 14 people, chosen by convenience and referral, in a single sector. · according to the author
  • Rules were still recent. The rules for innovation purchasing and for PDPs were new; results need to be followed for longer. · according to the author
  • Some figures are quotes. Data such as the number of active PDPs or the share of failed transfers come from interviewees, not official databases, and were left out of this page. · our note

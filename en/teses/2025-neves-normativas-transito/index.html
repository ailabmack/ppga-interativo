What helps in interpreting a traffic rule

Laws, resolutions, ordinances, service orders: officers at DETRAN-PR, the Paraná traffic department, use them to issue a driver's licence or register a vehicle. With 155 officers across the state, the thesis measures what is linked to more or less difficulty reading them: learning, contacts, balancing the new with routine, and the text of the rule itself. Explained in 12 screens.

03 The ideas in the thesis

Three personal capacities and the text of the rule

The thesis applies to the officer three ideas first developed to study companies: absorptive capacity (recognising, assimilating and applying new knowledge), social capital (ties inside and outside the agency) and ambidexterity (exploring the new without losing routine efficiency). 08 What it found · the model

Absorptive capacity is linked to every other piece

The thesis tested ten links at once (structural equations). Absorptive capacity is linked to the other capacities and to less difficulty. The text that gets in the way is linked to more difficulty. Ambidexterity and outside ties make no clear difference. Tap an arrow:

09 The finding

The text of the rule weighs more than any trait of the officer

With only the officer's capacities, the model explains about 10% of the differences in difficulty. When how much the text of the rule gets in the way (vague terms, badly worded sentences, multiple meanings) is added, the explanation almost triples. Switch the text on and off:

10 The unexpected

Officers with more ties in the agency reported slightly more difficulty

The hypothesis was that good contacts inside DETRAN would help in interpreting the rules. In the data, the link is small and goes the other way: more internal social capital, slightly more stated difficulty. Move the three factors that held up and see the total:

12 So what?

Train those who read and write better what is read

For the author, ongoing training for officers can be an effective strategy to develop absorptive capacity and improve the service to society. The data point to another target: the text of the rules itself.

For DETRAN and other agencies

  • Regular training and workshops on new rules, according to the author. Today there is no regular programme: from time to time, officers take online courses from the Paraná state school of government.
  • Lack of training was what got in the way most in the workplace (4.30).

For those who write rules

  • Vague terms (4.13) and badly worded sentences (4.03) top what gets in the way in the text.
  • In the model, a confusing text was the largest link to difficulty. Clear, well-defined rules, the author says, are essential for consistent interpretation.

For officers

  • Talking to colleagues about a new rule was the highest answer on absorptive capacity (5.07).
  • The author suggests discussion groups and support networks among officers to make readings consistent; the research did not measure the effect of these groups.

What this research does not say

  • One agency, one state. Only DETRAN-PR officers; other agencies and regions may differ. · according to the author
  • Closed questionnaire. Fixed answers miss the nuances; the author suggests interviews and focus groups. · according to the author
  • Stated, not measured, difficulty. Officers say how much difficulty they feel; the research did not test whether they interpret the rule correctly. · our observation
  • A single point in time. Everything was asked in the same questionnaire: it shows what goes together, not what causes what, nor the direction. · our observation
  • Much remains unexplained. The model explains 29% of the differences in difficulty; about 71% depend on other things. · our observation, with the R² in Table 9

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2019

Getting through judicial reorganization also depends on who files the case

The thesis looked at bankruptcy in Brazil from three angles: it forecast monthly bankruptcy filings from economic data, read one by one the 185 active judicial reorganizations in the state of Paraná, and assessed the law reform under debate. Getting through depended on the firm as much as on the case itself, such as having a lawyer who knows the law. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Eduardo da Silva Mattos Advisor: Prof. Dr. Leonardo Fernando Cruz Basso Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The question · introduction and chapter 3

Bankruptcy law exists to tell the firm that can still be saved from the one that cannot

For the author, the law should let viable businesses carry on and remove the others as efficiently as possible, so assets and people can be put back to use. The thesis’s overall question: how do you tell one from the other? Tap each path.

The 2005 law changed the game

Law 11,101/2005 ended the old “concordata” (a court-granted debt moratorium) and gave creditors the vote on the plan. Filing for someone else’s bankruptcy got harder: only with debts above 40 minimum wages and a prior formal protest. Before, creditors used bankruptcy filings as a debt-collection tool.

06 Chapter 2 · new dataset · Table 6

All 185 active reorganizations in Paraná, read one by one

The author, a lawyer, searched all 160 court districts in the state and opened every judicial reorganization case active on 1 January 2017, including those already turned into bankruptcy. Each outcome was read on 1 March 2019. Since this is the whole population, there is no sampling.

Many arrive late

Only the capital, Curitiba, has bankruptcy-specialized courts: 21.62% of cases. Only 14.59% of cases had a court-appointed administrator with a website for the case. Means and medians from Table 6.

08 Chapter 2 · a measure created by the author

Almost half of the filings arrived without the documents the law itself lists

Article 51 of the law lists the documents that must come with a reorganization filing. The author called a lawyer “specialized” if they attached all of them from the start. It is a simple criterion, but he saw filings amended four times and administrators removed for lack of experience. Tap a folder.

Cases without a specialized lawyer, by region (Table 6). In total, 89 of 185, or 48% (our calculation from the table).

09 The unexpected · Chapter 2 · Figure 3

Asking creditors for a bigger discount did not lower the chance of approval

The haircut is the cut the company asks for on its debts. Bigger cuts were expected to push creditors away. In the data, the sign came out positive in three models. The author’s reading: those who negotiate well already ask for the most creditors will accept; a zero haircut comes from those with no other card to play, often small firms. Tap the bars.

Approximate heights (~), read by us from the thesis’s Figure 3 (share of plans in each haircut band). The two boldest plans, with 85% and 95% haircuts, were rejected and the firms went bankrupt: there is a limit.

11 Chapter 3 · sections 3.3.1 and 3.3.3

In a R$1 million case, the court-appointed administrator would earn R$833 a month

More than 15% of Paraná’s reorganizations had debts below R$1 million. The author worked out the pay cap: 5% of the debts (2% for micro-enterprises), 60% paid over three years and 40% at the end. Less than one minimum wage a month, which may drive good professionals away. Change the size of the case.

For the small business, a fresh start

The author, drawing on Baird, notes that many small firms have no business to save: reorganization helps the owner sort out the debts and move on to the next venture. Giving them more favorable treatment without recognizing this difference may only delay the inevitable. The reform did not address it.

12 In practice · limits · credits

Look at the economy, the balance sheet and the process too

Firms in distress

Hiring someone who knows reorganization made a difference in the data. And come early: two-thirds of firms outside the capital filed when losses had already wiped out all past profits.

Banks and lenders

The author hopes the models help show how each sector and size reacts to the economy, and that factors from the case, not only the balance sheet, enter credit-risk analysis.

Lawmakers and courts

For the author, a filter to separate viable firms before the vote is missing, and choosing administrators on price alone and the low pay in small cases may worsen the service.

Researchers

The author suggests studying why GDP moved together with more bankruptcies, measuring unemployment and new firm creation better, and looking at creditor classes and court congestion.

What this research does not say

  • One state only. The 185 reorganizations are from Paraná; the author sees it as a good proxy for the country, but it was not tested elsewhere.
  • Association is not causation. The “specialized lawyer” is measured only by complete documents and may carry other things, such as company size, which the author himself mentions. · our note
  • Getting 71% right is less than it sounds. The probability models got 70.9% and 71.8% of cases right. Always guessing “the firm gets through” would get about 56% to 58%, the success rate in Table 6. · our comparison
  • Outcomes as of March 2019. A firm that is meeting its plan today may go bankrupt later, the author warns.
  • Bankruptcy forecasts with shifting weights. The models use many lags (the R² of 0.99 drops to 0.85 once adjusted), and the author recommends re-estimating them whenever possible. They are not meant to decide a loan on their own. · partly our note

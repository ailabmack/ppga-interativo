Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2023

Training teachers to teach the circular economy

Who will teach future managers to close the production loop, reusing instead of throwing away? The thesis designed and ran a 40-hour programme for management teachers at a public institution in Maranhão, in northeastern Brazil, and followed what changed in the thinking and actions of those who saw it through. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Veríssimo Nascimento Ramos dos Santos Advisor: Prof. Dr. Janette Brunstein Programme: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The starting point · the programme's theme

Today's economy runs in a straight line, from resource to rubbish

Extract, make, use and throw away: that is the linear model. The circular economy tries to close the loop, bringing materials and products back into production. For the thesis, it is a good theme for training teachers because it forces you to think about the environment, the economy and people at the same time. Compare the two drawings:

03 The problem · how the subject is taught

Teaching on the circular economy still looks more at technology than at society

There are very different ways of talking about the circular economy. A classification used by the thesis (Calisto-Friant and colleagues, 2020) crosses two questions: can we grow without destroying the environment, fast enough? (optimistic or sceptical) And does the conversation include social justice and political participation, or only efficient use of resources? (holistic or segmented). Tap a quadrant:

04 The method · pedagogical action research

The researcher studied, listened to experts and then taught the course

In action research, the researcher also intervenes. The author, a teacher at the institution itself, built the programme in stages: what the literature said, what experts did, what colleagues already knew. Then he ran the course and interviewed those who completed it. Tap a stage:

Sections 2.4, 3.3, 6 and 7 of the thesis. Bars on the same scale within each stage. The interviews were examined with thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke), looking for recurring themes.

08 The finding · three depths of reflection

Four of the six showed signs of rethinking their own premises

Mezirow distinguishes three kinds of reflection. On content (what the problem is), on process (how to solve it) and on premises, when the problem itself is questioned. Only the last usually changes one's perspective. In the final interviews, the author found all three. Tap a cube:

12 Takeaways

To educate critical students, you first have to educate critical teachers

The thesis argues for combining transformative learning, in its social and critical strand, with education for the circular economy. It concludes that we cannot rely only on each teacher's motivation: universities need to open spaces for ongoing, not one-off, teacher development.

For universities and business schools

  • Offer teacher development as institutional policy, with a certificate and support, not as an isolated initiative.
  • Take circular economy teaching beyond technology: include workers, communities and social justice.

For those who train teachers

  • Make the most of session time: short texts, videos and live questions worked better than long readings and weekly journals.
  • Return to the answers given at the start so everyone notices what changed.

For management teachers

  • Start from the problems and economic activities of the students' own town.
  • Mix content, field and critical-reflective strategies, rather than choosing just one.

What this research does not tell us

  • Whether it applies elsewhere. It took place in a specific region and a specific institution. · according to the author
  • How it would work in person and with students. The online format came from the pandemic, and there were no students, community members, site visits or many expert talks. · according to the author
  • Whether the changes last. There was no follow-up over time; the author suggests longitudinal studies. · according to the author
  • Six people and their accounts. Of 17 enrolled, 6 completed; the signs of change come from what they reported, with no comparison group. · our observation
  • Projects on paper. The six projects were planned during the course; the thesis did not follow their implementation. · our observation

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2018

Do companies with large, steady and growing cash take on more debt?

The thesis proposed a new reading of debt, the “modified trade-off”: lenders would look at the cash a company generates. Across 94 Brazilian listed companies from 2008 to 2016, no single cash trait explained debt on its own, and the cash that swung the most went with more debt, not less.

Author: Graciela Dias Coelho Jones Advisor: Prof. Dr. Wilson Toshiro Nakamura Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The question · chapters 2 and 3

The thesis proposed a third answer to an old question

A company started earning more. Should it now owe more or less? Answer before you see what the theories say.

Pick an answer.

owe less

Pecking order financing hierarchy

The company first uses the money it generates itself, then debt and, last, new shares. Whoever earns more needs to borrow less.

owe more

Trade-off balance

Interest on debt lowers the income tax due, and whoever earns more has more tax to save. The limit is the risk of not being able to pay, which grows with debt.

depends on the cash

Modified trade-off the thesis’s proposal

Look at cash instead of profit. Companies with large, steady and growing cash show they will keep generating it, earn the trust of banks and suppliers and can owe more. The thesis presents the idea as preliminary.

03 Where the idea came from · section 1.4

The idea came from the ratio the market uses most, debt divided by the cash generated

The thesis starts from the net debt ÷ EBITDA ratio, which it calls the one the market uses most. EBITDA is operating profit before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, close to the cash the operation generates. The lesson: looking at debt alone is not enough; you have to look at the cash that pays it.

The thesis draws on this ratio but does not use it in the tests: it measures cash by profit over assets, over invested capital and by market value (screen 04).

08 Panels · Table 5 and Figures 1 to 18

Of the 360 combinations tested in panels, 18 passed

Beyond the traits alone, the thesis combined them in two ways: side by side in the same model (+) or multiplied into a single number (×). Pick the cash measure, the controls and the combination to see on which debt yardsticks the test passed.

Our reading: with a 5% margin, about 1 test in 20 passes by chance even with no relationship at all; out of 360 tests, that would be around 18. This does not mean the 18 are chance, but it calls for caution.

09 The mixes · Figures 1, 3 to 5, 9 to 13, 15 to 18

The same mix pointed to more debt with ROIC and to less with ROA and the market

In the 13 panels that passed with multiplied traits (×), the direction depended only on the cash measure: every ROIC one pointed up, every ROA and market one pointed down. Each block is one panel; above the floor, more debt; below, less.

A multiplied number blends the traits: the sign tells where the mix points, not which trait weighs more. The page shows only the direction; coefficient sizes are not comparable across measures.

10 The surprise · panels and cross-section, Appendix D

In all 35 tests, more unstable cash went with more debt

Hypothesis 2 said steady cash makes room to owe more. Whenever cash swings entered as a separate piece and passed the test, the sign was the opposite: companies that swung more owed more. That held in the panels (5 of 5) and in the comparisons across companies (30 of 30).

“not by policy, but by necessity”

The thesis’s reading: in crisis periods, cash falls and swings more, and the company is forced to borrow. This is a proposed explanation, not a tested one: the thesis does not separate crisis years.

12 In practice · limits · credits

Look at cash over time, not just today’s debt

Managers

Steady cash did not come with more debt in the data. The thesis reads the debt of swinging companies as necessity in hard times, not choice. Our reading: it is worth asking whether a company’s debt is a plan or a lifeline.

Banks and suppliers

For the thesis, looking only at how much a company already owes leaves out the cash it generates today and may generate tomorrow. Cash growth was the trait that most often pointed to more debt.

Researchers

The thesis presents the modified trade-off as a preliminary proposal and suggests testing it with companies from other countries. Other works by the same advisor returned to cash and debt with larger samples.

Balance-sheet readers

The yardstick changes the conclusion: the same mix of traits pointed in opposite directions with ROA and with ROIC, and average debt runs from 17% to 59% of assets depending on what counts as debt.

What this research does not say

  • Moving together is not causing. The tests show association; the crisis and borrowing-by-necessity explanation was not tested. The thesis also cites a small sample, with survivorship bias (only companies with data in every year), and growth measured in the past, when what matters is the future.
  • Many tests. 18 of 360 panels passed, close to what the 5% margin would let through by chance (screen 08). · our observation
  • Comparisons across companies. In the cross-section, each company’s average is repeated across the nine years (846 rows for 94 companies), which tends to make results look firmer than they are. · our observation
  • Scales to check. In Appendix B, the market measure (mean 53,653) and net debt (mean 4.6 million) are not on a ratio scale, the mean of “ROA growth” in the cross-section equals that of ROA itself, and the printed formula of Div6 repeats that of Div2. That is why the page shows only directions and counts. · our observation
  • Only Brazilian listed companies. 94 non-financial B3 companies, from 2008 to 2016; the thesis suggests expanding to companies from other countries.

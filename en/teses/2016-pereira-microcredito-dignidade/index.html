Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

Microcredit money almost never went only into stock

Microcredit lends small sums, with no collateral, to people who run a tiny business; instead of collateral, a group of neighbors and friends answers for everyone’s debt. The rule says the money is for buying stock. The researcher followed eight such groups in the state of Paraíba, Brazil, and heard 46 people. Of the 23 group members, only 4 used the money only for stock. In 12 screens.

Author: Gilvanete Dantas de Oliveira Pereira Advisor: Prof. Dr. Maria Luisa Mendes Teixeira Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 What it is · group microcredit

A small loan with no collateral, where the group answers for everyone

In Brazil, microcredit has been public policy since 2005. Borrowers have no assets to pledge, so the solidarity group takes their place: three to seven people who know each other, live or work nearby and run separate businesses. If one does not pay, the others do. The credit agent goes to the client, not the other way round.

03 The lenses · dignity encounters

In every encounter between people, dignity can be raised or wounded

The thesis started from an idea by researcher Nora Jacobson: every interaction is a “dignity encounter.” When both sides stand on equal footing, dignity is promoted; when one side has far more power, it tends to be violated. Since a solidarity group brings together people who know and trust each other, the question was: do these encounters explain where the money goes?

The thesis also used legal scholar Ingo Sarlet’s four dimensions of dignity. Keep the highlighted one in mind: it returns in the discovery.

04 How the research looked · interviews and visits

The researcher went to the homes and businesses of those who took the money

It is qualitative research: instead of measuring, it seeks to understand the meaning of what people do. In the records of an Oscip (a nonprofit that lends microcredit) from 2000 to 2015, the author chose 8 groups: 4 that had paid on time for years and 4 in arrears. She then widened the listening, after finding that people borrowed from several institutions at once and did not put everything into stock.

06 The result · Table 6 and section 4.2.2

Only 4 of the 23 people put the money only into stock

Group microcredit exists for working capital: buy stock, sell it, pay the installment. Every interviewee knew that. Even so, none of the eight groups used the money only that way, and experience made no difference: those with many loans and those with few behaved alike.

07 Why · Chart 1 and the extra interviews

The money comes in cash, and life has other urgencies

The loan comes in cash, costs less than store credit and renews: whoever pays on time can take more next time. That is why it also draws people who need money for something else. Eight interviewees told of acquaintances who used the loan differently. Tap a use to see the reason they gave:

The “little extra”

Most of it, I invest. [...] The rest I spend. [...] If there’s a little extra, I take it right away.EA4 · extra interview · 3 years borrowing

Of the last loan, R$ 1,800, R$ 800 went into stock. The rest, EA4 said, goes to other things, sometimes it’s medicine, when we run out, because kids are always getting sick. The “little extra” is the surplus: when the lender releases more than the business needs, the gap makes other uses easy.

09 Dignity encounters · Table 6 and section 4.4

The group that helps is the same one that hurts

Every group had at least two kinds of encounter. When someone falls ill or is short of the installment, the others cover. When someone does not pay, everyone’s name goes on the debtors’ list, and the friendship ends. Violation appeared where one side held more power or authority: employer and household worker, parents and children, and most strongly among the youngest and the oldest.

10 The unexpected · “the loan that works out”

Paying on time does not mean using it as intended

The author began by assuming that those who had paid on time for years put the money into the business. That is not what she saw. For clients and agents, microcredit is the loan that works out: the one repaid on time. Lenders do not follow up on how the money is used after releasing it; they only call when a payment is late. Switch the lens:

They want to be paid fully on time. One day late and the phone is already ringing.EA6 · extra interview

11 The discovery · chapter 5

Culture outweighs the encounter: the Brazilian “jeitinho” decides where the money goes

Dignity encounters did not explain how the money was used: other uses showed up with promotion and with violation. What explained it was the historical-cultural dimension of dignity: what counts as “dignified” changes with culture. In Brazil, that brings in the jeitinho, the creative way of getting around a rule to solve a problem. Is paying a bill to get out of a tight spot undignified?, the author asks. See each side’s jeitinho:

Contribution 1. Dignity encounters cannot be seen apart from the culture in which they happen.
Contribution 2. Passing the money to third parties is also explained by excessive trust in personal relationships, which opens room for bad faith (an idea from sociologist Mark Granovetter).
12 Takeaways

Without a clear social goal, the solidarity group becomes just a way to get credit

In the thesis, groups worked more to protect their own members and get as much money as possible than to make sure it went into the business. The author suggests moving to microfinance, because low-income people’s needs go beyond the business.

For microcredit lenders

  • Set clear social goals and criteria: what loan, for whom. Without that, the jeitinho prevails.
  • Remember the client portfolio belongs to the lender, not the agent: too much trust between agent and client weakens the process.

For public policy

  • The poor are not reaching microcredit: no group member had income below R$ 220 per person, the poverty line used in the thesis.
  • In Paraíba, more than 2 million people are vulnerable to poverty (2010 figure cited in the thesis). How many reach microcredit?

For funders and researchers

  • Fund hands-on training for clients, who have no time for management courses.
  • Study leadership inside and outside the groups, which seemed decisive in how they form.

What this research does not say

  • A single central explanation. Time did not allow reaching one central category explaining where the money goes. · according to the author
  • How many do this. Groups chosen by convenience; extra interviews among the researcher’s friends and relatives. The stories show how, not how often. · our note
  • An outside view. The researcher has run a microcredit Oscip in Paraíba since 1996. That opened doors and records, and may also shape the reading. · our note
  • Other places and times. The picture is of Paraíba, April to June 2016, and of lenders that work with solidarity groups. · our note

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

After the customer pays the bill, another fight begins

Taxes, payroll, music royalties, noise fines: each rule gives someone the power to take a slice of what a bar has earned. The thesis listened to people who live this in São Paulo and proposes that the way each owner reads a rule (costly, impossible to follow or fair) pulls the response: comply, dodge it or grow less. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The thesis’s idea

Winning the customer does not end the fight over the money

Strategy theories usually stop at the moment a company beats its competitors. The thesis goes further: after the sale, other players fight over slices of what the company earned, each with the strength a rule gives them.

Struggle rent: “the share of the value generated by the company that is captured by an economic agent, conditioned by a formal rule of the institutional environment”. The author’s definition.

The order of the stops is ours; the players, rules and quotes come from the thesis interviews.

03 The thesis model

Between the rule and the decision lies the owner’s reading of it

The initial bet was simple: government rules (laws, norms, inspection) weigh on the decisions of those who run the business, and those decisions set how much value the company keeps. After the interviews, the author added a piece in the middle: qualification, the label the owner gives each rule.

The author calls these readings “institutional qualifiers”. Tap each piece of the drawing.

04 How the study looked · 5 interviews

Five long conversations with people who live the trade

Between July 2019 and February 2020, the author interviewed four people who are partners running bars and restaurants in São Paulo (from 1 to 10 venues each) and one from a law firm that specialises in the trade.

  1. Transcribed the conversations in NVivo, a text analysis program.
  2. Cut out passages and named them with the interviewees’ own words (“unfeasible”, “having a coffee”).
  3. Grouped the names into categories, and the categories into three big groups: the rules, how rules are read, and the responses.
  4. Checked what was said against the text of the laws, and discussed the results with the interviewees and other researchers.

05 The rules · 195 passages

Taxes and labour law top the complaints, with noise close behind

The interviewees mentioned 21 kinds of rule. Taxes and labour laws add up to 86 of the 195 passages; next come the city’s noise control (PSIU), tips and permits.

Height = number of passages tagged with the rule (Table 20). Counting passages shows what was talked about, not each rule’s weight on the books.

06 How rules are read · 115 passages

Almost everything said about the rules is negative

When they describe the rules, the interviewees talk about high cost, demands impossible to meet, complexity and inspectors with too much power: 101 of 115 passages. Praise shows up in 14, tied mostly to the new labour rules, the Economic Freedom Provisional Measure, the state tax and health inspection.

07 Rule × reading · Table 24

Each rule has its reputation: tax is complex and costly, noise is impossible to comply with

NVivo crossed the passages: how many times each rule and each reading appeared together. Labour law and tips are, above all, costly; tax is the most complex; PSIU, health inspection and ECAD are the most “unfeasible”.

Each column is a rule × reading combination; height = shared passages. Red = negative reading, blue = positive. Appearing together does not mean cause.

08 The responses · 80 passages

Not complying with the rule is the most cited response

Of the 80 passages on what to do about the rules, 53 talk about not complying: evading taxes, cultivating ties with inspectors, not passing on tips, using tricks to stay in SIMPLES. Even those who say they comply think most of the trade does not.

The quotes mix each person’s own business with what they see in the trade; the thesis does not separate the two. SIMPLES is Brazil’s simplified tax regime for small firms, which a company loses as it grows.

09 The unexpected

Two responses were not in the plan: lobbying and growing less

Before fieldwork, the author expected two reactions to the rules. The interviews yielded four. Which ones do you think were already expected?

10 The finding · Table 25

Rules seen as costly or impossible appear with non-compliance; inspectors who guide, with compliance

When NVivo crosses the reading of a rule with the response, the links do not mix: high cost, unfeasible and complex only appear with non-compliance and growing less; good social purpose and guidance, only with compliance. This is where the thesis’s central proposal comes from.

There are 25 co-occurrences in all (our count): few, from five conversations. The author treats them as hypotheses to test with numbers, not as proof of cause.

12 So what?

Those who follow the rules compete with those who don’t

In the interviews, those who pay for soundproofing, pass on tips or pay their taxes compete for the same customer with those who do none of that. For the author, the thesis’s main practical contribution is the map of rules and responses in the trade.

For those who make and enforce rules

  • Inspection seen as guidance appeared linked to compliance; rules seen as unfeasible, to non-compliance. (thesis)
  • Check whether rules fit together: the smoking law sends customers outside to smoke, and after 1 a.m. that clashes with the One O’Clock Law. (thesis example)

For business owners

  • Trade associations settled the tip deadlock through negotiation, with a dedicated collective agreement. (thesis)
  • Dodging a rule has a cost: front partners, dependence on the inspector, debts that force you to abandon the company. (our reading of Figures 39 to 44)

For researchers

  • Test the model with numbers, to see whether the readings are truly distinct and whether they explain the responses. (author)
  • Listen to other cities and sectors, and study political lobbying more closely. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Five conversations. Few interviews allow depth but limit the model’s reach. · according to the author
  • One city, one sector. Only bars and restaurants in the city of São Paulo; other places may have other readings and responses. · according to the author
  • Together is not cause. The matrices show what appeared together in the interviews; the author says he does not claim causality. · according to the author
  • A passage is not a weight. Counting passages shows what was talked about most, not how much money each rule takes, and the thesis does not measure the slices. · our note
  • The trade seen from inside. Accounts of non-compliance mix one’s own business with what one sees in competitors; inspectors, staff and government were not heard. · our note

What is a company worth beyond its balance sheet?

Brands, patents, know-how: the balance sheet barely records these intangible assets. The thesis estimated their value in 1,698 listed companies in Germany, England and Portugal, from 1999 to 2016. In all three countries, market value tracked that estimate; shareholder return, hardly at all.

02 The problem · sections 1.1, 2.3 and 2.4

The balance sheet only sees a brand when someone pays for it

Under the international accounting rule (IAS 38), the brand, the winning recipe or the team a company builds in house barely enter the balance sheet. Only when another company buys it does the gap between the price paid and the book value show up, as goodwill. Until then, that value stays hidden.

The thesis notes that on the stock market a company is worth on average twice its book value (Morgan Stanley world index, cited in section 1.1). Part of that gap is the intangible.

03 How to measure · section 2.5.9, Gu and Lev (2011)

The intangible is what is left of earnings after paying for the machines and the idle money

The thesis uses the Gu and Lev method. First, a “normal” earnings figure for the company, built from six years of EBITDA. Then it subtracts what machines and buildings would earn on their own and what invested money would earn. What is left is the intangible-driven earnings (IDE). Move the controls.

Finally, the thesis projects this surplus into the future in three periods (Figure 3) and brings it all to today’s value, discounting at 1.5% a year: that is the intangible capital (IC). Added to the intangibles the balance sheet already records, it becomes the comprehensive value (CV).

04 Each country’s setting · Tables 7 to 13

Each country’s economic setting enters the math through the rates

Gu and Lev used United States rates. The thesis switched to each country’s rates, 1999 to 2016. For machines and buildings, the rise in property prices net of inflation. For money, the interbank interest rate (Euribor; Libor in England) plus country risk, measured by the insurance against default on government debt (CDS).

06 Sensitivity tests · sections 2.5.9.2 and 2.5.9.5

Changing the assumptions in the math moved the result by less than 1%

The thesis added two tests to the method. One changes the weight of each EBITDA year: equal weights, or weights that rise toward the latest year, as in Gu and Lev. The other changes the forecast growth of the economy, from 0.5% to 2.5% a year. The thesis calls the differences irrelevant and keeps the Gu and Lev weights and 1.5% a year, the IMF forecast.

07 Hypotheses 1 and 2 · Tables 28, 32, 72, 76, 116 and 120

R&D spending went together with the intangible in all three countries

The thesis expected three kinds of spending in one year to come with more intangible the next year: R&D, purchases of machinery and equipment (CAPEX) and selling, general and administrative expenses (SG&A), where marketing and training sit. R&D pointed to more intangible in all three countries. The other two changed sign depending on the country.

We classified by the p-value in each hypothesis’s table: below 5%, confirmed; between 5% and 10%, a hint; above 10%, no clear effect. The thesis’s summary tables mark some of these results more strongly.

08 The finding · hypothesis 4, Tables 40, 84 and 128

In all three countries, market value tracked the value that includes the intangible

Hypothesis 4 compares one year’s comprehensive value (estimated intangible capital + balance-sheet intangibles) with the market value of the shares the following year. In all three countries, more comprehensive value came with more market value, with a p-value of 0.1% or less. The columns show how much of the differences the model explains.

09 The surprise · hypothesis 3, Tables 36, 80 and 124

More intangible-driven earnings came with slightly less shareholder return

Total shareholder return (TSR) adds the share price rise and the year’s dividends. Hypothesis 3 tested whether one year’s intangible-driven earnings (IDE) came with more return the next year, alongside EBITDA. Place your bet before you look.

10 Hypotheses 5 to 10 · Tables 44 to 64, 88 to 108 and 132 to 152

The six intangibility ratios barely explained shareholder return

Each hypothesis set a ratio created by Lev and by Gu and Lev against the next year’s shareholder return. Of the 18 combinations (6 ratios × 3 countries), 3 were confirmed, all in Germany, and 2 gave a hint, one of them the opposite way. Even when it passed the test, the ratio explained almost none of the return: at most 4% of the differences.

With thousands of observations, a tiny effect can pass the test. That is why it is worth looking at how much it explains. In the two English cases where the ratio came out with p 0.0000 and the whole model did not pass, we follow the model, as the thesis text does. Our reading.

11 In practice · limits · credits

The intangible helps understand a company’s price, not next year’s gain

Managers

The balance sheet does not show what the company has built in brands and knowledge, but the market seems to see it: market value tracked the estimated intangible in all three countries. R&D was the spending that went with that intangible in all of them.

Investors and analysts

The Gu and Lev method puts a number on what is not on the balance sheet. But, in the data, the intangibility ratios barely explained the following year’s return. Our reading: they help understand value, not forecast gains.

Accountants and regulators

The thesis notes that the rule (IAS 38) records intangibles at cost and only upon a purchase. The gap between book and market value is, to a large extent, this value accounting does not reach.

Researchers

The thesis suggests splitting companies by sector, by size and by tangible or intangible intensity, studying other countries and using other measures of value besides shareholder return.

What this research does not say

  • Moving together is not causing. The panels show association from one year to the next, not that the intangible causes market value. · our note
  • Math with choices. The thesis lists as limits the choice of rates that subtract machines and money, the discount rate of the intangible capital and the historical cost of machines, which tends to inflate the intangible.
  • Very different companies together. All sectors and sizes in the same panel, and Portugal with only 28 companies. A single measure of value creation, shareholder return.
  • Scales to check. Average comprehensive value is 13 times average market value in Germany and 12 times smaller in Portugal (Tables 21 and 109), and the stars in the summary tables do not match the p-values in each hypothesis’s table. So the page shows only directions, p-values and how much each model explains. · our note
  • The economic setting enters through the rates. The thesis does not test inflation or interest as variables in the panels: each country’s setting enters through the rates in the intangible math and the growth test. · our note

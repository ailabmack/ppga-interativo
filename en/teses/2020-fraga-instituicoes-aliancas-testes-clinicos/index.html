Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

Clinical trial companies praise the law and run into the agency’s delays

Before reaching the pharmacy, a new drug is tested on people. In Brazil, these trials are usually run by a contracted company, the CRO (contract research organisation), in an alliance with the drug maker. The thesis heard nine of these companies to understand how the country’s rules weigh on these alliances and how managing the partnership responds. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Elio Augusto Fraga Advisor: Prof. Dr. Walter Bataglia Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The problem

A new drug is tested on people in four phases, before and after it reaches the market

The road is long, costly and regulated. In Brazil, no trial on people starts without an authorisation letter from ANVISA, the health regulatory agency, and without ethical clearance from CONEP, the national research ethics commission. Tap a stop on the road:

03 Who runs the trial

The drug maker hires a CRO, and the contract decides who does what

Since the 1980s, the industry has handed much of its testing to specialised firms, the CROs. The thesis studies this pair as a contractual strategic alliance. The contract can be the whole package or just a piece of it. Pick the type and watch the tasks change sides:

    04 The thesis’s ideas

    Six propositions link the country’s rules, partnership management and results

    Formal institutions are the country’s written rules: laws, norms and the agencies that apply them. Relational capability is the skill of managing the alliance: coordinating, learning, getting ahead and changing the deal. Performance is satisfaction with results, goals, knowledge and money. The interviews added a fourth element, the agency’s power. Tap a proposition:

    Propositions to be tested, not measured results

    Figure 4 of the thesis, redrawn. The propositions were induced from the interviews and, in the author’s words, are “not definitive”, to be tested statistically in future studies.

    05 How the research looked

    Nine executives from five CROs and four drug makers explained how it works

    The author built a list of companies that run clinical trials (from the international registry clinicaltrials.gov and the industry associations) and interviewed the people who run the trials, between November 2018 and December 2019, in São Paulo. The conversations followed categories set in advance, and the cases were crossed two by two until the propositions emerged. Tap a layer of the funnel:

    07 What it found

    Abroad, approval takes 3 months; here, about a year

    That is how the CRO managers summed up the difference. The thesis also cites the industry association (INTERFARMA): reviewing a request takes 12 months on average, and Brazil missed out on 110 clinical trials in three years, mostly because the sponsor gave up.       09 The finding

      Managing the partnership cushions the blow of delay, but it costs more work

      While the request sits with the agency, the team does not stand still. The managers described what they do to keep the process moving. For the thesis, these relational capability routines act as a buffer between the rules and performance (proposition P4). Tick what the team does:

      The power to decide stays with the agency, and dialogue runs one way

      11 What it found

      To escape the wait, some drug makers take trials abroad

      Taxes, exchange rates and the cost of doing business in Brazil cut the margin, the companies say; what makes it unviable is the regulatory timescale (P6). Three drug makers described their ways out. Tap a route:

      Quotes from drug makers E6, E7 and E8 (section 5.3) and the discussion of specific objective 3 (section 5.4), which mentions moves to Argentina, Bolivia, China, Chile, India, Paraguay and Uruguay. For the author, when the drug reaches Brazil it arrives 10 to 15 years late and much more expensive.

      12 So what?

      Without the agency there is no trial, and without trials there is no medicine

      The sentence is the author’s: without the agency there is no clinical trial, and without clinical trials there is no medication. For him, the country’s rules set what can be done, in what order, how fast and at what cost; managing the partnership helps, but cannot override them.

      For the agency and government

      • Create a channel for dialogue: a committee with associations of CROs, industry and NGOs, or a forum companies can turn to. (author)
      • Standardise how reviewers assess requests and simplify importing trial supplies. (author)
      • Open a debate on telemedicine, virtual trials and real-world research. (author)

      For CROs and drug makers

      • Treat the relationship with the agency as part of managing the alliance: anticipate declarations, know the reviewer, check every import form.
      • Act together through industry associations, which proved more effective with the agency. (author)

      For researchers

      • Test the six propositions with numbers and a larger sample. (author)
      • Study the power of regulators over alliances, a topic that emerged from the data. (author)

      What this research does not say

      • Propositions, not proof. The study is exploratory, with companies chosen for convenience; the six links still need statistical testing. · according to the author
      • One side of the table. Only the companies spoke; no one from ANVISA or CONEP was heard about the complaints. · our observation
      • Nine conversations at one point in time. One interview per company, in São Paulo, between 2018 and 2019; six of the nine talked about trials in general, not one specific alliance. · author and our observation
      • Outside figures. Average timescales, the ranking and missed trials come from industry associations cited in the thesis, not from its own data. · our observation

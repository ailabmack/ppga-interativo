When choosing a partner, does a good name count?

With managers of 132 alliances of pharmaceutical companies in Brazil, the thesis shows that a partner’s good reputation goes hand in hand with more trust and with more successful alliances, and that almost half of that effect travels through trust. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The problem

Every alliance is a bet on the other side

By joining forces, a company gains what it lacks on its own, but it is exposed: the partner may act only in its own interest. Contracts and controls are costly and do not cover everything. So decision-makers also look at the partner’s reputation and at the trust it inspires. Try the test the thesis gave to managers in the sector:

The R&D director of a drug company is going to develop a new molecule to map cancer cells. They can team up with OBLIX, an established company with a long track record of successful partnerships in the field, or with XENON, a newly founded company with researchers respected for their deep technical expertise. Which one?

03 What was measured

Reputation has six faces; trust has two

Corporate reputation is what the market thinks of a company based on what it has done. The thesis measures it through six faces, following Fombrun and colleagues (2000). Trust has two: believing the partner means well (goodwill) and that it is competent. And alliance performance combines results and accumulated experience.

The statements come from the thesis questionnaire (Table 10), answered from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In all, 20 reputation statements, 9 on trust and 4 on performance.

04 How the research looked

First, listen to decision-makers; then, count

The thesis mixes two ways of looking. The main one is a questionnaire answered by alliance managers. Alongside it, conversations with four experienced managers in the sector, built around four short invented stories (“vignettes”), to check whether the model’s ideas make sense in practice.

05 The questionnaire

Of 832 companies invited, 132 valid answers

Every company in a database of 832 drug companies, pharmaceutical chemical makers and biotech firms was invited by email. 145 answered (17.43%). Thirteen questionnaires were dropped because more than 76% of the answers were the same, a sign of autopilot answering. Each of the remaining 132 is about one alliance with a partner.

The minimum calculated for the model was 68 cases; the sample has almost twice that (section 6.2.2).

08 Hypothesis 1 · 132 questionnaires

Rarely mentioned in conversation, the workplace weighed as much as the other faces

In the questionnaires, the six faces together make up reputation, and each weighs almost the same (0.81 to 0.87, on a scale that goes up to 1). And the more reputation managers saw in the partner, the more they trusted it: the link came out at 0.863, and reputation explains 74.5% of the differences in trust. 09 Hypotheses 2 and 3 · the finding

Almost half of reputation’s weight reaches results through trust

On its own, the partner’s reputation is linked to alliance performance at 0.717. When trust enters the model, the direct link drops to 0.385: the rest goes through trust (reputation → trust → performance). Both routes count, and together they explain 55.2% of the differences in performance.

Our calculation: the route through trust is 0.864 × 0.385 ≈ 0.333; added to the direct route (0.385), it gives 0.718, practically the 0.717 of the model without trust. For the author, this is partial (“complementary”) mediation.

Figures 14 and 16 (models 2 and 3), with each link’s t in brackets; above 1.96 the link is statistically clear.

10 All the tests

All three hypotheses held; neither idea changes the other’s weight

The author also tested whether reputation changes how much trust weighs on performance, and the reverse. In both cases the effect was almost zero (−0.032): reputation and trust add up, but neither strengthens nor weakens the other.

The test used is Student’s t (5,000 resamples of the sample, the “bootstrap”). The black line marks 1.96: to its right, the link is statistically clear at 5%. Bars above 10 are cut; the value appears beside them.

11 So what?

A good name helps cover what the contract doesn’t

For the author, reputation and trust complement contracts: they reduce uncertainty and the costs of watching over the partner.

For those building alliances

  • Take reputation into account when looking for new partners and when keeping current ones. (author)
  • A positive reputation attracts partners and signals less risk, which helps the partnership last. (author)

For those looking after reputation

  • Set up processes and routines to build, manage and recover reputation in a crisis. (author)
  • Our reading: in the conversations, what weighed most in a scandal was the media and clear blame (screen 7).

For research funders

  • A positive reputation, built on successful projects, could be one more criterion when granting R&D funding. (author)

What this research does not say

  • One sector, one moment. It holds for pharmaceutical alliances in Brazil in 2017; it cannot be extended to other sectors or countries. · according to the author
  • Overlapping questions. “I trust the partner company” counts as reputation, and “the partner has a good reputation” counts as trust; this helps explain such a strong link (0.863). · our observation
  • Satisfaction, not figures. Performance is how satisfied the manager says they are with the alliances, not measured profit or deadlines. · our observation
  • A snapshot. Each manager answered once, about the partner and about the results; the study is cross-sectional and shows what goes together. · our observation
  • The middle route. The route through trust (≈ 0.333) is our calculation; the thesis did not test that indirect effect separately, and the author suggests another possible mediator: the ability to manage partnerships. · our observation and the author’s

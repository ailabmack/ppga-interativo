How old do you feel?

At a contact centre where the average age is 22, most of the 272 people surveyed said they feel older than they are. And those who feel older worry more about being judged by their age and feel less included in their team. Explained in 11 screens.

08 The discovery

Those who feel older fear the age label more and feel less included

All five links were confirmed, all with p below 0.01. The strongest come from subjective age: it goes with more stereotype threat (0.640) and with less inclusion (−0.624). Tap an arrow:

Table 9 and Figure 8 (final model in SmartPLS 4). Arrow thickness = standardised coefficient (β): from −1 to 1, the further from zero, the stronger the link; a negative sign means “one goes up, the other goes down”. Adjusted R² for inclusion = 0.696. The data come from a single point in time: they show links, not proof of cause.

10 The test

Gender, marital status, education and time at the company do not change the picture

What if the links came from something else, such as being a woman or a man, having more schooling or more years of work? The author added these factors to the model, one by one and together, and the coefficients barely moved (the largest difference was 0.027). 11 So what? · limits and credits

Inclusion depends not only on the age on your ID, but on how people feel and how they think they are seen

For those who lead contact centres

Look after people, not only technology: value every age, train on age bias, give equal chances of internal promotion and make the first job of those who start at 18 more humane. (author)

For HR

In hiring and training, three fronts: do not tie tasks to an age (technology and speed are not “a young person’s thing”), do not put the threatened identity in the spotlight, and recognise each person for what they do, moving from “talking about” to “talking with”. (author)

For researchers

The thesis leaves Portuguese versions of the subjective-age, meta-stereotype and stereotype-threat scales. Next steps: see how subjective age changes over a week, also hear from managers and compare cultures and generations. (author)

What this research does not say

  • One company, one moment. The 272 come from a single unit with no daily targets, surveyed once: the links do not prove cause or hold for the whole sector. · our observation
  • “Older” workers aged 22. The average age is 22.3: the statements about “older workers” were answered mostly by young people. · our observation
  • All in one questionnaire. The four scales move closely together (correlations from 0.67 to 0.82, in absolute value). The author tested for bias with a marker variable and with social desirability, with no change. · our observation
  • What a high score means. The meta-stereotype scale asks about traits such as “competent” and “friendly”; the thesis reads high scores as more weight of meta-stereotypes. It is worth measuring again with other statements. · our observation
  • Inclusion questions with little weight. Six of the 24 fell below the expected level and were kept; the author suggests reviewing them in the sector where, for example, there is little freedom to collaborate and decide. · according to the author

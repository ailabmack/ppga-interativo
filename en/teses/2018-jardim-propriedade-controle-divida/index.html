Does who controls a company change how much it borrows?

The thesis followed 128 Brazilian listed companies from 2010 to 2016, traced who really owns each one and tested whether a family, the government, BNDES (Brazil's development bank), pension funds or foreign investors among the shareholders go together with more or less debt. In 12 screens.

03 How the research looked · Chart 4

To find the real owner, you have to group those who vote together

Often the shareholder on record is another company, with owners of its own. The author read the reference forms companies file with Brazil's securities regulator (CVM) and followed each chain to the end: the "ultimate largest shareholder". See four examples from the thesis.

A family counts as a single shareholder. Relatives, under the thesis's assumption, share interests and decide together.

04 How the research looked · Charts 9 and 11

With 10% of the votes, a shareholder already has a voice; with more than 50%, it is in command

The thesis did not measure ownership on a continuous scale: it split companies at two cut-offs. Above 10% of the votes, Brazilian corporate law already gives a shareholder oversight powers; above 50%, it is in charge. Drag and see what each slice of votes guarantees.

    08 The finding · hypothesis 1

    An owner with a voice goes with more debt; an absolute owner, with less

    The hypothesis was that concentrating votes would reduce debt. With the 50% cut-off, that is what showed up, in 6 of the 8 yardsticks. With the 10% cut-off, the sign flipped. For the author, concentration helps a company borrow up to a point; too much of it gets in the way. Switch the cut-off.

    09 The finding · hypothesis 2

    The more a family is in charge, the more yardsticks show debt falling

    With more than 10% of the votes in a family's hands, debt fell in 3 yardsticks. With more than 50%, in 5. For the author, families are averse to losing control, and debt brings lenders who demand rules and accountability. Switch the cut-off.

    12 In practice · limits · credits

    Before looking at the debt, look at who is in charge

    Family companies

    For the author, aversion to losing control leads families to fund operations with their own cash. Our reading: it is worth knowing what that choice costs in growth that does not happen.

    Banks and lenders

    The author reads foreign investors as a sign of better governance for lenders, and the government as a source of cheaper debt. Owner type is one more clue in credit analysis.

    Public policy

    Companies with BNDES or state pension funds among their shareholders show more long-term debt. For the author, the government can make debt cheaper, which makes leverage attractive.

    Researchers

    Suggested next steps: compare with Mercosur, Latin American and European countries, and use methods that deal with cause and effect (dynamic panels, simultaneous equations, instrumental variables).

    What this research does not say

    • Relationship, not cause. It may be the owner that changes the debt, or the debt that attracts a certain kind of owner; the author suggests methods to separate the two in future studies. · according to the author
    • Direction only, not size. The yardsticks have different scales and some averages in Table 4 are hard to read, so the page shows direction and strength of evidence. · our note
    • Many tests. With 72 results at 10%, about 7 would come out "significant" by chance. There were 32 (25 at 5%): well above chance, but each block on its own calls for caution. · our count
    • The 10% cut-off. The thesis does not say how many companies fall below each cut-off. Since the largest shareholder holds 63% of the votes on average, the group below 10% is likely small, and the "concentration helps" result rests on it. · our note
    • Brazil, 2010 to 2016. Only listed, non-financial companies; half of the original sample was left out for lack of data. · according to the thesis

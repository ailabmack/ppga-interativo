Bosses value in others what they learned to be

Why do some managers promote, and others violate, the dignity of the people who work with them? The thesis listened to 93 workers and 12 managers in accounting, finance and auditing and found a mirror relationship: managers tend to value those who resemble what they learned to be, often in childhood, and may hurt those who are different. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Carolina Moreira Otsuru Advisor: Prof. Dr. Maria Luisa Mendes Teixeira Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

10 The discovery

The same management promotes some and hurts others

The thesis calls this a mirror relationship: managers value in others what they learned to be. Those who resemble them tend to gain recognition and room to grow; those who are different are more at risk of being watched, ignored or dismissed. That is why, the author says, management is not only promoting or only violating: it is both. Build a manager with up to three values:

11 From past to present

What was learned in childhood weighs, but does not decide everything

The managers heard learned mostly from parents and grandparents, people now over 70, raised in the middle of the last century. From them come values such as loyalty, hard work, dedication, obedience and sacrifice. But the thesis shows a way out: reflecting on one's own history. One of the people interviewed, E10, was humiliated by authority figures in the family as a child and says that therapy, study and conversations with their son helped them not to repeat this with the team.

12 So what?

Changing management starts with looking at managers' own histories

For the author, many managers repeat without noticing what they learned at home. She asks for an empathetic view of them, but also for concrete action, because those who pay for violations are the workers.

Train managers

  • Courses on what violates and what promotes dignity, focused on accounting, finance and auditing.
  • Attention to what managers barely see: discrimination, sexual harassment, rights.

Listen to workers

  • Confidential, safe reporting channels, with awareness campaigns.
  • Make sure every report leads to action.

Look at one's own history

  • Therapeutic interventions for managers to revisit what they learned and what they value.
  • The author suggests considering candidates' life learning when selecting managers.

What this research does not say

  • How managers actually behave. There was no observation in the workplace; everything comes from what people said. · according to the author
  • What childhood was like, measured. Learning was reconstructed from the interviewees' memories; some were no longer managers. · according to the author
  • Whether it holds for other groups. All the managers interviewed are white, from Brazil's Southeast, in accounting, finance and auditing. · according to the author
  • Whether it is the same manager and team. Workers and managers came from different groups, and there are six life stories; the mirror is a proposed reading, not a test. · our note

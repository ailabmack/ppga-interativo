Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

Rules, taxes and owners change how companies use money, sometimes the opposite way to what was planned

In three essays, the thesis looks at emerging markets: the Chinese rule that forced companies to pay out profit, Brazil’s interest on equity and Brazilian family firms. In each case, the effect depends on who the company is, and two measures meant to help ended up with side effects. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The question · three essays

All profit has three possible destinations, and someone from outside can tamper with each

A company can keep its profit to invest, hand it to shareholders (dividends or interest on equity) or make up the shortfall with debt. The thesis asks what happens when a rule, a tax or the type of owner interferes with these choices. Pick an essay.

Our diagram. The highlighted arrows are the ones the chosen essay studies.

03 Essay 1 · China · 130 companies

In 2012 and 2013, China began requiring listed companies to pay out at least 20% of profit in cash

The rule from China’s securities regulator aimed to protect small investors. To measure its effect, the thesis compared two groups of 65 companies: those that, in the four years before, paid out less than 20% of profit every year (hit by the rule) and those that paid more than 30% (not hit). See, step by step, how the comparison works.

04 Essay 1 · what the rule changed in dividends

Companies began to pay out more profit, but compliance was incomplete

After the rule, the share of profit paid out by the affected companies rose by about 15 percentage points relative to the other group, and their chance of staying below 20% fell by 37 points. Even so, on average over the period, they paid out 7.8% of profit, far from the other group’s 53.4%. For the author, the rule took hold, but not fully.

05 Essay 1 · investment

Looking at the average company, the rule did not change the quality of investment

The thesis measures whether a company invests “in line” with its opportunities. Investing more than they justify is over-investing; investing less is under-investing. In eight tests, the rule’s average effect stayed stuck at zero. For the author, this may hide companies pushed in opposite directions that cancel out on average.

Dot = estimated effect; line = range of ±2 standard errors (our calculation, from the coefficient and the t statistic). “How far off” is in fractions of assets; “chance of over-investing”, in percentage points. Tables 3 and 4.

06 Essay 1 · the turning point · simulator

For a company with little access to credit, the rule made investment miss more

When the thesis separates companies by financial constraint (how costly it is for them to raise outside money), the effect appears. For the most constrained, the mandatory payout takes away the cheapest money they had, and investment drifts away from the ideal. Only in lightly constrained companies did the rule help. 07 Essay 2 · Brazil · 358 companies, 2000–2009

In Brazil, interest on equity was supposed to make shareholders’ money as attractive as debt

Interest on a loan reduces the company’s tax (34%); shareholders’ money does not. This pushes companies towards debt. Since 1996, Brazil has also allowed companies to deduct a kind of “interest” on shareholders’ capital, interest on equity (JCP), an idea the literature saw as a cure for this bias. But there is a condition: the deduction only applies if the money is paid out to shareholders. And the gain depends on who receives it. Pick a type of shareholder.

08 Essay 2 · the discovery

Looked at carefully, interest on equity increases debt instead of reducing it

In a simple comparison, companies that pay more interest on equity have less debt, and the cure seems to work. But those who pay it are not drawn at random: they are companies with more cash and certain types of owner. When the thesis corrects for this choice, the sign flips. For the author, it is a rebound effect: paying interest on equity takes money out of the company, which makes up for it with debt. Go through the four analyses.

We show the direction and the significance. Each analysis’s numbers are on different scales and should not be compared with each other. Tables 10 to 13.

09 Essay 2 · who decides

Companies make little use of interest on equity, and the type of shareholder weighs on the decision to pay it

Only 22% of company-years had interest on equity payments, against 74% with dividends. And, on average across all companies, the interest on equity paid was about one ninth of what they could deduct. The decision follows shareholders’ pockets: with pension funds among the owners, who pay less tax on it, the chance of paying rises; with control through chains of companies (pyramids) or another company as shareholder, for whom it yields little or costs money, it falls. The thesis calls this a clientele effect.

10 Essay 3 · Brazil · 1,568 company-years, 2005–2012

Family in charge lowers performance, and the way of handling risk changes with the family’s role

The thesis separated three situations: the family only controls (more than half of the votes), the family controls and runs it (chairs the company or the board) and the founder runs it. Since families choose which companies to stay in, the thesis corrects for that choice before measuring. Pick a situation and see the panel.

    11 Essay 3 · the unexpected

    In a simple count, family-controlled firms pay half the dividends; with the comparison corrected, they pay more

    Family-controlled firms differ from the others: smaller, older, with more cash. Compared without adjustment, they pay 3.5% of sales to shareholders, against 7.0% for the others. When the thesis accounts for what makes a company a family firm, the sign flips: family control increases dividends. For the author, it is a way for the family to discipline itself and avoid wasting cash, which still does not prevent lower performance.

    12 So what? · limits and credits

    For the author, rules and incentives need to look at the type of company they will affect

    For regulators

    A minimum dividend protects investors, but it can hamper investment by companies with little credit. The results can help redesign the Chinese rule or similar ideas in other countries. (author)

    For tax policy

    If the goal is to reduce the preference for debt, interest on equity needs redesigning: without requiring money to leave the company, as in Belgium and Italy, and without depending on the type of shareholder. (author)

    For family firms and the market

    The lower performance of family firms calls for a more developed capital market, less concentrated control or more effective governance. (author)

    What this study does not say

    • Small or older samples. 130 Chinese companies in essay 1; in Brazil, data from 2000 to 2009 (interest on equity) and 2005 to 2012 (families). Rules and taxes have changed since then. · our observation
    • Short term. In China, the thesis measures only the first years after the rule; the author expects the effect to worsen as companies’ financial slack runs out. · according to the author
    • Corrections rest on assumptions. The results for interest on equity and families depend on the models that correct for who chooses to pay or to be a family firm; the simple comparison can give opposite signs. · our observation
    • Not investment advice. The thesis studies companies’ decisions, not the returns of those who buy their shares. · our observation

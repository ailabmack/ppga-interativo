Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

Between airlines, trust runs through people

Many people buy a ticket from one airline and fly on a plane of another. That only works because airlines form alliances. The thesis interviewed 12 alliance managers at airlines from four continents, all partners of the same Latin American airline, to understand how trust between companies relates to the way the partnership is managed and to the variety of partners. 03 The question

Three ideas no one had studied together

The thesis asks: how does trust between companies interact with the ability to manage the partnership and with the variety of partners, in agreements between airlines? The author says he found no studies combining the three. Tap each block:

04 How the research looked

Twelve alliance managers, from four continents, at the peak of the pandemic

Between December 2019 and May 2020, the author interviewed the people in charge of alliances at the partners of a Latin American airline that owns one of the three largest loyalty programmes in the region. The interview guide was written in Portuguese, Spanish and English. The answers were analysed with categories defined in advance from the literature, with the help of the NVivo software.

05 The case

Outside the global alliances, the focal airline has partners from all three

The world has three global airline alliances. Joining one limits partnerships with members of the others. The focal airline joined none, so its portfolio brings together partners from all three plus independent ones.

Chart 1 and Table 29 of the thesis, which hides the names of the alliances (X, Y, Z) and airlines. Switching to alliance Z is our illustration based on the interviews: with the alliance’s approval, a deal with a member of another one can still happen.

08 The finding · proposition P2

Trust between companies rests on trust between the managers

At the focal airline, a single alliance director handled every partnership for 12 years and signed every contract. The partners talk about him. For the author, building trust between companies from different countries is “an exercise in diplomacy” that takes time and runs through people. See what changes in each situation:

09 The unexpected

Talking about trust made the managers go quiet

The topic kept some invitees away and shortened answers. When the guide reached trust, answers became “much more objective, often without detail and sometimes monosyllabic”. For the author, alliances between different countries make trust more delicate, and the pandemic weighed in. Tap the signs:

Sections 3.3 and 4.3.3 of the thesis. The author also records, “in practically the same proportion”, very open interviews. The quotes about doubts are from the thesis; it says “only two” reported doubts but quotes four excerpts, so we give no total.

10 The turn · proposition P3

Who owns a stake in whom matters more than the number of partners

One idea in the literature says more partners make trust harder. In the case studied, the author did not see that. What mattered was who the partners are: whether they own shares in the company, whether they belong to a global alliance and which group they are part of. Try the controls:

13 So what?

Choosing well who runs the alliances is the main lesson for airlines

For airlines

  • Choose alliance managers carefully: social skills, conflict resolution and trust built between people. (author)
  • Weigh the effect of selling shares to another airline or joining a global alliance on two-party partnerships. (author)

For government and regulators

  • Encourage and strengthen connecting hubs, which play an economic and social role in their regions. (author)
  • Monitor open-skies agreements and contracts between airlines (interline, codeshare, JVA, CPA), which can strengthen regional tourism. (author)

For researchers

  • Test the propositions with numbers, across the 60 airlines in the global alliances or the 290 in IATA. (author)
  • Create common metrics for alliance performance, even a scale for the sector. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Propositions, not proof. Twelve interviews and a single portfolio; the eight propositions still need testing. · according to the author
  • Aviation only. The author does not recommend carrying the conclusions over to alliances in other sectors. · according to the author
  • One side of the partnership. The partners spoke; no one from the focal airline was interviewed. · our observation
  • An unusual moment. Data collection coincided with the near-total halt of aviation in the pandemic, and some interviewees later left their companies. · author and our observation

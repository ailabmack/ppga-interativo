Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

In Brazilian companies, owning more machinery and buildings did not bring more debt

Lenders like collateral they can seize and resell. That is why studies in the United States find that companies with more plants and equipment can borrow more. The author followed 255 companies on the Brazilian stock exchange from 2007 to 2015, and that link did not show up in any of the four debt measures. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The idea

Lenders lend more to whoever offers something they can seize and resell

It is the logic of pawning a watch or financing a car. In a company, the “real” assets, the ones you can touch, are machinery, buildings and land. Accounting calls this group PP&E (plant, property and equipment). The thesis calls them real assets.

Tap an asset to see what the literature reviewed by the author says about it as collateral.

03 What was measured

Debt was measured in four ways

“How much the company owes” depends on the measure. Debt due within a year is not the same as debt spread over several years, and debt can be compared with everything the company owns or only with the owners’ money. The author tested all four.

04 Who was included

255 listed companies, followed for nine years, with gaps in the data

The author used the Compustat database at Mackenzie’s finance lab and left out banks, insurers and other financial firms, whose debt works differently. The plan was to separate buildings, land and machinery, but fewer than half of the companies had those numbers. So the three became a single block, PP&E.

06 The central result

In none of the four measures did machinery and buildings go with more debt

This was the thesis’s main goal. In all four measures, the PP&E sign even came out on the opposite side (less debt), but always too close to zero to say anything. Adding long-term receivables to PP&E changed nothing either.

07 The 18 factors

Almost the whole balance sheet stayed silent

Cash, investments, receivables, brands, profit: none moved clearly with debt. The firm signals were company size and the share of liabilities owed to banks. Inventory and interest expenses gave weak signals that switch sides depending on the measure.

08 What did show up

Larger companies owed more in the long term

When a company grows in assets, long-term debt grows with it, and debt weighs more against the owners’ money. For short-term debt and for total debt over assets, size made no difference.

Source: Tables 6 and 8 (size = logarithm of total assets; banks = debt owed to banks ÷ total liabilities) and section 4.2. Distance in standard errors, our calculation.

09 The unexpected

Looking at pairs alone, machinery seemed to lengthen debt. In the model, the signal fades

In the annex’s correlation table, companies with more PP&E had a larger share of liabilities in the long term and a smaller one in the short term, as the theory expects. But PP&E also moves with other balance-sheet items. When the model puts everything together, almost nothing is left for it.

Source: Annex II (correlation matrix) and Tables 5 and 6. Comparing the two readings is our reading; they use different scales and are not on the same axis. In the annex, short- and long-term debt are shares of total liabilities, not of assets.

10 The author’s explanations

Credit in Brazil follows other rules

The author notes that in Brazil the cost of and access to debt depend heavily on where the money comes from, not just on collateral. Flip the cards. None of these explanations was tested with the thesis’s data.

Source: sections 1.2, 4.1 and 4.2 of the thesis (with Brito, Corrar and Batistella, 2007; Brito and Lima, 2005; Nakamura, Martin and Kimura, 2004). These are the author’s hypotheses for the result, not findings.

11 So what?

What remains, for whom, and what this research does not say

For finance managers

  • Owning plants and equipment was not enough to owe more in this sample. (screen 06)
  • Size mattered more for long-term debt. (screen 08)

For banks and lenders

  • Understanding the role of collateral helps shape corporate policies, especially in crises like the one that began in 2015. (author)
  • Discounting receivables may be playing the role a machine would. (author’s hypothesis)

For economic policymakers

  • More studies on collateral and debt can serve policymakers. (author)
  • Subsidized credit and foreign-exchange lines may weigh more than collateral. (our reading of section 1.2)

For researchers

  • Link debt to the actual collateral in each contract, not only to balance-sheet totals. (author)
  • Separate buildings, land and machinery when data allow. (our reading of Table 1)

Limits

  • Incomplete data. Not every company had each item for all nine years, and buildings, land and machinery had to be merged. · according to the author
  • No interest rates, inflation or subsidized credit in the model. The author says these features of the Brazilian market were not controlled for. · according to the author
  • Numbers that call for caution. In the annexes, the debt measure has a standard deviation in the trillions, which a ratio would not have (a sign of extreme values); two of the four tables repeat the column of another kind of model (random effects); and some printed p-values do not match coefficient ÷ standard error, which we redid. That is why we show no effect sizes. · our note
  • “No relationship” is not “zero relationship”. With data like these, a small effect can go unnoticed. · our note

None of them was born a social entrepreneur

How does someone learn to run a business that seeks profit and social impact at the same time? The thesis listened to 13 founders of four social businesses in Alagoas, a state in Brazil’s Northeast: two in technology and two in health care. Very different businesses, yet the same five-step path, and the founders only came to see themselves as social entrepreneurs along the way. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The topic

A social business wants to make a profit and solve a problem in society

It is neither a charity living on donations nor an ordinary company that looks only at the owners’ returns. Profit is one of the goals, and it keeps the business standing while it creates impact, above all for people on low incomes. Compare the two models:

03 What was known · 124 papers

Hardly anyone had studied how these entrepreneurs become competent

The author searched five databases of scientific papers from 2004 to 2015. He found 124 papers touching on competences and social businesses, nearly always one or the other. Interest jumped in 2014. Tap the steps:

Papers per year (Chart 2): Ebsco, Scielo, Scopus, ISI and Proquest, updated to March 2016. They came from 37 countries: Europe 43%, the Americas 27%, Asia 21%, Oceania 7% and Africa 2%. Only one was from Brazil, and it was about corporate social responsibility.

04 Two ways of seeing competence

The literature answers with lists; the thesis asks how a person becomes competent

The most cited study in the review (Miller and colleagues, 2012) lists 35 competences of a social entrepreneur. Many are those of any company; others are new, such as empathy or valuing impact over money.

The thesis takes another route, Jörgen Sandberg’s: competence is not a kit of traits but a process of becoming. It grows out of the meaning people give to their own work, and that meaning changes with experience.

The 35 competences come from Chart 13 of the thesis, which reproduces Miller and colleagues (2012). The three highlighted are the only ones marked by all six groups in that study (for-profit, hybrid, NGO, entrepreneurs and investors, and what is taught in class). Names as in the original study.

07 The unexpected

The social-entrepreneur identity came after starting, not before

You might picture someone deciding to be a social entrepreneur and then opening the business. In all four stories it went the other way. Nobody had business experience; the awareness of doing social entrepreneurship emerged along the way, through practice and contact with other entrepreneurs.

In the founders’ words
We kind of landed in it by parachute, without even knowing what a social business was. Wow, I have a social business and I’m creating social impact. We realised where we were. You can’t hire a salesperson if you don’t know how to sell. In the NGOs where I worked, I saw they were legal entities but were not organised as businesses, nor professionalised.

Quotes from four founders (sections 6.1.3 and 6.2.3), unnamed, translated by us. In health care, this awareness led to a social screening of patients and to dropping the logic of giving everything for free.

08 Step 5 · Competences

Each business learned what its public needed, from ontologies to sign language

Technical competences were learned along the way, many of them as a group. The education platform needed artificial intelligence and adaptive teaching; the translation app, Brazilian Sign Language (Libras) and 3D animation; the clinic, surgical and patient-care techniques; the association, grant writing and public tenders. 10 Step 4 · Shared meanings

The outlook changed direction, and the whole team came to share it

The change was not the same in both worlds. In technology, partners who thought about the technical fix came to think about the collective. In health care, the outlook was collective from the start; what changed was leaving charity-style aid behind and professionalising management. Drag the timeline:

11 So what?

The path works as a mirror for those just starting out

The author suggests that start-ups use the five elements to assess their own path, and that incubators and business schools use them to train new social entrepreneurs.

Those starting out

Look at the triggers: the unease, closeness to the problem, and how to build the material and human conditions.

Incubators and accelerators

Connect entrepreneurs to networks and training spaces, where the thesis saw the social-business identity take shape.

Business schools

Teach competence as a process of becoming, not just as a list of traits.

12 Takeaway

Above all, an identity built and shared along the way

For the author, what best explains the success of these four businesses is not a list of skills but the social-business identity the founders built and spread through their teams: not charity-style, professional, and aimed at solving a problem in society in a way that also turns a profit.

What this research does not say

  • Whether the path holds elsewhere. These are 13 people from four businesses in Alagoas, two in technology and two in health care. · according to the author
  • What employees and partners think. Only the partners were heard. · according to the author
  • The conflicts. Tensions among partners, and between partners and investors, were left out and are suggested for future study. · according to the author
  • Stories told by those who succeeded. The author himself notes an almost heroic tone in the narratives; there are no failed cases to compare. · our note, based on the thesis

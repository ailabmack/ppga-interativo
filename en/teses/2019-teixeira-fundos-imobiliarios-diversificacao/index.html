In real estate funds, owning several buildings did not make the fund calmer

Theory says that spreading money over several buildings reduces the ups and downs. The thesis followed 74 real estate funds in the IFIX index, month by month, from 2011 to 2018: funds with several buildings swung as much as those with one. Explained in 13 screens.

Author: Odilon Ferraroni Teixeira Advisor: Prof. Dr. Denis Forte Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The subject

A real estate fund splits buildings into units bought on the stock exchange

1Many people pool money in a fund, which buys buildings (shopping malls, offices, warehouses) or securities tied to real estate.
2Rent becomes income paid out to unit holders. Units are sold and bought on the stock exchange, at a price that changes every day.
3For individuals, the income is exempt from income tax if the unit is traded on an exchange, if they hold less than 10% of the units and if the fund has at least 50 holders.
03 The question

Does “don’t put all your eggs in one basket” hold for real estate funds?

The thesis splits the funds into three groups. Before seeing the numbers, place your bet.

Specialized: a single property Diversified: several properties Securities: securities (units of other funds or real estate receivables)

1. Which group goes up and down more from one month to the next?

2. Which group earns more, on average, per month?

05 The method

Each month’s return adds the price change and the income paid

The author gathered the prices and income of each fund (Bloomberg, B3 and CVM) and looked at two windows: the long one, since IFIX was created, and the short one, with the 71 funds that had a price in every month.

The Selic (Brazil’s base interest rate) is the thesis’s “risk-free” rate. 0.85% a month is the average from Jan 2011 to Aug 2018 (Table 5). The other values are made up.

06 Group averages

Diversified funds swung slightly more than single-property funds, in both windows

This is the opposite of what theory expects from diversifying. Securities funds were the calmest. In average return, the difference between groups is small and switches sides depending on the window.

Source: Table 5. Columns: standard deviation of monthly return (how much it moves around the average). Bars: average monthly return; the dashed line is the average Selic rate for the period.

07 The unexpected

In the return-per-swing score, first place changes from one window to the other

The Sharpe ratio measures how much the fund earned above the Selic rate for each point of swing. In the long window, securities funds came 1st and single-property funds came last. In the short one, the specialized funds flipped to the top.

Sharpe = (average return − Selic) ÷ swing

Source: Table 5 (monthly ratio; we checked the calculation against the table’s own averages). Values near zero: in the long window, all three groups earned, on average, a little less than the Selic rate; in the short one, only the specialized funds were clearly above it.

08 By property type

Property type sets funds apart more than strategy does

Between the three groups, average monthly return varied by at most 0.12 points. Between property types, the gap was over 0.8 points. For the author, the property sector influences the relationship between risk and return.

Source: Table 5 (averages) and Table 4 (number of funds). Tap a point. Types with few funds (hotel, hospital, education) may reflect one or two funds. The comparison between the spread of groups and of types is our reading.

09 The models

Diversified funds followed IFIX’s ups and downs more closely

The author ran regressions that explain each group’s return by the return of the fund market (IFIX). The market coefficient says how much the group moves when the index moves. In logistics, it was the opposite.

Sources: Tables 6 and 7 (groups) and 9 and 10 (types, three-factor model with swing). Solid bar: statistically significant coefficient; hollow bar: not significant. Coefficients of standardized variables, for comparing groups, not for reading in reais.

10 Hypothesis 3

Charging a performance fee did not clearly change returns

A performance fee is an extra the manager earns when the fund beats a target. The idea is to align the manager’s interest with the unit holder’s. Of the 74 funds, 12 charge this fee. In 16 comparisons, none showed a statistical difference in return.

0,0114 → 0,0164IFIX coefficient without → with fee (Jan 2011–Aug 2018, Table 16)
0,0122 → 0,0197the same, Mar 2014–Aug 2018: funds with a fee followed the index more closely

Sources: Tables 14 (without controls) and 15 (with controls). On each block, the sign of the fee coefficient (+ or −); none is significant. Tap a block. For the author, the fee did not bring more risk, which does not point to a conflict of interest.

11 Hypothesis 4

Only in securities funds did the exchange price move with the book value

Every unit has two values: the book value (what the buildings are worth divided by the units, usually revised once a year by an appraisal) and the market value (the exchange price). If the fund were wound up, the holder would be paid the book value, not the exchange price.

Illustration

In September 2017, 36 of the 74 funds had a price below book value and 38 above it (Table 3, our count). The thesis tested whether the monthly change in book value helps explain the exchange return:

significant, same direction significant, opposite direction not significant

Sources: Tables 17 (groups), 18 and 19 (types, without controls). With controls, by type (Tables 20 and 21), the picture is the same; by group (Table 15), securities stay significant only in the long window. For the author, this points to information asymmetry: the exchange price does not reflect book value, partly because the buildings are revalued only rarely. In funds of other funds, the book value already follows exchange prices.

12 So what?

Several buildings do not guarantee a steadier fund

For the author, Brazilian real estate funds do not follow the theory’s idea that diversifying reduces risk. The thesis helps to discuss the sector’s rules, especially how buildings are valued.

For investors

  • The exchange price can drift far from book value; in a wind-up, book value is what counts, net of expenses. (author)
  • Property type weighed more than the number of buildings in the averages (screen 08). (our reading)

For administrators and regulators

  • Revaluing buildings once a year, by appraisal, can split book value from market price; this is a risk factor for individuals, who are a large share of holders. (author)
  • The debate on marking buildings to market can make prices more informative. (author)

How the author sums up

  • According to the models, diversified funds had higher returns and higher volatility than specialized ones, except in logistics. (author)
  • In the simple averages (screen 06), the diversified funds’ higher swing appears in both windows; the higher return, only in the long one. (our reading)
Not investment advice. The thesis describes the past (2011 to 2018) of a group of funds; past returns do not guarantee future returns.
13 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • Few funds per group. Few charge a performance fee, and there are few specialized and diversified funds of the same property type; so the analysis by type was limited to malls, offices, logistics and securities. · according to the author
  • Groups compared side by side. Each group had its own regression; the difference between groups was not tested directly, and the simple averages differ only slightly (at most 0.12 points a month). · our observation
  • Return or moving with the market. The author compares groups by the IFIX coefficient; here it appears as how much the group follows the index (screen 09), and return comes from the averages (screen 06). · our observation
  • Today’s funds looking back. The sample is the IFIX funds of September 2017; funds that left before then are not included. · our observation
  • Goes together, not cause. The regressions show association; they do not prove that the strategy or the fee causes the return. · our observation

For the author, these limitations should shrink as the real estate fund market grows.

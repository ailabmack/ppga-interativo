Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2018

What decides what a currency is worth in the long run?

The thesis tested three classic answers: prices, productivity and the competitiveness of countries. In 25 developing countries, 10 pairs of rich countries and 20 European countries, none of them held as a general rule. Each one worked only in some of the countries.

02 Why it matters · chapter 1

The exchange rate moves almost every price in a country

The thesis recalls that the exchange rate weighs on inflation, exports, imports and economic activity, from the small firm to the final consumer. A stable exchange rate helps growth last longer; an unstable one can reverse it.

inflationexportsimportseconomic activity

The thesis has three independent essays. Each tests a different explanation of where the exchange rate goes in the long run, with different countries and years.

03 Essay 1 · the theory · section 2.2

By the oldest rule, the exchange rate should equalize the price of the same basket in both countries

It is purchasing power parity, proposed by Cassel in 1916: with the currency converted at the exchange rate, the same basket should cost the same here and there. In the version tested in the thesis (the relative one), what counts is the change: if prices rise faster in one country, its currency should lose value in the same proportion. Move the prices.

04 Essay 1 · 25 countries × US, 1960–2015 · Table 1

In 89 tests with data, only 6 followed the price rule

For each country and each index, the author tested whether exchange rate and prices move together in the long run and in the proportion the rule demands, always against the dollar. Of 100 possible combinations, 11 had no data. Of the remaining 89, 6 passed, in only four countries: Mexico, Sri Lanka, Saudi Arabia and Trinidad and Tobago.

05 Essay 2 · the theory · section 3.2.1

A country more productive in its factories tends to have a dearer currency

It is the Balassa-Samuelson effect, from 1964. What is sold between countries (tradable goods) tends to cost the same everywhere. What does not travel (a haircut, rent) does not even out. When a country becomes much more productive in what it sells abroad, the prices of what does not travel rise at home, and the currency ends up stronger than the price rule would say. 06 Essay 2 · method · Tables 2 and 3

The author measured productivity in three ways, one of them counting services as “tradable”

Each country's productivity came from the World KLEMS database, split into nine broad sectors. What counts as “tradable” changes from author to author. The thesis tested three versions. The third follows the OECD and the European Commission and puts energy, trade, transport and communication next to agriculture, mining and manufacturing: according to the author, a classification cited in the literature but almost never applied in tests.

Annual data from 1980 to 2007, for Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Spain, Finland, France, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, each compared with the United States. Productivity is total factor productivity (how much is produced with the same labor, capital and inputs).

07 Essay 2 · results · sections 3.4 and 3.5

Productivity explained the exchange rate in 13 of 30 tests, and only Finland passed all three measures

With ten countries and three measures, there were 30 tests. Thirteen confirmed the effect: six with all sectors, four with the Lorenzo version and three with the new one. Belgium, Denmark, the Netherlands and the UK passed in two; Austria, France and Spain in none. Since the effect does not show up in most, the author concludes it cannot be generalized and is weaker than previously thought.

Several tests passed only at the 10% threshold, looser than the usual 5%. The new measure did not score more hits, but the author judges that, in Denmark, Finland and the UK, the curves built with it follow each country's economic history more closely.

08 Essay 3 · method · sections 4.2 and 4.3.1

A country's competitiveness comes in four pillars, measured by 342 criteria

The third essay asks whether these pillars, from the IMD world competitiveness index, explain the productivity of 20 European countries from 2001 to 2018. And it goes further: does the real effective exchange rate (the currency's value against the basket of trading partners, net of inflation) change that relationship? In technical terms, it is a moderator: it strengthens, weakens or switches off a pillar's effect. Tap the pillars.

09 Essay 3 · results · sections 4.3.3 and 4.3.4

With all 20 countries together, the exchange rate had no effect; country by country, it did in 8

In the analysis with all countries together, no model worked: neither did the pillars explain productivity, nor did the exchange rate change that relationship. So the author ran the models country by country. Then the exchange rate showed up as a moderator in 8 of the 20, and in each of them on different pillars. Estonia was the strongest case, with an effect on three pillars.

The tables only report significance, not the size or sign of the effect: the thesis shows where the exchange rate matters, not whether it helps or hurts. The abstract speaks of “half the countries”; the conclusion lists 8 (our count). Several results passed only at the 10% level.

12 The thesis in three numbers · implications

None of the three explanations holds as a rule for every country

Prices

6 of 89country × index combinations followed the price rule

Productivity

13 of 30country × measure tests confirmed the Balassa-Samuelson effect

Competitiveness

8 of 20countries where the exchange rate moderated some pillar (none with all together)

Governments

The author argues for competitiveness and growth policies that take the exchange rate's role into account, with macroeconomic stability and industrial policy so that a stable, competitive exchange rate helps develop and diversify the economy.

Exporting firms

Ready-made “fair exchange rate” formulas mislead: neither prices nor productivity explained the exchange rate in most countries. · our reading

Those who compare countries

The author notes that comparing baskets of goods across countries is hard, with different currencies and consumption habits, which makes comparisons of prices and living standards doubtful.

Researchers

The thesis suggests counting services as goods traded between countries, using the new World KLEMS sector classification, looking at each of the index's 342 criteria and studying how different exchange rate policies act on competitiveness and productivity.

13 Limits · credits

What this research does not say

  • Limited series and countries. The author notes that the price rule is about the long run and that longer series and more countries were missing; essay 2 used 10 pairs, and the new KLEMS sector classification still had a short series.
  • Incomplete index. The competitiveness index database did not yet include all 342 criteria, which leaves gaps in the measure.
  • Where, not how much. Essay 3 only shows whether the effect is significant, without size or sign; we cannot say whether the exchange rate helped or hurt. · our note
  • Loose threshold. Some of the hits in essays 2 and 3 passed only at the 10% level. With many tests per country, some hits may be chance. · our note
  • Our counts. The “6 of 89” in essay 1 comes from the tests in Table 1 (the text says 4 of 100); the “8 of 20” in essay 3, from the list in the conclusion (the abstract says half). · our note

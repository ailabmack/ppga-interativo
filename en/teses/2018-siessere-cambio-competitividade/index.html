Do exchange rates, wages and national wealth really move together?

Three essays test three textbook ideas: prices in Brazil and the US meet again over time (1948 to 2016); the exchange rate moves Brazilian wages (1999 to 2015); and “intellectual capital” makes European countries grow (2007 to 2016). In 12 screens.

02 The starting ideas · section 1.2

Not everything a country produces crosses the border

The law of one price says the same basket of goods, converted into the same currency, should cost the same in two countries; the exchange rate would adjust to make it so (purchasing power parity). Balassa and Samuelson pointed out a hole: part of what we buy does not travel. Tap the items and separate what can be sold across countries from what stays where it is made.

Our examples The thesis defines tradables as manufactured goods and raw materials and non-tradables as labor-intensive services (section 1.2.4); the items above are our illustrations of the two categories.

Travels (tradable)

Factories and farms gain productivity fast with technology, and the price tends to equalize on the world market.

Does not travel

Services gain productivity slowly. In a rich country, high factory wages pull up the wages (and prices) of these services.

This is the Balassa-Samuelson effect: richer countries tend to have higher prices because they are relatively more productive in what is sold across countries (section 1.2.4). The first essay tests whether, between Brazil and the US, the income gap and the price gap move together in the long run.

04 Essay 1 · Charts 1.1 and 1.2

The income gap shrank until 1980 and then stalled

In 1949, output per person in the US was about six times Brazil’s; by around 1980, about three and a half times, and from then on it stayed between three and a half and four. The essay compares this gap with the real exchange rate, which says how far one country’s prices, converted into the other’s currency, drift from the other country’s prices. 05 Essay 1 · Tables 1.2, 1.4 and 1.5

The long-run link only shows up when the model remembers three years

The Johansen test asks whether two wandering series are held together by an invisible “rubber band.” The answer depends on how many past years the model takes into account. The statistical criteria disagreed: three asked for three years, two asked for one. Pick the memory.

Trace statistic for “no link” (rank 0) and the 5% critical value (15.41), Table 1.5. With three years, the next test (at most one link) stays below the threshold (3.12 vs 3.76): there is exactly one link. The thesis also tested predictions from one series to the other (Granger, Table 1.2) with 2, 4 and 5 years: none passed 5% (smallest p = 0.12).

06 Essay 1 · error-correction model (Appendix A)

When the relative price drifts from equilibrium, about 40% of the gap closes the next year

With three years of memory, the thesis estimated an error-correction model. The relative price’s adjustment coefficient was −0.41 and highly significant. In plain words: each year, the relative price gives back about 41% of whatever is off the equilibrium line. Choose the size of the initial push and watch the rubber band pull back.

07 Essay 2 · Table 2.1 and section 2.2

After 1999, trade with the world weighed more in Brazil’s economy

Exports plus imports were 16% of GDP in 1998 and reached 30% in 2004. With a floating exchange rate from 1999 on, the thesis asks whether exchange rates and wages began to move together. Textbooks disagree on who pulls whom. Before the result, make your guess.

The three models in section 2.2: in Canitrot and Rozenwurgel (1986) and in Carlin and Soskice (1990), the exchange rate pulls the real wage; in Rocha (1997), the wage pulls the exchange rate.

08 Essay 2 · Tables 2.5 and 2.8

Exchange rates and wages were not tied to each other

In the rubber-band test, the statistic was 6.48, far below the 15.41 threshold: no long-run link between the real exchange rate and industrial wages from 1999 to 2015. The thesis also tested whether one’s past helps predict the other, with 2 to 5 quarters of memory. Switch the direction and see where each test lands.

Granger causality (Table 2.5 and Appendix B): the further left, the stronger the clue that one series’ past helps predict the other. “Helping predict” is not the same as causing (our note). Johansen with 2 quarters (what four of the five criteria asked for): 6.48; with 3: 6.59; threshold 15.41.

09 Essay 3 · Tables 3.1 and 3.2 and Figure 3.3

A country’s wealth also rests on what you cannot touch

The third essay uses the idea of nations’ intellectual capital: people and what they know, knowledge stored in patents, and relations with the rest of the world. It tests whether each one went along with GDP-per-person growth in 21 European Union countries, 2007 to 2016, with the austerity years (2011 and 2012) set apart. Tap each pillar.

2007–2016 means from the Stata output (Appendix C). The thesis does not list which 21 countries. “Innovation and sophistication” (World Economic Forum score, mean 4.72, from 3.36 to 5.86) comes in later, as a possible booster of each pillar.

10 Essay 3 · Tables 3.7 to 3.10 and 3.12

Foreign investment went along with growth in every estimate; patents, in none

The thesis ran the same calculation four ways. Relational capital (foreign direct investment) passed in all four; structural capital (patents) in none; human capital depends on the method. Austerity came with about 1 percentage point less growth per person in three of the four. Switch the method.

t statistic of each coefficient (Appendix C, same as Tables 3.7 to 3.10): the bar crosses the dashed lines when it is significant at 10%, 5% and 1%. In the innovation-and-sophistication model, each pillar is replaced by the product pillar × innovation score, one at a time (Tables 3.8 to 3.10).

12 In practice · limits · credits

Textbook links show up weaker and more conditional in the data

Anyone comparing prices across countries

Between Brazil and the US, parity does not hold year to year. Our reading: the gap tends to close gradually (about 40% a year in the model), and the result changes with the memory chosen.

Economic policy in Brazil

From 1999 to 2015, the real exchange rate and industrial wages were not tied. Our reading: moving the exchange rate did not bring, in these data, a stable effect on wages.

European governments

Attracting foreign investment went along with more growth per person, and the austerity years with about 1 point less. For the author, as countries develop, intellectual capital matters more and more.

Researchers

Next steps suggested by the author: quarterly or monthly data, other countries, tradable and non-tradable sectors separately, employment and productivity, other competitiveness factors and European regions.

What this research does not say

  • Relation, not cause. Granger tests say whether one series’ past helps predict the other; the clue that wages lead the exchange rate only shows up with 5 quarters (p = 0.06 in the F test) and vanishes with 2, 3 or 4. · our note
  • Results sensitive to memory. In the first essay, the long-run link shows up with three years and not with one or two; the model has non-normal residuals and autocorrelation. · according to the author
  • Annual data and spliced indices. The plan was to use quarterly data, but there were no reliable series; the Brazilian price index joins the IGPC (until 1979) and the INPC. · according to the author
  • The innovation boost is not isolated. In the innovation-and-sophistication models, the product replaces the pillar, without the pillar and the innovation score alongside; the test does not separate the pillar’s effect from the combination’s. The dynamic panel has instruments rejected by the Sargan test. · our note and Table 3.12
  • 21 countries, 10 years. The plan was a larger group and a longer period; the lack of reliable data narrowed the sample, and the thesis does not say which countries were included. · according to the author

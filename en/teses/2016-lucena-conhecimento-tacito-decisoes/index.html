Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

What managers know but cannot explain when they decide

Switching industries, replacing a factory’s machines, opening a different kind of school: these decisions come with no manual. Ten executives described how they made one, and the thesis looked at every step for the knowledge that comes from experience, perception and intuition. It showed up in all of them, even after the choice, when the decision was evaluated. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The kind of decision · section 2.1.1

Some decisions come with no manual

Working out the cheapest way to ship something has rules and clear steps, and can be repeated. Changing a company’s direction cannot. Mintzberg and colleagues (1976) call this second kind an unstructured decision process: there is no identical case before it and no ready-made list of answers.

03 The concept · section 2.3, Table 8

There is knowledge people use but cannot put into words

This is tacit knowledge: it comes from experience and study, is tied to practice and is hard to explain, even with drawings or metaphors. Decision-makers say things like “really by feeling”, “I knew it had to be done”.

The author gathered what different scholars describe and built a list of 40 forms in which this knowledge shows up (our count, Table 13), in three layers. Click a layer.

04 Who spoke · Tables 1 and 2, Table 12

Ten executives, each with a hard decision to tell

In 2015 the author interviewed ten managers from different sectors, each with at least five years of strategic decisions. Each chose one memorable decision. Before analysing, the author checked that all ten had the three signs of a decision with no manual: novelty, complexity and openness. All of them did.

05 The method · sections 3.4 to 3.6 and 4.3

Reading what people said for signs of tacit knowledge

The interviews were recorded and transcribed. The author sorted each passage by decision step and tagged it with a three-letter code for the form of tacit knowledge he saw in it (helped by the NVivo software). He then drew a mind map of each decision and counted the marks. Three of the ten interviewees checked the analysis.

10 interviews→passages by step→3-letter codes→10 mind maps→221 marks

The author acknowledges that telling similar forms apart (intuition, perception and sensitivity, for example) involves some subjectivity; that is why he wrote a definition of each one (Table 9).

07 Results · Tables 3 to 10

Each step drew on a different kind of knowledge

To notice that something needs deciding, perception counted most. To design the solution, values. To look for options, conversations with trusted people. To make the final call, sharing responsibility with others. Pick a step.

08 Results · Table 10

Perception, study and experience were the forms used most

Of the 40 forms on the list, 21 showed up in the interviews, for a total of 221 marks. For the author, the first six (in red) are the core of tacit knowledge in decisions with no manual. Click a form to see the steps where it showed up.

09 The finding · Table 9 and conclusions

The classic model stops at the choice, but tacit knowledge carries on afterwards

The 1976 model only hints at reviewing the decision through its looping arrows. In the interviews, however, everyone described how they judged whether the decision had worked, and the same tacit knowledge showed up in that judgment: 28 marks, led by perception (7) and study and analysis honed by experience (5). That is why the author proposes adding evaluation as an explicit step.

10 The cases · Tables 3 to 9, Figures 10 to 19

None of the ten decisions went through a step without tacit knowledge

This was the thesis’s proposition: tacit knowledge enters every step. The grid confirms it case by case: no cell is empty (our count). Click a row to read excerpts from that decision.

More marks do not mean a better or worse decision: it depends on how much each person told and how the quotes were read (our note).

11 The unexpected · Table 11

Retail, industry and services showed differences, but with few cases

Comparing the average per organisation, three differences caught the author’s eye: in retail, more study and analysis and less mention of experience; in services, more values. But there are only 3, 2 and 5 organisations per group, and the author himself says no conclusion can be drawn: the differences call for further studies. Pick a form.

12 So what?

Deciding well without a manual is learned through study and practice

For the author, tacit knowledge cannot be left out of strategic decisions. Because it forms through practice (“learning by doing”), developing it requires formal study and experience together.

For those who train managers

  • Develop the skills that showed up most: perception, study and analysis with experience, intuition, leading people and talking with trusted people. (author)
  • Combine classroom and practice, because this knowledge forms by doing. (author)

For decision-makers

  • After deciding, set aside time to check whether it worked; in the ten stories, this step also drew on experience and perception. (our reading of the thesis)
  • When looking for options, talking with consultants, peers and trusted people was the resource cited most. (Table 6)

For researchers

  • Measure with a quantitative study how much tacit knowledge weighs in these decisions. (author)
  • Study tacit knowledge in strategies that emerge along the way, the influence of social context and what the brain does at those moments. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Ten cases, no statistics. The study does not generalise to all decisions with no manual; it helps understand, not measure. · according to the author
  • Blurred boundaries. Intuition, perception and sensitivity look alike; reading each quote involves some subjectivity. · according to the author
  • Only what was said. Much tacit knowledge never surfaces in words; the 221 marks are a minimum, not a total. · according to the author
  • The decision-makers’ memory. The stories were told afterwards by the people who made the decisions; there was no observation at the time nor deeper psychological study. · author and our note

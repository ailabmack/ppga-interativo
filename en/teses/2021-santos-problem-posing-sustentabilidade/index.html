Asking what the problem is before solving it

In business courses, students usually get a ready-made problem and chase the solution. The thesis tested another route with 173 students: asking them first to question the problem itself (problem-posing). With that guide, every group reached the deepest kind of reflection. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem

Business schools mostly teach students to solve ready-made problems

Business textbooks and teaching cases usually hand over a defined problem and ask for the best solution: that is problem-solving. For sustainability, the thesis argues, that is not enough. It requires rethinking beliefs and the very way business is done, and that starts by asking whether the problem really is the one given.

Problem-posing comes from Paulo Freire, Donald Schön and Jack Mezirow. See the difference with the same order from a boss:

The order

In two weeks, I want to know what to do to make our factory an environmentally sustainable business.

03 The teaching case

Every group read the story of a garment maker that wanted to become sustainable in two weeks

Beleza Tropical is a lingerie factory in the intimate-apparel cluster of Nova Friburgo, Rio de Janeiro state. Travelling in Denmark, the owner calls and asks the manager for a plan in two weeks. The manager starts out thinking it is simple and keeps finding new layers. Stack up the discoveries:

The case “Being sustainable to sustain itself? The case of Beleza Tropical” (Almeida, 2011) was chosen from 22 Brazilian teaching cases on sustainability (Scielo, Spell, EnANPAD, ESPM and GV Casos). It was chosen on purpose because it already had questions that lean towards problem-posing. That way, if the new guide made a difference, it would do so even with a strong case.

07 Result · the first two steps

Both guides led every group to reflect on content and process

All 44 groups discussed what the problem was and proposed how to solve it: fabric waste, employee engagement, new products. No group failed to reflect. In the details, those given the solving guide looked a little more at the “how” and at what they already knew.

08 The finding · the premise step

All 23 problem-posing groups questioned the premises of the problem, against 16 of 21 problem-solving groups

Questioning the premise means asking what lies behind the problem: the beliefs, who gains, whether it really is the problem. Climb the steps and see how many groups reach each one:

At the premise step: 100% against 76%, a gap of 24 points (“almost a quarter more”, in the thesis's words). Tables 5, 7 and 9. The five guide B groups that did not get there come from all three classes.

10 What they said · in the groups' own words

Students reframed the problem in three different ways

The author sorted the premise reflections into three axes: emancipatory (looks at society, power and one's own role in the world), metaphors and assumptions (links the case to one's own story and changes the way of seeing) and rational processes and means (redefines the problem to solve it better). 11 So what?

To teach sustainability, schools need to make time for framing problems

Business schools

Bring problem-posing into the curriculum, the goals, the content and the grading criteria, as a school culture rather than one teacher's isolated effort.

Teachers

Swap the class with an expected answer for dialogue: flipped classroom, reflective journals and rounds just for raising problems, starting from an article, a news story or a student's company.

Students

Hold back the rush to solve and first question one's own assumptions, in dialogue with classmates.

Teaching materials

Teaching cases that point out fewer problems, letting students find and define the problem and question the tools in the textbooks themselves.

12 To take away

The thesis ends with more questions than answers

On purpose. For the author, more important than any solution is framing, or reframing, a real problem that matters to the individual and to the community. With a guide built for that, all 23 groups that used it reached the step where the problem itself is questioned.

What this research does not say

  • What would happen without grades. Students knew the activities were graded, which may have drawn out more elaborate reflection. · according to the author
  • Whether it holds in other schools. A single institution, final-year students and one teaching case in all three classes. · according to the author
  • What each student thought. Everything was analysed by group: one member reflecting on premises was enough for the whole group to count. · according to the author
  • Whether the change lasts. Reflections were observed in two classes; the author suggests following students over time and taking the test into the workplace. · according to the author
  • A count, not a statistical measure. There are 23 and 21 groups coded by two readers; the thesis has no statistical test of the difference. · our note

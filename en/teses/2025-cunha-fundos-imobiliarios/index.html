What makes a real estate fund perform well?

With a real estate fund, anyone can buy a slice of office towers, warehouses and shopping malls on the stock exchange. The thesis measured the efficiency of 102 of these funds from 2012 to 2023 (how much return they deliver for the risk they take) and tested a popular rule: picking funds by their dividend yield. What mattered most was the fund itself, not the economy. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The starting point · chapter 1

A real estate fund splits properties into units traded on the exchange

The fund pools the money of many unit holders, and a manager invests it in properties or in securities tied to property. Unit holders earn in two ways: from the income paid out and from the rise in the unit price.

1Units cannot be redeemed from the fund: whoever wants out sells on B3, the Brazilian exchange, to another investor.
2By law, the fund must pay out at least 95% of its earnings every six months; in practice, most pay every month.
3For individuals, the income is exempt from income tax, under some conditions.

04 Article 2 · method · sections 3.4 and 3.5

An efficient fund earns more for the same risk

The thesis used Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA): the best funds in each year draw a frontier, and each fund gets a score from 0 to 1 depending on its distance to it. Risk enters in two ways: the total swing of the unit price and the swing that follows the market (the basis of the Sharpe and Treynor ratios).

05 Result · Tables 3.2 and 3.6

The average score stayed near 0.46 and only fell clearly in 2019

From one year to the next, the average efficiency of the 102 funds barely changed. The exception was 2019: the average fell to 0.406 and scores became far more spread out. In 2020, the year the Selic policy rate hit its low of 2%, came the best average of the series.

After adjusting the risk measures (the thesis calls it “input-oriented”), the average rose a little in every year, and the statistical test (paired t) found a significant difference in each one.

09 Article 3 · method · sections 4.3 and 4.5

The rule tested: buy the funds whose dividend yield rose most

The dividend yield (DY) is what the fund paid in 12 months divided by the unit price. The idea of momentum says that whoever is doing well keeps doing well for a while. The author ranked funds by the change in DY and built portfolios with those at the top (“winners”) and those at the bottom (“losers”).

In brick funds, the “losers” earned more than the “winners”

If momentum held, the winner portfolios should earn more. In brick funds, the opposite happened in every window: the losers had a positive and growing average return (up to 1.47% in the 12-month window) and the winners, a negative one.

11 The test · Table 4.4

None of the 45 strategies passed the statistical test

Three strategies, three fund types and five windows give 45 combinations. The author tested whether each one’s return beat IFIX. In none was the difference large enough to rule out chance: the smallest p-value was 0.325, far from the usual 0.05.

The most promising was buying paper winners with a 12-month window (+1.2% on average), which the author sees as a “trend” without significance. For her, splitting funds by type may have diluted the effect, and the recent market may be pricing DY better.

12 In practice · limits · credits

Look at the fund and its property type before the dividend

For managers

Efficiency depended more on internal factors (the asset type and risk management) than on interest rates and inflation. Heavily traded funds had lower scores; the author suggests that a stable base of unit holders favors long-term strategies.

For investors

Compare funds by risk-adjusted return and within the same property type. The change in dividend yield, on its own, did not guarantee gains in the period studied. This page is not investment advice.

For researchers

Study how different forms of management affect efficiency, include property characteristics (location, size, profile of the region) and use longer series that cover full cycles.

What this research does not say

  • Association, not cause. That more heavily traded funds have lower scores does not prove that trading lowers efficiency. · our observation
  • Only the past. Historical data do not foresee crises or extraordinary events; management and long-term strategy are hard to measure. · according to the author
  • The properties were left out. Location, size and the profile of the region were not part of the score. · according to the author
  • Missing data filled with averages. Replacing missing values with the fund’s average smooths the series and may hide swings. · according to the author
  • Numbers to check. The text places the drop in efficiency in 2016, but the tables show it in 2019; we follow the tables. In article 3, the thesis does not say whether portfolios use the 10%, 20% or 30% cut of funds. · our observation

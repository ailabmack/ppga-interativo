Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

Soybean farmers in Mato Grosso learned their trade in three stages, almost all of it outside the classroom

The thesis listened to 10 farmers and 12 staff members of two cooperatives in Sorriso, Brazil’s “agribusiness capital”. They learned first from family and the savanna, then from the market and crises, and finally by swapping experience in the cooperative. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Adriana dos Santos Caparróz Carvalho Advisor: Prof. Dr. Diógenes de Souza Bido Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

03 The place · Sorriso, Mato Grosso

Almost all of them arrived to clear the savanna before the town existed

Sorriso was born in the late 1970s, when the government encouraged settlement of the Legal Amazon, and became a municipality in 1986. Settlers from Paraná, Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul came first. In 2012 a federal law named it the National Agribusiness Capital.

07 Stage 1 · early career · Table 9

At first, the classroom was the family and the savanna itself

What worked in the South did not work in Mato Grosso’s sandy soil and short rain “windows”. The farmers learned by doing, by copying their parents and by going to talks and “field days”. One of them described how the crop changed; step through it.

08 Stage 2 · mid-career · Tables 10 and 11

In mid-career, the challenge moved from the field to the market and the office

With machines and technicians handling the planting, the farmers began learning about what lies outside the farm gate (prices, exchange rates, government plans) and inside it (costs, succession, people). Economic plans and years of low prices became the big lessons. Click a side of the gate.

09 Stage 3 · after joining the cooperative · Table 12

In the cooperative, people who already knew how to farm learned from peers how to negotiate and manage

The cooperatives offer no technical assistance. They offer a place farmers visit almost daily (committees, suppliers’ room, “chat room”) where, for the author, swapping experience lies behind almost everything learned. Click an item.

12 So what? · limits and credits

For the author, a cooperative that wants to teach should organise the conversations already happening

For cooperatives

The meeting spaces already exist; what is missing is to organise them so that what the group builds reaches every member, not just those who visit the head office. (author)

For those who teach management

Farmers with little schooling showed management skills built through practice, which questions the idea that such knowledge is learned only through formal curricula. (author)

For newcomers today

The three stages are tied to the migration context. Newcomers today find expensive land and highly mechanised farming, and will learn along a different path. (author; the last sentence is our reading)

What this research does not say

  • It does not apply to every farmer. Two cooperatives in one town and ten farmers; the value lies in knowing the case in depth. · according to the author
  • It is memory told today. The stages are rebuilt years later by those who lived them, and by those who “made it”: those who went back South were not interviewed. · author and our note
  • Only one cooperative validated the results. They were discussed with Cooalfa’s president; Coobeta did not reply. · according to the author
  • No women deciding. There are women among the members, but none was mentioned as the family’s decision-maker; the author suggests studying this. · according to the author

What do managers have to unlearn when their company changes hands?

After a merger or acquisition, the way of working changes overnight. The study listened to 20 managers who went through it and found that 13 deliberately stopped doing something they knew how to do. For most of those who spoke about it, unlearning meant putting it in a drawer, not erasing it. Explained in 13 screens.

02 The problem

The company changes hands, and so does the right way to work

Mergers and acquisitions bring new rules, bosses and targets. Many studies say that, to learn the new, people need to unlearn the old. But little is known about how this happens in the minds of those who lived through the change, and authors do not even agree on what the word means: discard, abandon, forget?

“To me, unlearning is the process by which a person, because of a given situation, gives up or abandons old learning, such as knowledge, ways of thinking and behaviours. Does this idea […] make sense to you?”The question the researcher asked each manager (section 3.3, translated by us). Not everyone agreed, as screen 08 shows.

03 How the study listened

Twenty managers told their own story, from 1997 to 2014

Instead of a questionnaire, an interview that asks for stories: “tell me what it was like to live through the merger”, “did you have to unlearn anything?”, “what helped?”. Each manager spoke once, for 40 to 70 minutes, in person or on Skype. The researcher retold each story and sent it back for checking: 6 replied, 5 approved the whole text and 1 asked for changes in two passages.

04 What it found

The same merger took managers down four paths

Thirteen said they had to stop doing something the old way. Seven did not: three felt there was no need, three refused, and one says the word makes no sense, that what they went through was relearning. What separates the paths is how each person judged the change and what they decided to do about it.

Sections 4.2.1 and 4.2.4, Figures 3, 4 and 5. The path of the one who “relearned” has no figure of its own in the thesis; that line shows only what it says (section 4.2.1.1).

    05 The 13 who unlearned

    Unlearning was, above all, a way to adapt and keep one's job

    The most common reason was “acting in line with the changes under way”. The second was keeping one's job or avoiding being fired. Learning new things and career growth came up far less. Many unlearned even when they thought the change was bad.

    “a matter of survival in a process in which change was imperative”G16, quoted in section 4.2.1 · translated by us

    06 The content

    The deeper the habit, the fewer people unlearned it

    What the managers stopped doing fits into three layers. On the surface, work techniques and methods. In the middle, attitudes and habitual ways of acting. At the bottom, beliefs about how an organization should be run. For the author, each layer tends to be shaken by one kind of change: in processes, in management model and culture, or in the company's very “way of being”.

    Charts 16, 17 and 18. Width of each layer = number of managers (8, 7 and 3); two managers appear in two layers. The link between layer and kind of change is in the final remarks.

    07 Enablers and obstacles

    The company helps with information and training; what weighs most against is feeling

    On the person's side, it helped to understand what the new context required and to see the new way as better. On the company's side, it helped to explain what was changing, to change gradually and to provide training. The obstacle mentioned most was emotional: frustration, powerlessness, insecurity, loss of power. It appeared in 7 of the 13, including people who agreed with the change.

    Charts 20 and 21: number of managers, out of 13, who mentioned each factor. Blue = personal factor; red = organizational factor. In Chart 20, a typo in one name was read as G03.

    08 In their words

    Not even those who unlearned agree on what unlearning is

    Asked, the managers gave six different answers. The most common links unlearning to learning something new in its place. Others say stopping is enough. Four warn that one can unlearn “for the worse”, swapping the right way for a shortcut. One says the word makes no sense.

    Chart 15. Two managers appear under two notions; five did not say what they understand by unlearning.

    09 What remains of the old

    What is unlearned is not erased: it goes into a drawer

    Eight of the 13 talked about what happened to what they stopped using. Most say the knowledge is still there, ready for another company or situation. For three, the new changed the old way of thinking. And two noticed a cost: what stays stored stops being updated and can grow old.

    10 The finding

    Unlearning is stopping on purpose in order to adapt

    The behaviour by which a person deliberately interrupts the practice of prior learning, in order to adapt to a situation of change, which may or may not require acquiring new learning.The definition the thesis proposes (final remarks, translated by us), valid for the managers heard in M&A situations.

      11 The link to learning

      Unlearning makes room; learning the new makes the change stick

      For the thesis, these are two different processes that feed each other. Stopping the old way leaves the person ready to change; learning the new gives new reference points and holds the change in place. In 10 of the 13 cases, what was learned afterwards was about the same subject as what was unlearned. But there is not always something new to learn: sometimes the task simply left the manager's hands.

      “I learned that to be a manager, there are moments when […] there are times when you have to issue a warning to the employee, because you already talked to them and they didn't comply.”G07, on setting aside excessive leniency with the team (section 4.2.3) · translated by us

      12 The unexpected

      The three who refused to unlearn left the company within about a year

      All three saw the change as a loss: of culture, of technical standards, of values. All three had a way out (retirement or another job offer) and all three resisted letting go of a way they thought was right. Not unlearning, the thesis shows, also blocked learning: the new way was never even tried.

      “[…] at that moment, it was the area where I played music without needing to look at the score. By changing my area, they were taking me out of my comfort zone, forcing me into this unlearning […]”G15 (section 4.2.1.1) · translated by us

      13 So what? Limits and credits

      Organizational change needs room for unlearning, not just learning

      What the study suggests

      • For those running mergers: explain what is changing and why, change gradually and train people in the new way; this is what managers cited as help.
      • For HR and leadership programmes: include unlearning in training, leadership development and coaching, not just teaching the new.
      • For those going through change: what you stop using does not disappear; but, kept in storage, it can become outdated.

      The first two items come from the final remarks and Chart 20; the third is our reading of Chart 22.

      What this study does not say

      • Stories, not measurements. These are recollections, which may omit or distort facts. · according to the author
      • Those who came through HR. Seven managers were referred by their company, which would receive a short report; they may have avoided criticism, and some used the interview to air complaints. · according to the author
      • Only managers, only M&A. The definition holds for these 20 and for this kind of change; it does not say how many managers, in general, unlearn. · our observation
      • One conversation per person. The mergers had taken place from just over 1 to almost 20 years before the interview, with no follow-up over time. · our observation, from Chart 3

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2023

The country changes how people answer the same engagement survey

One of the world’s largest glass packaging makers asks its employees the same 12 questions in many countries. The thesis compared 3,226 answers from 18 countries and found differences on every question, which follow traits of each place’s culture, its “informality rules”. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The concept

Not every rule in a society is written down

Institutional theory sees three layers of rules. On top, laws and regulations that anyone can read. Below, norms and traditions nobody wrote down but everyone follows. At the bottom, what is taken for granted and hardest to change. The thesis calls the two lower layers informality rules. Tap each layer.

Informality rulesno database exists →Country culture (GLOBE project)

Since no database of informality rules exists, the thesis uses each country’s culture as a proxy: the scores of the GLOBE project, an international study that measures nine cultural traits in dozens of countries. Section 2.2 and Figure 1 (Scott’s pillars).

03 The engagement survey · Appendix A

Every year, the same 12 statements reach every site, in any country

The company uses Gallup’s Q12 questionnaire, widely used around the world to measure engagement. Each person says how much they agree with each statement, from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). There is also an overall satisfaction question (Q00). Answer as if it were about your own job.

According to the thesis, the survey is the same in every country and does not consider local differences; results come out by site, but the analysis tends to treat them as a whole. Your answers are not stored.

05 A method safeguard · section 3.5

Before comparing, the thesis discounts how high each country tends to answer

In some places people mark 4 and 5 for almost everything; in others they are more reserved. If that were all, countries would just be “more optimistic” or “more critical”. So, besides the original scores, the author subtracts the country’s overall average from each answer and looks at what is left: which questions sit above or below that place’s way of answering.

Illustration with made-up numbers. Two imaginary countries and three questions. In the thesis, the tests run in both versions: the “direct” one and the “modified” one, with the average removed.

06 Hypothesis 1 · Tables 5 and 14

On all 12 questions, the countries answered differently

An analysis of variance (ANOVA) compares the averages of the 18 countries on each question. On every one, the difference is too large to be chance (p < 0.001), both with the original scores and with each country’s average removed. In other words, it is not just one country being more optimistic than another.

08 Hypothesis 2 · Table 9 · 18 countries

Engagement follows how society acts, not how it says it should act

GLOBE measures each cultural trait in two ways: practices (“how things are”) and values (“how things should be”). The thesis matched the 18 countries’ engagement averages with each trait. The rule: a trait only counts if it links strongly to at least 3 of the 12 questions (dashed line). Tap a column.

“Strongly” = p < 0.05 on each question. Two practice traits pass: assertiveness (11 questions) and institutional collectivism (3). No value trait passes. With only 18 countries, the thesis accepts 90% and 80% confidence as medium and weak hints, shown in the panel.

09 Hypotheses 3 and 4 · Tables 10 and 11

More assertiveness goes with less engagement; more collectivism, with more

Assertiveness measures how tough and confrontational people in a society are in their relationships. Institutional collectivism measures how much institutions encourage collective action and reward. Each bar shows how a trait links to one question (t statistic): to the left, reverse; to the right, direct.

10 Why “3 or more” · section 4.2

With 12 questions, one hit by luck is easy; three at once is rare

Each test at 95% confidence has a 5% chance of flagging a link that does not exist. With 12 questions per cultural trait, that happens often. Draw a made-up trait, with no real link at all, and count the false “hits”.

Our reading
That is why the thesis requires 3 or more strong links: by chance, this happens for about 2.1% of traits (with 2, it would be 12.0%). Assertiveness, with 11 of 12, is far beyond chance. Institutional collectivism passed with exactly 3, the minimum on the ruler.
11 In practice, limits and credits

What this research does not say

For people managers in global companies

For the author, one program applied in every country, without looking at the local context, loses information: each place’s informality rules need to enter the decision.

For those who read the engagement survey

Comparing one country’s score with another’s mixes the way people answer with engagement. Removing each country’s average helps show what really differs (screens 5 to 7).

For researchers

The author suggests repeating the study in other companies and sectors, with more country variables and follow-up over the years. Entrenchment, in the title, is left for another study.

  • One company and one year. Only the 2022 survey of one glass maker; the Q12 survey has no academic validation, and there were no data on age, gender or tenure. · according to the author
  • Data processed by the company. The data came without identifying people and without a single “1” answer. The step that removed repeated rows cut 81% of the rows (our count); without an identifier, some of them may be different people who answered the same way. · our observation
  • 18 points for 12 questions. On screens 8 and 9, each country is one point, and the 12 questions enter the same model together: little room is left to estimate. France accounts for 41% of the answers. · our observation
  • A link is not a cause. The thesis speaks of the “impact” of informality rules; the design measures association between country averages. Answers with the average removed show which questions sit above or below the others in the same country; reading this as “lower engagement” is the author’s. · our observation
  • Culture as a proxy. GLOBE scores treat the country as the unit and were collected earlier; criticism of Hofstede and GLOBE is discussed in the thesis. · according to the author

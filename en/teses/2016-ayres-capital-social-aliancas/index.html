Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

Companies that know their partners well seek and adjust more alliances

In 60 companies of the Brazilian pharmaceutical industry, those with more social capital (contacts, trust and shared goals with partners) saw less risk in their partner and had more routines to look for new partnerships and to change ongoing ones. Distrusting the partner, on its own, did not move these routines. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The problem

Two routines almost no one has

In the pharmaceutical industry, companies team up for research, clinical trials, production and distribution: these are strategic alliances, bound by a contract, a way to shorten the road from research to the medicine on the pharmacy shelf. Managing these partnerships well is a skill made of several routines.

The thesis looks at two of them, which earlier studies describe as rare: proactiveness, looking for partners before the opportunity knocks (instead of repeating those you already know), and transformation, changing the terms of an alliance already signed when the world changes. The question: what leads a company to have these routines?

Introduction and section 2.4 of the thesis (Schilke and Goerzen, 2010; Reuer and Zollo, 2002). The statements come from the questionnaire (Table/Chart 2), answered from 1 to 5.

03 The concepts

Social capital has three faces; partner risk, two

Social capital is what a company gains from its network of relationships: whom it knows, how much it trusts and how much it thinks like its partners (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Relational risk is the fear that the partner will not cooperate or will act in bad faith (Das and Teng, 2001). Tap the blocks or the buttons.

Section 3.2 and Chart 2 of the thesis. Questionnaire statements, abridged, answered from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). One statement of the cognitive face (similar culture) was dropped from the model because it measured too little of what it should.

05 The idea

Four pieces and seven bets

The thesis brings together the sociology of networks (social relationships produce trust) and transaction cost economics (partners may act in their own interest). Seven hypotheses follow. 07 Result · H1

More social capital, less risk seen in the partner

Where the manager says the company has more contacts, trust and shared goals with partners, they also see less chance that the alliance partner will distort facts, break promises or block cooperation.

Too calm? The author does not read this result as good news only.
Contacts and trust make information from the network feel right and give a sense of control. This can lead the manager to overestimate what they know and underestimate the risk. For the author, one must watch out for a fall, or even a loss, in risk perception: an implication she calls contrary to what one would expect.

08 Result · H2 and H3

Social capital goes with seeking partners and, even more, with adjusting agreements

Companies with more social capital say they look for partnerships more than their competitors and take the initiative (proactiveness). And they say they are willing to set contract terms aside and are flexible about changing the ongoing alliance (transformation). The link with transformation is much stronger.

10 Result · H5 and H7

With risk and social capital counted together, the picture changes

To test H5 and H7, the thesis added one more piece to the model: the combination of social capital and risk. That combination passed both tests, and the model came to explain much more. But the weights of the other pieces changed in size and sign. Compare the two models.

How the author reads it

With risk in the air, social capital loses effectiveness: in the presence of risk, the more social capital, the less companies carry out these routines, perhaps because the network sends signals that discourage change.

Our reading

The weight of the combination is positive and above 1, and social capital, in this model, does not pass the test. When the combination is built from the original values, without centring, the weights of each piece can no longer be read on their own. What remains is a clue that risk and social capital act together; the direction needs a new test.

Figures 8 and 9 (without combination) and Figures 10 to 13 (with combination) of the thesis, section 6.2.4. Striped bars: t below 1.96. Scale from −1.3 to +1.3.

11 So what?

Looking after the partner network helps find and adjust alliances

For the author, social capital only brings value to a partnership if it is actually managed by the company that has it, like any other capital.

For alliance managers

  • Investing in ties, trust and respect with customers, suppliers and partners helps form partnerships and curb opportunism. (author)
  • Use the network as a source for finding partners and as inspiration for improving ongoing alliances. (author)

To stay alert

  • Be wary of a network that makes everything feel too calm: trust can lower one’s guard about the partner’s risk. (author)

For research

  • Test the model with more companies, in other sectors, by type of alliance and with confirmatory techniques. (author)
  • Our reading: redo the risk × social capital combination with centred values and check the direction of the effect.

What this research does not say

  • Small sample. 60 companies that agreed to take part, in a period of crisis that led many to refuse: the results hold for them, not for other sectors or countries. · according to the author
  • Exploratory. PLS is an exploratory technique; the constructs should later be tested with confirmatory analysis. · according to the author
  • A snapshot. Data from a single moment, without seeing how social capital builds up over time; what we see is what goes together. · according to the author
  • Short measures. Proactiveness and transformation ended up with two questions each, and social capital and risk explain less than half of their own questions (AVE 0.408 and 0.337). · our observation
  • Combinations. In the H5 and H7 models, weights go above 1 and change sign: read them as a clue. · our observation

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

What leads a business teacher to take the class out into the community?

The author listened to 16 teachers who run university outreach projects and read the accounts of 60 of their students. At the root is empathy, learned at home and at school. But for the students, the project was mostly a first contact: only 3 of 60 described a deep change. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Diogo Reatto Advisor: Prof. Dr. Janette Brunstein Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 A children's game

The first lesson in competition can come from musical chairs

Business teachers educate people who will make decisions for whole companies. One of the teachers interviewed (P2) used the party game to explain the concern: from an early age, we learn to take someone else's chair. Play the music and see.

“You put out several chairs and you have to take the other person's chair and, in the end, one is left without. What can you expect from a society that teaches that to children? […] The child who takes another child's chair, do you think they won't clear a forest?”P2 · thesis section 6.1

The thesis starts from there: emotions such as empathy are not a gift, they are learned through living with others. And if they are learned, school and university can teach or unteach them.

Six children, six chairs. It has not started yet.

03 How the research looked

Teachers told their own story; students wrote theirs

In 2019, the author sought undergraduate business teachers who had run outreach for at least two years, focused on social or environmental problems. It was hard: many said no and meetings were postponed for months. With those who agreed, the conversation was a narrative interview, wherever the teacher chose, even under a tree. Then students from those projects answered in writing.

04 The teachers' stories

The urge to help was learned at home, at school and at work

When telling their lives, teachers went back to their families, to memorable teachers and to crises at work. Tap a layer. Below, the three images (metaphors) the author drew from their own words to describe this drive.

The thesis calls what is learned in family and school primary socialisation, and what comes later, at work, secondary. For several teachers it was easier to talk about work, which is more recent, than about childhood.

05 How emotion becomes action

First something bothers you; then you put yourself in the other's place and act

The author read a quote by P12 as a chain. Discomfort is a reflexive emotion (like anguish, shame, guilt); empathy is an emotion of putting yourself in the other's place; acting to help is prosocial behaviour. Step through the quote.

    08 How they engage students

    Almost every strategy stops at showing the problem

      09 What the students wrote

      Only 3 of the 60 students described a deep change

      So as not to call any change a “transformation”, the author used three levels of engagement. Most accounts showed practical learning (“I learned how to dispose of electronics”). Try classifying six real excerpts as he did.

      ExposureTook part and can say what they learned and why it matters.

      Table 12 of the thesis, adapted from Brunstein and King (2018). The three red squares are the students the author placed at transformation. He did not count how many stayed at exposure or interaction; about 10% (6) left the questions about change blank.

      A clue from the author: those at exposure tend to write in the first person singular (“I learned”, “it helped me”); at interaction, “we” appears (“we learned”, “we took part”).

      10 The unexpected

      Students see more change in the community than in themselves

      When the question was about their own change, answers were short (“I'm more aware”). When it was about change in others, they came with enthusiasm. For the author, the focus was solving someone else's problem, not rethinking one's own assumptions. Teachers, on the contrary, find it very hard to reach the community. Tap the mirror or the window.

      “But over time, with the project done and the grade given, we turn our backs on the community again to get on with our lives. I feel we used those people, once again, to reach our own goals.”E38 · one of the 3 transformation accounts
      11 The cost of doing outreach

      Teachers carry on “overloaded, but happy”

      The phrase comes from a student. Teachers described little help from local governments and from their own university, communities that do not keep the project going and a career that rewards papers. And the author notes a deeper point: the problem chosen is the teacher's, almost never built with the community. Tap each barrier.

      Tap a starfish to return it to the sea.

      “Thousands of starfish on the ground […] Then someone walks by and says: ‘what are you doing? Do you think you're going to put all these starfish back in the water?’ And he answers: ‘I'm not, but for this one I made a difference’.”P10 · the parable one of the teachers uses to keep going
      12 So what?

      Outreach should guide teaching, not sit on its margins

      The author calls for a “pedagogy of urgency”. Brazil trains more than 200,000 business graduates a year, and few go through projects like these, which have few places: participants, he says, are a kind of elite.

      For teachers

      • Make room in class for emotional experiences, their own and their students'.
      • Go beyond showing the problem: critical reflection, debate and action, with time for change to happen.
      • Listen to the community to define the problem together with it.

      For universities

      • Institutionalise and support outreach, so it does not depend only on the goodwill of a few.
      • Make outreach the starting point of teaching, not an add-on.
      • Support teacher development and work across disciplines.

      For programs and students

      • Rethink the individualistic, highly specialised teaching of each course, even the most technical.
      • Encourage student organisations as a collective way to change teaching.

      What this research does not say

      • Not a national sample. These are 16 teachers who agreed to tell their story; there are many others. · according to the author
      • A snapshot, not a follow-up. The author suggests long-term studies, with observation of classes and projects. · according to the author
      • Change reported, not measured. The levels come from reading written accounts; someone who writes little may have changed more than they say. · our note
      • The community was not heard. Change in the communities appears through the eyes of teachers and students. · our note

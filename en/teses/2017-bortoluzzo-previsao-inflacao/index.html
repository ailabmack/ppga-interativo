Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2017

For a year of inflation, almost all of 155 models beat the simplest guess

The thesis set 155 models to forecast Brazil’s consumer price index (IPCA) 1 to 12 months ahead, month after month, from 2007 to 2015. Beyond 4 months, the simplest guess fell behind. But a guess that only looks at the calendar was never safely beaten, and the best forecast came from taking the IPCA basket apart into 52 items. Explained in 13 screens.

02 The problem · Introduction

Anyone who signs a long-term contract is betting on inflation that has not happened yet

Wages, mortgages, debts and the Central Bank’s policy all depend on what inflation will do. The thesis recalls that, in studies with US data, almost no economic indicator clearly beats two simple guesses: repeating what inflation has just done, or close to it.

Next month much like this one. That guess, AR(1), is the opponent in the screens that follow.

In Brazil, Carlo and Marçal (2016) had already beaten those guesses by forecasting each piece of the index separately and adding them up. The thesis compares both routes: models with economic data and piece-by-piece forecasts.

03 The setting · Section 3.3

From 1995 to 2015, Brazilian inflation took several jolts

Crises, an election and policy shifts move the level of inflation suddenly. For a forecaster, each jolt is an off-pattern month that can fool the model. That is why some of the thesis’s models flag those unusual months on their own as the data come in.

The data start in January 1995: before the Real Plan, inflation exceeded 1,000% a year and, according to the thesis, earlier figures cannot be used.

04 How the study looked · Sections 3 and 3.4

Each model forecast the next 12 months using only what had already happened

The thesis simulated a forecaster’s routine. With data from January 1995 to December 2006 (144 months), each model forecast cumulative inflation 1 to 12 months ahead. Then one more month entered the data, the model was re-estimated and forecast again. And so on until December 2015: 96 months of forecasts to compare.

The thesis calls this pseudo real time: the data used are the revised ones, not exactly those published in each month.

Three yardsticks to judge who forecast best

Size of the miss (MSE)

The average of squared misses: missing by 2 points weighs four times missing by 1. Smaller is better. On these screens we show its square root, the typical miss in percentage points (our calculation).

Best group (MCS)

It separates the models that cannot be told apart from the best from those clearly behind, with 90% confidence. A model left out really lost, not by bad luck.

Does anyone beat the opponent? (SPA)

It asks whether at least one model beats a reference guess, without being fooled by whoever got lucky among so many trying.

05 The models · Charts 1 to 3

22 data combinations times 7 ways of trimming the model give 154 contenders, plus the simple guess

Each model combines inflation with other series (other price indices, interest rates, output, public debt…) and looks at the last 12 months of all of them. That is a lot of information, so an algorithm, Autometrics, starts from the big model and keeps cutting what does not help. Some ways also flag off-pattern months.

06 Result · Table 2

For next month everyone ties; the gap appears as the horizon grows

One month ahead, the simple guess misses as much as the best (about 0.2 point). At 12 months it misses cumulative inflation by 1.6 points, against 1.3 for the best. The calendar guess keeps pace with the best from start to finish.

08 The unexpected · Tables 5 and 17

No model with economic data safely beat the guess that looks at the calendar

The SPA test asks: is any of the 155 better than this guess? Against the simple guess, the answer is yes from 2 months on. Against the calendar guess (12 months of inflation and the month of the year), only in month 1. Economic data did not add a clear win over inflation’s own memory.

Bar below the 0.10 line: the guess was beaten at that horizon. In the smaller base (40 months), which adds the item-by-item forecasts of another study, the calendar guess is beaten from 1 to 10 months.

09 Sensitivity · Section 4.1, Tables 6 to 11

When forecasting too little inflation costs more, the simple guess rejoins the best group

For a central bank or a firm, forecasting less inflation than actually happens may hurt more than forecasting more. The thesis redid the best group giving 1.5, 2 and 3 times more weight to misses on the low side. The larger the weight, the more months the simple guess joins the group.

Why the simple guess benefits

Our calculation from Tables 2, 7, 9 and 11 (12 months): how much of each guess’s squared miss came from forecasting above and how much from forecasting below the inflation that happened.

The simple guess mostly missed on the high side. Once a high miss counts for less than a low miss, its score improves and it rejoins the best group (our reading; the thesis does not show the sign of the misses).

Red month: the simple guess is in the best group at that horizon (best-group p-value shown in the month, Tables 3, 6, 8 and 10).

10 Robustness · Figure 6

With only two years to judge, the winner changes, and the simple guess even came second

The thesis repeated the 12-month comparison in seven two-year windows. The simple guess was the worst in six of them. In the last one, 2014 and 2015, when inflation rose to 10.67%, everyone missed by a lot and it had the second smallest miss. In five of the seven windows, first place changed hands.

Values read from the chart in Figure 6 (approximate), in MSE × 10⁴. The blue band runs from the smallest to the largest miss of the other 11 models in the figure. The author concludes that a much smaller sample could give completely different results.

11 Disaggregated models · Tables 12 to 17

Forecasting each of the 52 IPCA items and adding them up missed least at every horizon

The thesis added to its forecasts those of another study (Carlo and Marçal, 2016), which forecasts pieces of the index separately. In a shorter 40-month period, SARIMA-52, which forecasts the 52 items one by one, had the smallest miss from 1 to 12 months, and no test could say anyone beat it.

What about averaging all models?

Averaging the forecasts (plain, or weighting those that missed least over the last 12 months) gave a good result, not the best: 30th and 24th of 157 at 12 months, but always inside the best group and never beaten in the SPA test.

12 So what? · Final remarks

Which model is best depends on the horizon, the period and the cost of missing on each side

Inflation forecasters

Economic data help beat the simplest guess from a few months on, but the calendar guess is a more honest opponent. Forecasting the IPCA items separately was the most accurate route. · according to the author

Central bank and market

If underestimating inflation costs more, a very simple model may be among the best. The yardstick used to judge changes the winner. · according to the author

Firms and households

Even the best models missed 12-month inflation by more than 1 point on average. When adjusting contracts or planning debts, it pays to work with a margin, not a single number. · our reading

Researchers

Judging models over a few years misleads: the ranking changes from window to window. The author suggests testing other model types (nonlinear, FAVAR), rolling windows and data as originally published. · according to the author

13 Limits · credits

What the study does not say

  • One family of models. All 155 are of the same type (unrestricted VAR). Nonlinear or factor models may perform differently. · according to the author
  • Revised data. The models used the corrected figures, not those published at the time; the race is not exactly the one an analyst lived through. · according to the author
  • Item forecasts over 40 months. SARIMA-52’s win comes from a shorter period, with less powerful tests. It remains to be seen whether it holds in other windows. · according to the author
  • Untested patterns. The impression that trimming methods do better at short horizons, and full models at long ones, was not tested. · according to the author
  • Period from 2007 to 2015. The author himself shows the winner changes with the period judged; other years may tell another story. · our reading

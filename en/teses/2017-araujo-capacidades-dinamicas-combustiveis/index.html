Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2017

If they all share the same tanks, what makes a fuel distributor operate better?

At the port of Suape, in the Brazilian state of Pernambuco, fuel distributors store fuel in the same terminals and sell to the same filling stations. The thesis interviewed 11 managers and ran 70 questionnaires: do those that sense and react to the market operate better? And does learning new things help? In 11 screens.

02 The setting · Suape, Pernambuco

The fuel is the same; what changes is the routine

Since 1993, unbranded filling stations in Brazil can buy from any distributor. In Suape, distributors rent tanks in the same terminals and sell to the same stations. For the author, what sets them apart is how they run three routines, the unit of analysis of the thesis. Pick one:

Introduction and Sections 4.4 and 4.5. The steps of each routine are those listed by the author. In Suape the terminal stores and loads the fuel and checks its quality; the distributor sells, invoices and organises deliveries.

08 Result · learning new things in four steps

Of the four steps of learning new things, the strongest is the last: putting it to use

Following Zahra and George, a company acquires knowledge, understands it, adapts it to what it already knows and, finally, uses it. In Suape, the score for using was above the other three for every size. Pick a size:

Table 4 (all 70 people) and Tables 5 to 8 (by size). Each step’s height starts at score 1, the scale minimum. For the author, bases live on selling and loading every day, and there is no time to let one step mature before the next.

Tables 12 to 15. Cost, timeliness and flexibility: difference between sizes at 1% in the Kruskal-Wallis test; quality, only at 10%. These are scores each person gave their own base (for example, “we offer a lower freight price than competitors”), not measured costs or delivery times.

11 So what?

Sensing and reacting goes with operating better; new knowledge only counts once it is put to use

For the author, the model helps distributor managers see which routines to improve; the results were presented to the participating companies.

For those running a base

  • Mapping the market, having decision criteria and following regulation went with better operating scores (Table 17).
  • A clear division of tasks and a shared language about routines (the “use” step) was the learning step that counted.

For small distributors

  • They lagged on almost every part, with little autonomy at the base and almost no training (the author’s reading).
  • Training newcomers, instead of leaving each to fend for themselves, is a hint of where to start (our reading of the interviews).

For research

  • Zahra and George’s four steps did not show up together in this sector: the theory may depend on the pace of the business.
  • Compare with other fuel hubs in Brazil and with production, retail and consumers (the author’s suggestions).

What this research does not say

  • Few people, one place. 70 answers from 11 distributors in a single hub; the author asks for more informants. · according to the author
  • Self-reported performance. Cost, quality, timeliness and flexibility are scores given by the people themselves, in the same questionnaire as the other measures; there is no sales or delivery data. · our note
  • Moving together is not cause. Everything was measured at once; the author says he did not design the model to prove cause and effect. · according to the author
  • The use model came afterwards. The third model was built after seeing that only use had a link; the indirect path had no test of its own. · our note

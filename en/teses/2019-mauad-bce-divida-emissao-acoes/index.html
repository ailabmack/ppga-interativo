Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2019

When the European Central Bank started buying corporate debt, the stock market received new share sales worse

In March 2016, the ECB announced it would buy corporate bonds to make credit cheaper. The thesis followed 1,141 share offerings in the euro area from 2012 to 2018: after the announcement, the price fell by 3.4% on average in the five days around the offering, against 1.3% before. 02 The topic

To raise money, a company can borrow or bring in new partners

A listed company that needs money to invest has two doors in the market. It can sell debt securities (bonds): it borrows and pays interest. Or it can sell new shares: buyers become partners and share future profits.

The thesis studies seasoned equity offerings (SEOs): companies already on the stock exchange that sell more shares. Stock market debuts (IPOs) were left out.

03 The context

After the 2008 crisis, the ECB began buying securities, and in 2016 it added corporate ones

To revive the economy, central banks cut interest rates and bought securities on a massive scale (quantitative easing). The ECB went beyond government bonds: with the CSPP, the Corporate Sector Purchase Programme, it bought debt from non-financial companies in the euro area.

04 The question

Cheaper debt should help shares. But investors know the programme will end

The author builds a chain of effects (Figure 4 of the thesis). Push the first piece and see where it leads.

H1After the programme, companies selling new shares meet a more negative reaction than before.
H2The largest and most profitable prefer debt; the smaller and less profitable sell shares, and this gap widens.
H3After the programme, companies issuing debt meet a more positive reaction than before and than those selling shares.

Bet before you look: after the programme was announced, the market received new shares…

05 The method

The yardstick: how much the share moved on the announcement days, beyond what the market did

It is an event study. For each offering, the author estimates how much the share usually rises or falls when the country’s market rises or falls (using the 230 trading days about a year earlier). The gap between what the share did and what was expected, added up over the days around the announcement, is the cumulative abnormal return.

The thesis windows: 5 days (from the 2nd day before to the 2nd after the announcement) and 3 days. Event date: the filing of the offering; for debt, the issue date. 06 Hypothesis 1 · euro area

After the ECB announcement, the average drop around a share offering nearly tripled

Selling new shares already tended to push the price down: investors read the offering as a sign that the share is expensive. After March 2016, the reaction got much worse, in both windows.

07 The 1,141 offerings

The average got worse, but each offering is its own case: many rose

The thesis appendix lists every euro-area offering with its result. Using that list, we counted how many ended below what was expected: 65% before the programme and 67% after. The change is less in the number of drops and more in their size.

08 The unexpected

In the UK nothing changed; in the US, without the programme, the reaction also worsened

To separate the ECB’s effect from a general change in stock markets, the author repeated the calculation in two markets outside the euro, with the same cut-off date. In the UK, the reaction barely moved. In the US, it worsened almost as much as in the euro area; for the author, because the Fed was doing the opposite: ending purchases and raising rates.

09 Hypothesis 3

At the other door, the reaction improved: issuing debt now came with a slight rise

In the 1,296 euro-area debt issues, the share’s reaction went from almost zero before the programme to +0.39% after. With rates at record lows, investors may have read new debt as cheap money for growth. Putting the two doors together, the gap between them widened by 2.6 points.

10 Hypothesis 2

Companies issuing debt are large and profitable, and the gap to those selling shares grew

The author compared the two groups before and after the programme. Debt issuers were already much larger and profitable. After 2016, share sellers became even smaller, raised less and posted bigger losses, as hypothesis 2 predicted.

11 So what?

A programme aimed at debt also moved shares

For the author, investors saw an extra risk in the programme: interest rates were artificially and temporarily low. Those selling shares at that time paid with a bigger drop; those with good ratings took advantage of cheap debt.

For companies

  • Large, profitable companies with good credit ratings benefited most from the programme: cheaper debt and a positive market reaction. (author)
  • Selling shares at a time of artificially low rates was met with a bigger discount. (our reading of screen 06)

For central banks

  • Buying corporate debt does not stay in debt: the effect reaches the stock market. (author)
  • The exit from the programme matters too: investors react to what they expect at the end. (author)

For researchers

  • It is the first study, as far as the author found, on the programme’s effect on share offerings. (author)
  • It remains to separate the programme from other changes of the time: in the US the reaction also worsened (screen 08). (our observation)

What comes next, according to the author

On 19 Dec 2018 the ECB stopped buying new bonds and moved to reinvesting only what matured. In 2019, that would mean €6 billion in corporate bonds, about 3.4% of what it bought in the first phase.

The author’s conjecture: with fewer purchases, funds would return to the bonds of large companies, and rates for smaller ones would rise, closing the window of cheap debt. Not tested in the thesis.

This is not investment advice. The thesis describes the market’s average reaction between 2012 and 2018; each offering had its own result (screen 07), and the past does not guarantee the future.
12 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • Before and after is not cause. The March 2016 cut-off separates two periods; whatever else changed in those years is mixed in. Against the UK, the difference holds only at 10%; against the US, it is not significant. · our observation
  • The explanation was not measured. The idea that investors anticipated the end of the programme and began demanding more return is the author’s reading; the thesis measures the reaction, not the reason. · our observation
  • Only the announcement days. The return is measured over 3 and 5 days; what happened to these shares in the following months is left out. · our observation
  • Little explained per company. The regressions explain 8% to 15% of the variation across offerings (R²); each case varies a lot (screen 07). · our observation
  • A topic still unfolding. The programme’s new phase began in 2019, after data collection, and only 35 companies made both kinds of issue in the period, too few to study separately. · according to the author

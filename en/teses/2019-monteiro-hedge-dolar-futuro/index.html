Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2019

Hedging the dollar with futures cuts about 86% of the swings, whether the math is simple or sophisticated

The thesis compared 49 combinations of models that recompute the hedge every day with the simplest fixed rule, using 17 years of spot and futures dollar prices on B3, the Brazilian exchange. Almost all of them removed the same share of risk. Explained in 10 screens.

Author: Wagner Oliveira Monteiro Advisor: Prof. Dr. Leonardo Fernando Cruz Basso Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The problem · Data

In 17 years, the dollar ranged from about R$ 1.55 to over R$ 4, with many ups and downs

Anyone who will pay or receive dollars does not know what that will cost in reais. The author gathered 4,168 days of spot and futures dollar quotes on B3, from January 2001 to December 2017.

03 The idea · Chapter 2

Futures move in lockstep with today’s dollar, so they work as a counterweight

Someone with dollars to receive can take the opposite position in the futures market: if the dollar falls, they lose on one side and gain on the other. The thesis asks how much futures to use for each exposed dollar, the so-called hedge ratio.

04 Review · Chapter 2

“Optimal” depends on the goal: the thesis reviews nine recipes and tests the lowest-swing one

Some recipes look only at risk; others also weigh return, or only losses. Tap a recipe to see what it aims for.

In blue, the recipe used in the tests: minimum variance, from Johnson (1960) and Ederington (1979). Each model is judged by how much it lowers the variance of the hedged portfolio compared with the unhedged one (section 4, equation 120).

05 Out of sample · Tables 18 to 21

The fixed rule barely moves; dynamic models change the dose every day

The simplest rule (ordinary least squares regression, OLS) uses a single ratio, recomputed with the accumulated data. Models in the GARCH family estimate each day how much the dollar and futures will swing and move together, and the ratio changes with that.

08 The unexpected · Tables 24, 26 and 27

On the test days, the fixed rule topped the table, by a tiny margin

The fixed rule cut 86.64% of the risk; the best dynamic combination, 86.55%. Between the first and the last of the 36 that worked, the gap is 0.65 point. Switch the scale to see that gap vanish when the ruler starts at zero.

On the learning days, the dynamic models were slightly ahead (up to 87.24%, against 87.08% for the fixed rule). On new days, the edge disappeared.

Our reading of the tables: the thesis’s conclusion says only that performance was similar across all models. The method describes a test to tell real differences from luck (the Model Confidence Set), but its results are not reported; so treat the order as a statistical tie.

09 So what? · our reading of the results

To cut dollar risk, most of the work is hedging at all; sophistication changes little

Corporate treasuries

With the dollar between 2012 and 2017, hedging about 96 cents of futures per exposed dollar removed close to 86% of the swings, and the fixed rule did it as well as the models. · our reading

Model builders

Doing well on days already seen does not guarantee doing well on new days. And a model can break silently: one of the equations (FIGARCH with the BBM method) protected little or nothing in every version. · our reading

Researchers

The author suggests more multivariate models, other distributions, regime-switching models, other futures markets and rolling windows to check whether the result holds.

Teachers and students

A good example of why learning days and testing days must be kept apart: the ranking of models changed from one period to the other. · our reading

The thesis has no practical-implications section; the three marked cards are our reading of the numbers. Not investment advice.

10 Limits · credits

A risk comparison, without costs and without a test of differences

What this research does not say

  • Risk, not profit. The measure is how much the swings fell. There are no trading costs, margins, daily settlements or taxes, and less risk does not mean more money. · our note
  • A tie without a test. The test that would separate real differences from luck (MCS) is in the method but not in the results. · our note
  • Tables with repeated rows. The DCC reductions repeat the normal CCC ones, RiskMetrics rows repeat other models and one cDCC ratio table repeats the DCC one; we left those out. The abstract mentions data up to 2018; the data section and the graphs end in December 2017. · our note
  • One market, one day ahead. Only the commercial dollar on B3, with the most traded contract and a hedge for the next day. The author suggests testing other markets and rolling windows. · according to the author

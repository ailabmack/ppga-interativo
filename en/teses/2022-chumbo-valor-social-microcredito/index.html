Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2022

What microcredit is worth to the people who receive it

Social businesses say they create social value, but rarely ask the people on the receiving end. The thesis listened to 30 micro-entrepreneurs in Brazil's Northeast who keep renewing their loans and found two ways of living with that money: to help the business survive or to help it flourish. 02 The problem · 53 articles

Everyone talks about social value, almost no one asks the people who receive it

Creating social value is the whole point of social entrepreneurship. But what is it? The author read 53 articles on the subject, 42 international and 11 Brazilian. Most of them stress how important it is to create social value without saying what it is, and some treat it as a synonym for social impact. Choose what to highlight:

The thesis's proposal. The author turns to an old definition from sociology, by Thomas and Znaniecki (1918): social value is something concrete or abstract that has meaning for a group and leads that group to act toward it. A social business product, such as microcredit, becomes social value through the meaning beneficiaries give it as they use it. Listening to that meaning shows the social value that actually arrives.

The thesis's literature review (Scopus, Spell and Scielo; section 2.3). The thesis counts definitions only among the 42 international articles. One cube per article; the position of each is illustrative.

03 The context · productive, guided microcredit

On paper, microcredit is money for the business, with someone keeping an eye on it

In Brazil, microcredit means productive credit (for an income-generating activity) that is guided (granted after a credit agent visits the business and assesses it). The study worked with the client portfolio of a for-profit social business that lends microcredit, is backed by a philanthropic venture capital fund and operates in four states of the Northeast. 04 The method · 30 interviews in the Northeast

The researcher went to the homes and businesses of 30 clients

08 The finding · outcome space

Flourishing includes surviving: it is not better, it is broader

People who live credit as flourishing also take care of keeping the business going, and go further: they expand and diversify. People who live it as survival described no expansion at all. That is why one circle fits inside the other. The thesis warns that this hierarchy is not a value judgement about the people. Tap the parts of the drawing:

12 Takeaways

Social value is measured in the voice of those who receive it

The thesis proposes looking at social business products as social values: they are worth the meaning beneficiaries give them as they use them. Comparing that meaning with the mission is one way of assessing social impact, and the author suggests other social businesses do the same.

For microcredit providers

  • Visit and assess the business again at renewal, not only for the first loan.
  • Add financial and management guidance to credit, especially for those who live it as survival, and spaces where micro-entrepreneurs can learn from each other.
  • Reconsider lending to those who no longer need it, which may leave out those who do.

For social investors

  • Learn, from the clients' own voices, whether money earmarked for a social need is delivering on its promise.
  • Paying on time shows the debt is honoured, not that credit changed the borrower's life.

For research and evaluation

  • Use the outcome space as a qualitative evaluation of any social business product or service.
  • Repeat the study in other regions and in favelas, to see whether broader ways of living with credit emerge.

What this research does not say

  • How many clients live each way. These are 30 people from one company, in four states of the Northeast; phenomenography looks for the range of experiences, not proportions. Other regions may show other ways. · according to the author and our own note
  • Whether credit lifted anyone out of poverty. The thesis listens to how people live with credit; it does not measure income before and after. · our own note
  • Interviews with the company nearby. Agents picked the clients (many were swapped on the spot), the supervisor and agent were present during the conversations, and clients saw the researcher as an MC1 employee. The author says the answers were spontaneous. · our own note
  • An unusual moment. Fieldwork took place during the pandemic, without a pilot interview; the agent also attributed part of the lack of visits to Covid-19. · according to the author

Why do companies choose to train people with game elements?

Points, levels, rankings and challenges have entered corporate training. The thesis heard 16 HR and technology professionals who build this kind of training in award-winning companies. What weighs most in the choice is interactivity, the chance to measure progress and learners' engagement. Explained in 12 screens.

Author: Siméia de Azevedo Santos Advisor: Prof. Dr. Almir Martins Vieira Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The idea

Gamifying does not mean turning training into a game

Gamification is using game elements and techniques in something that is not a game, such as a company course. The thesis starts from a pyramid widely used in business: what you see (points, rankings) sits at the base, but it only works with the rules and the meaning above it. Tap a layer.

Interactivity shows up alongside almost every other topic, above all playfulness

09 Results · motivation

Prizes help, but the motivation that lasts comes from within

Intrinsic motivation comes from interest and satisfaction with the activity itself; extrinsic motivation, from rewards such as prizes, coins and discounts. In the interviews, intrinsic motivation links to seven other topics; extrinsic, only to engagement. Tap a pan of the scale.

10 The finding

Gamification works when it is part of the training design

For the interviewees, game elements come in from the start: in diagnosing what the team needs to learn, in the learning model and in the course rules. They are not "a few points or a prize at the end". Compare the two ways and see the answers to the thesis's three questions.

12 In practice, limits and credits

Before points and prizes comes the design of the training

HR and training managers

Start from what the team needs to learn and from the business strategy; use gamification as part of the design, with interaction, choices and practice, not as a layer of points at the end.

Those who build the solution

Dose the fun, give instant feedback, let people choose their path and adjust the difficulty. Tangible rewards come in as a complement to inner motivation.

Those who measure results

Measuring progress, completion and participants' opinions helps the team and motivates learners. Participants need to know what data is collected and agree to it, as the open gamification code of ethics cited in the thesis asks.

Participants

Ask for time within working hours to train and for content in short doses. Without time, no game mechanic keeps people engaged. · our reading, based on the interviews

What this research does not say

  • It only heard people who had already chosen gamification. So, as the author acknowledges, few counterpoints appear; the thesis explains the decision, it does not compare it with people who gave up or never used it.
  • It did not hear the training participants. What is said about motivation and retention comes from those who build the courses; the author suggests studying users, with mixed methods.
  • 16 people from companies with good-practice seals, found through events and professional networks. Companies without that structure may decide differently.
  • Counting excerpts does not measure effectiveness. Figures quoted in the interviews, such as a 98% approval for one training, are the interviewees' reports, not results measured by the thesis. · our note

Doctoral thesis · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

Suppliers that invest in research go with less indebted companies, but only among durable-goods makers

The thesis looked at 521 listed companies in the United Kingdom, from 2005 to 2014, and asked whether those who sell to them and those who buy from them help explain how much they owe. The expected link showed up with the suppliers of durable-goods makers; with customers and employees, it did not. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The idea

Whoever invests in a partnership loses a lot if the partner goes bust

A supplier that develops a custom part, a customer that depends on spare parts, an employee who learns something that only counts there: all of them lose if the company fails. Titman (1984) suggested that, so as not to scare these partners away, the company keeps little debt, as a promise that it will last.

Source: sections 1.1, 2.7.2 and 3.4 (Titman, 1984; Titman and Wessels, 1988; Maksimovic and Titman, 1991). The part, spare-parts and skill examples are ours.

03 The question

Five bets: three partners and two traits of the company itself

Studies of US companies (Kale and Shahrur, 2007; Banerjee, Dasgupta and Kim, 2008) found less debt when partners invest more in the relationship. The thesis wanted to know whether this also holds in the United Kingdom.

Your bet: with which partner will the “more investment, less debt” link show up?

Source: sections 1.1 and 3.4, Chart 7. The thesis measures partners’ investment by spending on research and development (R&D).

04 The method

Partners were measured by sector, using the table of who buys from whom in the economy

Companies do not say who their suppliers are. The thesis used the United Kingdom input-output table for 2010 (127 sectors), which shows how much each sector buys from every other. Each company thus inherits the typical suppliers and customers of its sector.

“Suppliers’ R&D” is the average research spending of the supplier sectors, weighted by how much the company buys from each. Move the weight of one supplier in a made-up company.

Source: sections 3.1 and 3.2 (equations 1 and 4; each sector’s R&D = R&D spending ÷ assets). The same calculation, weighted by sales, gives customers’ R&D. The example’s sectors and values are illustrative.

05 Who was included

521 British companies, and only one in five makes durable goods

Listed UK companies in the Compustat database with some R&D spending were included, without banks and utilities. The sample leans towards services and technology, so the thesis split it into three groups: durable goods (machinery, electronics, vehicles), non-durable goods (food, chemicals, medicines) and others.

06 What is measured

Debt was measured in four ways, and the average runs from 10% to 59%

All debts (including to suppliers) or only long-term loans; divided by balance-sheet assets or by the company’s value with shares at stock-market prices. Using all four tests whether the result survives a change of yardstick.

    09 The unexpected · customers

    With customers, when there was a link, it went with more debt

    The bet was the same: customers that spend more on R&D would want a company with little debt. In 8 of the 12 tests there was no difference; in the other 4, all in durables and “others”, debt was higher.

    Source: Tables 31, 33, 35 and 37 (after the correction). Before the correction (Tables 30, 32, 34 and 36), none of the customer tests passed. The thesis notes that it measures customers by sector, not one by one.

    10 The other bets

    More research-oriented employees also went with more debt, not less

    Each strip holds the tests of one piece: blue confirmed the bet, red went the other way, grey did not pass the test. Employees (H3) went against the bet in 7 of 12 tests. Asset turnover went with more debt; product uniqueness barely stood out.

    11 So what?

    The supplier chain enters the debt equation, at least among durable-goods makers

    For finance managers

    • In durables, companies with more research-oriented suppliers owed less, on all four yardsticks. (screen 07)
    • When deciding how much to borrow, it is worth thinking about who depends on the company to invest. (our reading)

    For company analysts

    • The link changed sign across groups and yardsticks; looking at sector and yardstick avoids generalizing. (screens 07 and 10)
    • With customers and employees, the result was the opposite of what US studies found. (thesis)

    For researchers

    • Repeat with Brazilian or Latin American companies and with models that follow changes over time. (thesis)
    • Build more precise measures of partners; in Brazil, the challenge is that companies rarely disclose R&D spending. (thesis)

    What this research does not say

    • Partners by sector. Suppliers and customers come from the sector’s 2010 table, not from each company’s own relationships; the same table was used for every year. · according to the thesis
    • Small sample. At most 4,401 records, against 76,290 and 34,716 in the US studies, and with many services and technology companies. · according to the thesis
    • Sensitive to method. The supplier result in durables only appeared after the error correction; and the employees’ sign on total debt flipped from negative to positive with it. · our observation
    • Turnover is not vertical integration. The thesis uses sales ÷ assets as a sign of vertical integration and says the more integrated company has lower turnover; so higher turnover with more debt does not show that integrating leads to more debt. · our reading
    • Extreme values. The data were not treated for extreme values; one company has total debt of 58 times its assets. · our observation (Table 3)
    • Association, not cause. The models do not say whether suppliers lead the company to owe less or whether something else explains both. · our observation

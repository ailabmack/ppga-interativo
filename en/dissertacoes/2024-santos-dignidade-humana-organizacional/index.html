What I feel and what the company does

Dignity at work has two sides: the dignity of each person and the dignity that shows in the company's practices. With 110 workers in the state of São Paulo, this research measured both together for the first time and found a strong link between them. Explained in 10 screens.

02 The problem

Dignity at work has two sides

Studies of dignity in organizations follow two paths that had never been measured together. One looks at the person; the other, at the company's practices. Pick an aspect and compare:

Human dignity

The dignity every person has

Pirson and colleagues (2023), United States
Organizational dignity

The dignity the company practices

Teixeira and colleagues (2014), Brazil

The dissertation's question: how are the two related, from the point of view of people who work in public and private companies in the state of São Paulo? Source: chapter 2 and Charts 1 to 3.

04 Who answered

210 people answered; 110 responses remained

The invitation went out in June and July 2024 through professional networks, LinkedIn and WhatsApp groups. Incomplete responses, people outside the target profile and those who marked the same number on almost everything were removed. The final test used 110 workers, twice the calculated minimum.

Circle area proportional to the number of people. Before the final test, the human dignity scale went through a pre-test (61 people) and a validation (116); the practices scale, through a validation (173). Source: section 4.4, Tables 1 to 4 and Final remarks.

06 What it found · the picture

The company doesn't deceive, but it hardly promotes

Two in three workers feel dignified, but only half feel their boss and organization treat them that way. On practices, almost everyone says the company doesn't deceive; few see employee advancement or social responsibility. Tap a bar:

07 The finding

Those who feel respected see a more dignified company

The hypothesis was confirmed. The more people feel their dignity is protected and promoted, the better they rate the company's practices, toward them and toward customers and society. It is a strong link: a coefficient of 0.670, out of a maximum of 1.

08 What it found · the model

Feeling dignified accounts for almost all of it

The model explains about 46% of the differences in how people rate the practices. Besides human dignity, the author tested the profile: education, gender, age and holding a leadership position. None of them made a significant difference; almost all the explanation comes from the dignity the person feels. Tap each factor:

09 Why it matters

Treating people well is the basis of dignified practices

For the author, how workers rate their company depends heavily on how they feel treated. The dignified treatment that managers and the organization give people is the starting point.

For managers and leaders

  • Workers don't separate how the boss treats them from how the company treats them (screen 05).
  • On the scale, a good manager recognizes people, is fair, respects autonomy, includes others in decisions and apologizes when wrong.
Section 5.2, Discussion and Chart 1

For companies

  • Go beyond the law: paying on time and granting vacations was the most recognized (60.4%), and the author argues it should be close to 100%.
  • Advancement and social responsibility are barely perceived (22.5% and 28.1%), despite all the talk about ESG.
Discussion, Table 14

For HR and consultancies

  • The two scales complement each other: the human dignity scale assesses how managers treat their teams; the practices scale also looks at customers and society.
  • HR has a central role in tracking the perception of dignity, which is linked to well-being.
Final remarks

For workers

  • Dignity at work is more than being paid on time: it means being treated as a person, not a resource, and having your opinion count.
  • The scale statements help put a name to what is missing.
Our reading of Charts 1 and 2

For the author, the study shows how far organizations still need to go toward more dignified working relationships, as seen by those who work in them.

10 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • A small sample from one state. 110 valid responses, from São Paulo. It is worth repeating with more people, other sectors and other states. · according to the author
  • Leaders and non-leaders. Holding a leadership position came close to making a difference (p = 0.06). Comparing the two groups is a next step. · according to the author
  • The scale still needs testing. The three parts became two in Brazil; new validations should show whether this happens again and why. · according to the author
  • The order of the questions. The practices came first, and more people answered that part. It is worth reversing the order in another sample. · according to the author
  • Invited through networks, at a single point in time. It is not a representative sample (more than half have a graduate degree), and each person answered once: the results show association, not cause. · our note

The unions heard have a department for women and another for young people, but none for older workers

Brazil is ageing fast, and people past 40 find it harder to keep a job or find a new one. The dissertation heard 11 leaders of trade unions and union confederations: they see companies prefer the young and lay off older workers first, yet what they do for these workers is little, sporadic and almost always after the layoff.

02 The problem

Brazil will age in 25 years as much as France did in 145

Fewer children are born and people live longer. In Europe, governments, companies and unions have discussed for decades how to keep older people working. In Portuguese, the author found only three articles on unions, ageing and employment. The question: what have unions done so that older workers keep their jobs or get back into work?

05 The unexpected

Seven of the 11 leaders say the workers in their sector are not getting older

Their explanation is turnover: companies lay off older workers and put young people in their place, so the workforce renews itself and stays young. The author noticed surprise in some faces and voices, as if they were realising something for the first time.

Table 1 of the dissertation (Amorim, 2016). Our reading: across the country, the share of people aged 50 or over among the employed grew. The leaders speak about their own sectors, not the country, but the contrast shows how easily the exit of older workers can go unnoticed.

06 The mirror

Leaders criticise companies' stereotypes and repeat the same ones in their own words

Almost all say companies see older workers as less capable. But over the conversations, the author heard the same ideas from the leaders themselves. For him, no one escapes the beliefs of the society they live in, not even those who defend workers. Compare the two sides:

07 What weighs besides age

A higher salary, less schooling and less technology weigh against those who grow older

The author calls these three “aggravating factors”: they are not beliefs but facts common among older workers that add to the stereotypes. Those who studied 30 years ago had less schooling and fewer computers, for lack of opportunity, not of ability. Tap a row:

09 The finding

What unions do for older workers is little, scattered and almost always after the layoff

Faced with the question about actions, the author saw perplexity and embarrassment. The answers range from “we've never done anything” to a verbal agreement with two companies. No one mentioned an organised agenda for these workers. Tap a step:

Section 4.10. The order of the steps is ours; the same leader may appear on more than one. The collective-agreement clauses mentioned protect those close to retirement for a short time; for the author, they do not keep older workers employed.

10 A way out?

Eight of the 11 leaders back social dialogue, but distrust companies and mistake it for the usual bargaining

For the ILO (International Labour Organization), social dialogue means government, companies and workers talking to solve problems that affect many people, with concessions on both sides. That is how European countries such as Finland and Norway kept older people at work. In Brazil, attempts were short-lived: the automotive industry's sector chamber (1992, about three years) and the National Labour Forum, which did not move forward.

11 So what?

Older workers need to get onto the unions' agenda, as women and young people already have

Unions and confederationsThe author expects unions to take up the defence of older workers despite companies' resistance, and recalls studies showing more jobs for the young where older people stay employed.
Companies and governmentThe government wants to delay retirement, which requires keeping older people employed. For the author, social dialogue can address this, if there is first trust, autonomy and a broad agenda.
Workers aged 40+Leaders advocate retraining (courses, partnerships with universities). Our reading: asking your union what it does for people past 40 already puts the issue on the agenda.

What the research does not say

  • Only São Paulo and Curitiba. The local unions come from these two metropolitan areas and four sectors; other regions, sectors and confederations may think differently. (author)
  • Without the government. The government's role was left out of the study, although it is central to the issue. (author)
  • These are perceptions. What we know about companies and hiring and layoff ages comes from 11 leaders' words, not from company records or from workers. (our note)
  • A snapshot of 2017–2018. The quotes refer to the November 2017 labour reform as recent; positions and actions may have changed since. (our note)

Next steps suggested by the author: study age discrimination across sectors, regions and education levels; include government and civil society; and cross age with gender and race.

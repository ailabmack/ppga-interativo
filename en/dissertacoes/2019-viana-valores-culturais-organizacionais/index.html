Do the values you bring from your country change how you see your company?

Everyone arrives at work with values learned since childhood: how much they accept hierarchy, how much they like rules, how much they put the group first. The dissertation asked 203 Brazilian workers about these values and about the culture of the company they worked for. Those who put the group first see the company a little more as a family, but the link is small. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The problem · Hofstede (1985)

Every founder was once a child, and brings to the company what they learned at home

Geert Hofstede crossed two values of each country, power distance (how much people accept power staying with a few) and uncertainty avoidance (how much people look for rules to avoid surprises), and drew four models of organization. He never tested the idea with data. Tap a quadrant:

03 The other side · Cameron and Quinn (2006)

Family, lab, arena or bureau: four ways a company works

Cameron and Quinn cross two choices every company makes: looking inward or outward, and giving freedom or seeking control. Out come four types of culture, which live together in different doses in the same company. The author saw a likeness to Hofstede: adhocracy resembles the “village market”, and hierarchy the “pyramid of people”. Tap a type:

06 The unexpected · section 6.1 and Table 14

Hofstede saw a hierarchical Brazil; these workers did not

In Hofstede's data, which measure countries, power distance is Brazil's 2nd strongest trait. Here, measured in each person, it fell to last place. Another study of individuals (Marques, 2014) reached averages very close to these. Tap a value to follow its position:

The author gives two possible explanations: measuring a person is not the same as measuring a country, and answers may lean toward what is socially approved (“social desirability”), at a time of public debate about equality in Brazil.

10 The finding · Table 13

Putting the group first pulls toward clan and away from market, but explains little

Putting all values into one model (regression), three links held up. The bet that every country value would weigh on every company type was not confirmed. Pick a type and see how much of the differences between people the values explain:

Table 13: collectivism → market, p = 0.038 (the table prints 0.38; the text and the F of 4.340 give 0.038, our check), explains 1.6%; collectivism → clan, p = 0.001, explains 4.4%; uncertainty avoidance adds 1.8% to clan (about 6% together, our sum). In the simple correlation (screen 9), uncertainty avoidance and clan did not move together (−0.086): the link only shows with collectivism in the same model, and the author calls it “less clear”. Masculinity, linked on screen 9, did not stay in the model. “Impact” here is statistical: moving together does not prove cause.

11 So what?

Country culture counts, but company culture depends on much more

In the author's reading, knowing the values people bring helps build teams and bring companies together, as long as one does not expect too much of them.

For those who hire and build teams

  • Those who put the group first tend to adapt better to clan-type companies and to struggle more in market-type ones. · author
  • The link is weak: useful for a conversation, not as a hiring filter. · our reading

For mergers, expansion and going abroad

  • Understanding how society's culture and the company's touch helps in mergers, acquisitions and internationalisation. · author
  • Purpose weighs too: a police station leans to hierarchy more than a record shop, wherever its founder comes from. · Hofstede, cited by the author

For researchers

  • Use a company culture scale of the “agree/disagree” kind, which matches the values scale better. · author
  • Look at where each person was raised and socialised, education, and the company's size and sector. · author

What this study does not say

  • It does not speak for every Brazilian worker. Convenience sample: 83% in the Southeast and 55% aged 18 to 30. · Table 1, our note
  • Two rulers that do not fit well together. Agreeing from 1 to 5 and splitting 100 points produce numbers of different kinds; the author adjusted the data but points out the problem. · according to the author
  • Answers may lean toward what is approved. Especially on hierarchy and gender roles. · according to the author
  • Education, size and sector were not asked. · according to the author
  • Moving together does not prove cause. The effects explain from 1.6% to about 6%. · our note

A large bank's sustainability courses teach more about how to act than about why

The dissertation analysed the 42 courses in the sustainability track of a Brazilian state-controlled bank and heard 11 instructors and 81 employees. Only 9 courses invite people to question, face-to-face sessions shrank, and about a third of learners say they changed how they see or act.

Author: Vanessa Mariano de Castro Vieira Advisor: Prof. Dr. Janette Brunstein Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

03 The idea behind it

Solving a problem is different from questioning the problem itself

The author draws on Jack Mezirow's theory. In problem solving reflection, a person improves the content or the way something is done. In critical reflection, or problem posing, they ask whether the problem is framed well and where their beliefs come from. For the author, sustainability calls for the second kind, because it means changing how we produce and consume.

04 How the research looked

Eleven instructors and 81 employees told how they experienced two face-to-face courses

The case is a Brazilian state-controlled bank with over 90,000 employees, listed in the stock exchange's sustainability indexes. Between March and May 2017, the author read about 700 pages of documents and course materials, interviewed instructors and e-mailed ten open questions to people who had taken the Sustainable Development course or the Citizen Education workshop.

05 What the bank offers

Of the 42 sustainability-related courses, 37 are taken alone on the intranet

The track mixes ethics, volunteering and the environment with ergonomics, first aid and even fighting the dengue mosquito. The author read each course's goal: 33 teach how to act and 9 invite asking why. The most taken are the ethics courses, which belong to a mandatory track. The five face-to-face ones, the only ones with group debate, reached 1% to 4% of employees.

07 The two initiatives studied

The course explains the bank's model and the workshop puts employees face to face with the community

The Sustainable Development course lasts two days, for employees only, and teaches how to apply the bank's model so branches can plan social, environmental and business actions. The Citizen Education workshop lasts three days, draws on Paulo Freire and welcomes outside guests: waste pickers, community leaders, volunteers. Instructors speak of “shock”.

To tell the truth, the workshop has only kept running because of these people from the community, because if it depended only on the bank's employees we might not have the workshops we still have…E2 · workshop

08 Play as in the course

In the course game, whoever raises the yellow card thinks they are winning

In the course, the class splits into four groups. In each situation, each group raises a card: yellow gives points only to itself and takes from the others; blue gives points to everyone. Instructors say that most of the time groups choose their own interest. You are group A. Play five rounds.

09 The finding

About a third of learners say they changed how they see or act

In the interviews, instructors of both courses talk about changing how people see and fighting individualism. Learners' replies are dominated by “I got to know the bank's actions” and one-off actions: saving paper, sorting waste, donating. For the author, the discourse is transformative, but the transformation is occasional.

What people lack is not information, it's that they really rethink, internalise, change their actions…E1 · course and workshop

10 What weighs on it

Five elements explain why reflection fades when employees go back to the branch

The author proposes five elements for analysing corporate education for sustainability. At the bank, almost all of them work against it: short-term targets, lack of support from managers, courses far from daily routine and ever fewer face-to-face classes. Click each block in the cycle.

11 Takeaways

For the author, reflection needs meeting in person, dialogue and support after the course

She does not propose that everyone take the courses, but that more people do and the topic does not fade. Some suggestions come from the instructors themselves.

For those who design company courses

  • Classes with half employees and half outside guests.
  • Two meetings: one to discuss dilemmas and another, some time later, to discuss what turned into action.
  • Video calls to multiply classes without travel costs.

For managers and HR

  • Support after the course: managers, teams and targets that make room for the topic.
  • Instructor follow-up after the course, including inviting retired instructors back.
  • Evaluate the effect of classes and keep content tied to everyday work.

For those who take a course like this

  • Asking why something is done, not only how, is what the theory calls critical reflection. (our reading)
  • The dilemmas of going back to work (today's target × long-term effect) are part of learning, not a sign of failure. (our reading)

What this research does not say

  • What happens in class. Observing classes was planned but not authorised; this is the main limitation, according to the author. · according to the author
  • Whether it holds for other banks. It is a single case; the author suggests comparing with other banks and sectors. · according to the author
  • What all learners think. 7% to 8% of those invited replied, voluntarily; the author says she was aware of the bias of people who identify with the topic. Some had taken the workshop years earlier and no longer remembered. · according to the author
  • How much each course makes people reflect in practice. The classification of the 42 courses comes from written goals, not attended classes. · our note

In seven of the world's eight largest stock markets, stocks moved as a herd on turbulent days

When the market jumps or plunges, do investors stick to their own analysis or do what everyone else does? The dissertation followed the 50 most traded stocks in eight stock markets, day by day, from 2008 to 2017. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The question

On a day of panic, do you follow your own analysis or the crowd?

Classical finance assumes a rational investor who decides alone with the information available. Behavioral finance studies departures from that rule. One of them is herd behavior: setting your own view aside to do what the market does.

−8%
Picture it: the market opens sharply down, as on the days of the 2008 crisis. Your analysis says your stock is worth more than today's price. What do you do?

Choose an option

There is no right answer: the research does not look at people, it looks at the trail everyone's choices leave in prices.

Source: chapters 1 and 3 of the dissertation (Christie and Huang, 1995; Chang, Cheng and Khorana, 2000). The research question: “Is there evidence of herd behavior in the stock markets of these countries?”

03 The method

Herding shows up when stocks stray little from the average on a day of big moves

The measure is dispersion (CSAD): the average distance between each stock's return and the market's average return that day. On a turbulent day, if everyone decides alone, stocks spread out more. If everyone follows the same direction, they stay together.

In the dissertation, dispersion is computed every day for the 50 stocks of each market (equation 3). The ten returns on the right are made up, to show the arithmetic.

05 The test

Without herding, dispersion would grow in a straight line; with herding, the line bends down

The dissertation uses the Chang, Cheng and Khorana (2000) test: a regression of dispersion on the size of the market's move. If the curve term (γ2) comes out negative and significant, dispersion grows less than expected on big days: a sign of herding.

The lines use the coefficients estimated for each market (Table 3). The straight line is the same formula without the curve term, for comparison. The standard deviation of the market's daily return was between 1.18% and 2.09% (Table 2): days above 4% are rare.

06 The result · 2008 to 2017

Seven of the eight markets showed signs of herding; Germany did not

Over the whole period, the curve term came out negative and significant (at 1%) in seven markets. India had the strongest bend and Japan the weakest, close to Brazil. In Germany, the term was positive and not significant.

Height: size of the curve term (γ2, absolute value), Table 3. Red: negative and significant at 1%. Glass: positive and not significant.

07 Five two-year periods

Split into two-year periods, the herding sign appeared in 32 of 40 combinations

The dissertation re-ran the test for 2008/09, 2010/11, 2012/13, 2014/15 and 2016/17. China, Korea, India and Japan showed the sign in all five periods; Brazil and the United Kingdom in four; the United States in three; Germany in one, only at 10%.

08 The unexpected

Herding was not stronger on down days

The dissertation's hypothesis came from loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky): the fear of losing weighs more than the pleasure of winning, so herding would be stronger on down days. Before you look, place your bet:

09 Trading volume

On busy trading days, herding was stronger in six markets; in the United States it was the opposite

A day counts as “busy” when traded volume exceeds the average of the previous 30 days. Korea, China, the United Kingdom, Japan, Brazil and India had a stronger bend on those days, most clearly in the three East Asian markets according to the author. In the United States the bend was stronger on quiet days. In Germany, no sign on either.

10 The finding

When stocks in one market spread out, stocks in the others spread out too

The dissertation added the dispersion of the other seven markets to each market's equation. Of 56 links, 35 came out positive and significant (our count): dispersions move together. Neighbors weigh more (Korea with Japan and China; Germany with the United Kingdom), and so do the big ones (United States and Germany).

Source: Table 6 (daily data, 2008 to 2017, 2,584 days). Line thickness: coefficient size. Dashed blue lines: negative and significant coefficients. Germany, with no sign of herding, has the largest weights on the others (0.349 in the United States and 0.314 in the United Kingdom). The link shows that markets move together, not who copies whom (our observation).

11 So what?

If markets spill over, spreading money across countries protects less than it seems

Diversifying works when assets do not move together. If herding appears in several countries at once, for the author, diversification across them loses part of its effect.

For international investors

  • Take the strong links between markets into account when building a global portfolio. (author)
  • Neighbors and large economies weigh more: markets in the same region offer less protection against each other. (our reading of screen 10)

For those who watch the market

  • Herding can threaten financial stability in turbulent periods, such as the 2008 crisis, which began in the United States and spread. (author)
  • Busy trading days were the ones with the strongest herding in most markets (screen 09). (Table 5)

For research

  • Mature and emerging markets in the same test: herding appeared in both groups; only Germany stayed out. (Table 3)
  • Suggested next steps: more countries and periods, real estate, funds and commodities, less traded stocks and corrections for multiple testing. (author)
This is not investment advice. The dissertation describes the past (2008 to 2017) of 400 stocks in eight markets. It measures a pattern in prices; it does not test trading strategies or costs, and what held in the past does not guarantee the future.
12 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • A sign in prices, not in people. The test looks at the dispersion of returns; it does not observe who bought or sold or why. Other causes that pull stocks together on the same day (news that affects them all) also reduce dispersion. · our observation
  • Only the 50 most traded stocks. Less traded stocks may behave differently; the author suggests comparing them. · according to the author
  • Untested differences. Up × down and busy × quiet days were compared by coefficient size, without testing whether the difference is significant. · our observation
  • Many tests at once. With dozens of regressions, some results at 10% may appear by chance; the author mentions multiple-testing corrections as a next step. · according to the author
  • Moving together is not copying. Screen 10 shows that dispersions move together on the same day; it does not say which market drives the other. · our observation

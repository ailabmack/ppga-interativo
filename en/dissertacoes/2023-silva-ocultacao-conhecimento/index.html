Why some people hide what they know at work

A colleague asks for help and the person says they don’t know, although they do. With 231 employees of a technology company, the dissertation measures what goes along with this behaviour: feeling that the knowledge is yours, wanting to protect your own territory and fearing a loss of power when you share. Explained in 12 screens.

08 What it found

People who feel knowledge is theirs say they hide more

The feeling of ownership is linked to hiding directly, and so is territoriality. But what goes along with territoriality the most is loss of power. Tap an arrow or a control:

09 The discovery

Fear of losing power is what most leads people to guard their territory

Those who feel sharing takes away their exclusivity are the ones who most say others should not use their ideas. That fear also strengthens the weight of ownership. Move the control:

12 So what?

To reduce hiding, work on the sense of ownership and the fear of losing power

For the author, the way out is to make knowledge feel “ours”, not just “mine”, and to reward those who share.

For managers and HR

  • Strengthen the sense that knowledge belongs to the team, with teamwork actions and commitment to the organisation. (author)
  • Rewards and promotions for those who share relevant knowledge, to balance the sense of loss. (author, with Tian and colleagues, 2021)

For the organisation

  • Create sharing situations: on-the-job training, mentoring, job rotation and storytelling among colleagues. (author)
  • Raise awareness of the harm of hiding, because just encouraging sharing is not enough. (author, with Lanke, 2018)

For people at work

  • Our reading: notice when “this is mine” turns into “nobody may use it”, and remember that sharing also brings recognition (57% say knowledge makes them valued).

What this research does not say

  • A single company. One nuclear technology company, with 75% of respondents in formally secure jobs; the comparison studies are almost all from China. · according to the author
  • Wish to look good. There was no scale to control for social desirability, and almost everyone denied hiding. The test with unrelated statements showed no method bias. · according to the author
  • What goes together. Everything comes from a questionnaire answered once by the person: it shows associations, not what causes what, and does not observe real hiding. · our observation
  • Small effects. Apart from loss of power on territoriality, the effect sizes are small; the model explains 43% of the differences in hiding. · author and our observation
  • Tacit and explicit. The research did not separate tacit knowledge, which is easier to hide, from explicit knowledge. · according to the author

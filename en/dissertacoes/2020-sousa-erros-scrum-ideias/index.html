Master’s dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

In an IT team, can a mistake turn into an idea?

With 123 IT professionals who work with Scrum, the dissertation finds a chain: where the team discusses mistakes and uses them to learn, people feel safer; those who feel safe say they learn more from mistakes; and those who learn say they have, and push forward, more ideas. Each link is clear, and each one explains only part of the story. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem

People who build software cannot avoid every mistake

In settings built on creating and testing, such as technology teams that work with Scrum, mistakes are part of the road. The dissertation opens with a study by Amy Edmondson in hospitals: the teams where people felt safer recorded more mistakes, not fewer. Why? Make your guess.

05 How the research looked

123 IT professionals with Scrum experience

The online questionnaire circulated through LinkedIn, WhatsApp, Facebook and email between February and April 2020, without identifying anyone. The people kept were those in IT departments with at least three months of Scrum, the way of working in short cycles with frequent reviews.

07 What it found · 123 professionals

All six bets along the chain held up

The dissertation tested a chain: climate → safety → learning from mistakes → four sides of ideas. The numbers on the arrows: the closer to 1, the more the two things rise together. Tap a bet:

Table 6 and Figure 3 (SmartPLS, 5,000 resamples). Climate combines two parts: the team analysing and discussing mistakes, and using mistakes to learn (loadings 0.914 and 0.895).

08 The finding

People who learn from mistakes say they have more ideas, above all looking for what to improve

Innovative behaviour was measured in four sides. Learning from mistakes rises along with all four; the strongest link is with exploring (looking for chances to improve), the weakest with generating new solutions. Switch the scale and tap a bar:

Table 6. The difference between the four bars is small and the dissertation did not test whether they differ from one another (our note).

09 The size of the effect

From climate to ideas, the effect thins out link by link

10 The unexpected

Outside the chain, there are shortcuts too

The model only tested the links in a row. But the dissertation’s correlation table shows that climate goes along with learning (0.577) more than safety does (0.413), and that safety goes along with ideas as much as learning does. Compare:

Our readingCorrelations from Table 5 (all significant at 5%). A correlation shows what goes together, without accounting for anything else; the dissertation did not estimate the shortcuts, so we cannot tell whether they would vanish if entered alongside the chain.

12 So what?

Treating mistakes as a team matter is part of innovating

For the author, anyone who wants people who create, share and put ideas into practice cannot neglect the climate, safety and learning from mistakes.

For the team

  • Encourage communication and tolerance among colleagues when there is a mistake. (author)
  • Our reading: discussing mistakes in detail had the lowest mean (screen 6), a place to start.

For those who lead

  • Do not neglect climate, safety and learning from mistakes if the goal is innovative behaviour. (author)
  • Reflect with the team on what a mistake means, without fear of reprisal or dismissal. (author)

For the company and HR

  • Analyse the role of mistakes in work processes, above all where people experiment and test. (author)
  • Study whether Scrum itself helps people see mistakes positively. (author’s agenda)

What this research does not say

  • IT tilts it. People who work in technology already live with tests and experiments; the positive view of mistakes may reflect the field. · according to the author
  • 123 people. A sample spread through social media does not allow generalisation; area, setting and personal values cannot be told apart. · according to the author
  • Link is not cause. A questionnaire answered once shows what goes together; the author suggests studies over time and with interviews. · our note and the author’s
  • Only the chain. The shortcuts (screen 10) and the indirect effect (screen 9) were not tested. · our note
  • The same person answers everything. The author ruled out this bias because the scales have different anchors (agree × how often); there was no statistical test of it. · our note

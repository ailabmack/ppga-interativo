By the dissertation's math, Brazilian companies could carry more debt: the tax saved would outweigh the risk of failing

Interest on debt reduces taxes. But too much debt lowers the credit rating and brings bankruptcy closer. With 38 Brazilian listed companies, from 2010 to 2018, the dissertation calculates that the average firm would have to more than double its debt to lose one notch in its rating, and that the tax saved would be worth 23.5% of the firm's value, against a 2.0% expected cost of bankruptcy. With the yardsticks of two foreign studies, that slack shrinks a lot.

02 The tax saving

Every real of interest cuts taxes, but only until interest swallows the profit

In Brazil, a company taxed on actual profit deducts interest on its debt before computing taxes. At the top rate of 34% (15% income tax + 10% surcharge + 9% social contribution), every R$ 1 of interest saves up to R$ 0.34 of tax.

Once interest exceeds operating profit, there is no profit left to deduct from. The dissertation uses the yardstick operating profit ÷ interest (the kink): above 1, the firm still has room for more debt and is called conservative; below 1, aggressive. Move the interest.

03 The other side

Firms that owed more had worse credit ratings

The credit rating (from AAA, the best, down to C) tells how much the market trusts the firm to pay. Each notch down raises the chance of default and makes credit costlier: that is the risk of failing side.

Among the 38 firms, from 2010 to 2018, half of the yearly ratings were BB. Average debt rises at each notch, from 24% of assets for A-rated firms to 64% for CCC. Tap a notch.

04 Two questions

Why don't companies use all the tax saving that debt could give?

Hypothesis 1 · risk
If the firm took on more debt, would the tax saved exceed the expected cost of getting closer to bankruptcy? If so, it is being conservative.
Hypothesis 2 · other shortcuts
Does the firm skip the debt tax saving because it already cuts taxes in other ways, such as accumulated losses?

05 The test, step 1

To lose one notch in its rating, the average firm would have to more than double its debt

The dissertation estimated how the credit rating responds to debt, profit and size. More debt pulls the rating down; more profit and larger size push it up. The average firm sat at BB.

Source: Table 11 (an ordered probit model, estimated jointly with the debt equation) and Table 13. Fixed assets at the average. The ruler shows the band the score falls into, as the dissertation does in its test; the model actually gives a probability for each rating. At the average, debt has to go from 41% to 96% of assets (2.36 times) for the rating to fall from BB to B.

06 The test, step 2 · the finding

At every tax rate tested, the tax saved beat the cost of the risk

If average debt rose from R$ 18.9 billion to R$ 44.7 billion, at a 34% tax rate the tax saved would be worth 23.5% of firm value. On the other side, falling from BB to B raises the 10-year chance of default by 12 points; since bankruptcy costs 10% to 23% of value, the expected cost rises by only 2.0%.

That is why the dissertation concludes that the firms are conservative: they leave tax savings on the table. Change the tax rate used in the math.

Source: Table 13 and section 4.4. The balance for each rate is the dissertation's; the tax saved at the lower rates is our calculation (balance + 2.0). The math assumes perpetual debt and a fully used deduction (Graham, 2000); the chance of default comes from S&P (2018) and the cost of bankruptcy from Andrade and Kaplan (1998), with US firms.

07 The unexpected

With the yardsticks of two foreign studies, the slack shrinks or vanishes

The rating model was estimated with only 38 firms. The dissertation redid the math with the models of Molina (2005, 3,048 firms) and Clemente-Almendros and Mira (2018, 88 firms). In them, the rating drops much sooner: with debt at 52% or 48% of assets, not 96%.

Less extra debt means less tax saved. Pick a yardstick and move the cost of bankruptcy: with Clemente-Almendros and Mira's, the balance changes sign.

Our calculation with Tables 13, 15 and 16

Expected cost = rise in the 10-year chance of default (11.99, 8.78 and 18.66 points, S&P 2018) × loss in bankruptcy (10% to 23%, Andrade and Kaplan, 1998). Balances printed in the dissertation, with the average cost: 21.5%, 3.2% and −1.1% (the last with a cost of 4.3%, at the top of the range). In the conclusion, Clemente-Almendros and Mira's yardstick gives −1.1% to 0.8%.

08 Firm by firm

For firms with the least slack, the gain from owing more was the smallest

The dissertation split the firms by the operating profit ÷ interest yardstick. Those averaging below 1.2 already owed 63% of assets: owing more until the rating fell would yield a balance of only 1.0% of value. Among those with slack above 3, the balance would be 41.5%.

09 Hypothesis 2

Firms with accumulated losses to deduct from taxes left more slack in their debt

Losses from previous years also reduce taxes. Firms with such a balance showed a profit ÷ interest yardstick 2.5 points higher, in all three models. For the dissertation, this confirms hypothesis 2: other tax shortcuts take away part of the advantage of borrowing.

The other two shortcuts tested went the opposite way: deferred taxes above operating profit and higher depreciation went with less slack. Tap each factor.

10 Behind the scenes

Estimating debt and rating at the same time changed two results

Debt moves the rating, and the rating moves debt. That is why the dissertation estimated both together (simultaneous equations) and compared with the usual model, which looks only at debt.

11 So what?

There may be room for more debt, but the answer depends on the yardstick

For finance directors

  • On average, operating profit covered interest 4.3 times: there was profit left to deduct more interest from. (screen 02)
  • Firms already working near 1 would gain little from more debt. (screen 08)

For lenders

  • More debt came with a worse rating; more profit and larger size, with a better one. (screen 05)
  • Riskier firms borrowed less. (screen 10)

For tax managers

  • Accumulated losses went with more slack in debt: the interest deduction matters less when another deduction already exists. (screen 09)

For researchers

  • Measure the cost of bankruptcy and the chance of default by rating in Brazil, and compute the marginal tax rate with Graham's method. (dissertation)
  • Include interest on equity (JSCP), another Brazilian tax deduction. (dissertation)

What this research does not say

  • Only 38 firms, all large. Requiring a rating every year left few firms; for the dissertation, this may have overstated the debt needed to lower the rating and, with it, the gain. · per the dissertation
  • Outside parameters. The cost of bankruptcy comes from US firms and the chance of default from global S&P tables; in Brazil, bankruptcy may cost more. · per the dissertation
  • The yardstick changes the answer. With other studies' models, the balance falls close to zero or turns negative. · screen 07
  • Simplified math. It assumes perpetual debt, a fully used deduction and the rating by score band; it is not a recommendation of how much any firm should owe. · our note

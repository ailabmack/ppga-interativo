To explore the new without dropping what already works, shared goals and culture were what counted

Among 93 people from Brazilian companies, of the three faces of working relationships (who is connected to whom, how much people trust each other, and what goals and culture they share), only the last was linked to ambidexterity: the ability to explore the new and exploit what already exists at the same time. 02 The problem · ambidexterity

A company needs to seek the new and make the most of what it already has, at the same time

March (1991) called the search for the new (researching, experimenting, taking risks) exploration, and making the most of what already exists (refining, executing, being efficient) exploitation. Doing both at once is ambidexterity, like using both hands. Sort the activities:

Activities taken from the questionnaire statements (Appendix 5) and from March’s definition cited in section 2.2. For March, those who only explore spend a lot and reap little; those who only exploit get stuck in what they know and grow obsolete.

04 How the research looked

93 people from 62 companies answered 45 statements

In October and November 2021, the author ran an online questionnaire at a large multinational, called Alpha in the dissertation (27 responses), and among people from 61 other companies in her network (66 responses). There were 18 statements on relationships and 27 on exploring and exploiting, rated 1 to 7. Beforehand, 10 volunteers tested the questions.

06 What it found · the scores

All the means sat above the middle of the scale, and competition was the least felt

In relationships, the highest score was respect (6.11), and the lowest were friendship and closeness of the network (4.78). In exploring and exploiting, radical technology innovations came out on top (6.05) and competition at the bottom (4.86). Tap a column:

Tables 2 and 3 (93 responses). Columns start at 1, the lowest point on the scale. Relationship scores did not change with profile; seven exploring and exploiting statements differed by company or by age (Table 4).

07 The hypotheses

The literature bet on all three faces. What about you?

The author tested three hypotheses: each face of relationships, with the other two in the same model, would lead to more ambidexterity. Bet before you look:

Hypotheses H1 to H3 (section 2.4) and Figure 9 (SmartPLS 3, 2,000 resamples). Numbers on arrows = standardized coefficient (β). On the arrows leaving ambidexterity and under each side, how strongly exploring and exploiting make up ambidexterity and each part makes up its side.

08 The finding

Only the cognitive face was linked to ambidexterity

With all three faces together in the model, the cognitive one (shared goals and culture) had a coefficient of 0.601, with p < 0.001. The structural one was 0.193, with no clear effect at 5%, and the relational one practically zero (−0.006). Switch between the theory’s bet and the data:

Figure 9 and section 4.2. Height = β without its sign. The dissertation does not report the p of the structural and relational faces; it only says they were not significant at 5%. The 56.7% is the R² of the model with all three faces.

09 The unexpected

The three faces move so closely together that they are hard to tell apart

Trust was not linked to ambidexterity in the model, yet on its own it goes hand in hand with knowledge practices (correlation of 0.72). Before seeing why, a test: which face does each questionnaire statement belong to?

Correlations from Table 7 (first-order parts) and Table 8 (cognitive × structural, 0.819). Overlap drawn by us, only to give the idea. Our reading: when the faces move together like this, the model tends to give the weight to one of them, and the others are left with no effect of their own.

10 How relationships show up on each side

Relationships go more with exploring than with exploiting

The dissertation’s subtitle asks how relationships show up on each side. The correlations in the tables give a clue: every part of relationships goes more with knowledge and innovation practices than with competition, efficiency, time horizon and partnerships. Choose a part:

Tables 7 and 8. Correlation shows what moves together, not what causes what. Our reading: the relationship statements are about the team and the company from the inside, while partnerships are about outsiders, which may explain the weak link; partnerships are also the part that weighs least in exploitation (0.586 in Figure 9).

11 So what? · limits and credits

What counted was the team sharing goals and culture

For those who lead

In the author’s reading, clear goals and culture kept teams on course even with the remote work of the pandemic. Investing in shared goals and a common way of working across departments seems the place to start. (author)

For people managers

The questionnaire can become a map: applied at a company, it shows which faces of relationships and which parts of exploring and exploiting deserve attention. (author) Trust and ties stay on the list: they move together with the faces that counted. (our reading)

For researchers

Study the three faces separately in more companies, to see each one’s effect on exploring and exploiting, and repeat the research outside the pandemic period. (author)

What this research does not say

  • An unusual moment. Data were collected online in late 2021, with many people working from home or hybrid; this may have weakened ties and trust. · according to the author
  • Mixed companies. The 93 responses come from one multinational and 61 other companies; a single-company sample would give more uniform data, but would only hold for that company. · according to the author
  • Overlapping faces. Between trust, ties, network and shared goals, correlations range from 0.75 to 0.82: each face’s weight is hard to separate; “no effect” in the model does not mean trust and ties do not matter. · our observation
  • A small sample for three arrows. The minimum of 68 was calculated for a single predictor; the model has three. A moderate effect such as the structural one (0.193) may have fallen below what 93 responses can detect. · our observation
  • Once, by the same person. Each person answered everything at a single moment, giving their own perception: the numbers show links, they do not prove cause. · our observation

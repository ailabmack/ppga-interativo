Does the climate enter a stock's debut price?

When a company debuts on the stock exchange, its shares often close the first day above the offer price: a discount for those who bought. The research gathered 880 debuts in 25 countries, from 2015 to 2024, and asked whether environmental rules and climate change move this discount. Explained in 12 screens.

02 Try it

At the debut, shares tend to come out cheap

Going public (an IPO, initial public offering) means selling shares on the stock exchange for the first time. The company and its banks set an offer price. On the first trading day, the market says what the share is worth.

When the first-day close is above the offer price, there is a debut discount (underpricing): a quick gain for those who bought at the offer. The math is simple: (close − offer) ÷ offer. Drag to see.

It may or may not be on purpose. According to the dissertation, the discount can be a strategy to attract investors and give the shares liquidity, or something unexpected.

03 The theories behind it

Buyers know less than sellers

At the debut, managers know the company from the inside; investors do not. To make up for the doubt, investors ask for a discount. Three finance theories explain this game, and all point to the same remedy: clear, reliable information. Tap the cards.

H1 · Firm. The discount is smaller for firms more committed to climate policies.
H2 · Country rule. The discount is smaller where disclosing environmental practices is mandatory.
H3 · Country climate. The discount is larger in countries with more climate change.

05 Measuring the firm's commitment

Few debutants show a carbon score, and the scores are low

The CDP (Carbon Disclosure Project) gives firms a score from F to A for how they handle climate issues. It is voluntary: only 145 of the 880 debutants had a score.

The average was 0.86 on a 0-to-8 scale, close to D−: firms that are starting to disclose but do not yet manage their impacts in a structured way. None reached A.

06 The method

Firms within countries: a two-floor analysis

Debuts in the same country share the same law, economy and climate. So the research uses a multilevel regression: it separates what belongs to each firm (upper floor) from what belongs to the country (lower floor).

08 The main result

More people hit by disasters, a larger discount

The more people a country has affected by climate disasters, the larger the debut discount. For each 1% more people affected, the discount rises by 0.025 percentage points. For the author, investors in countries more exposed to the social effects of climate see more risk and ask for a larger discount.

The number of disasters pointed the other way: more disasters, a smaller discount. For the author, frequent disasters would make the market more cautious. But it is a weak hint that did not hold in the robustness tests (screen 10).

09 All the variables

Technology, size, age and auditor also move the discount

The features of the debut itself matter a lot. High-tech firms and larger offerings had larger discounts; older firms and those audited by one of the four big audit firms (the Big Four: PwC, EY, Deloitte and KPMG), smaller ones.

10 Robustness tests

Three tests, one result that holds

11 Why it matters

What changes for those who invest, go public or regulate

At the debut, investors already seem to charge for the social risk of climate. Disclosure rules and carbon scores, however, do not yet show up in the first-day price.

For investors

  • The impact of climate on a country's people enters the debut price.
  • Looking at this exposure helps make better-informed IPO decisions.
Sections 1.5 and 4.3

For firms about to go public

  • Explaining how the firm handles climate risks can reduce investors' doubts.
  • The research suggests adjusting communication to attract funds at the debut.
Section 1.5

For regulators and governments

  • The results can guide policies on disclosing environmental and climate risks.
  • The mandatory rule alone did not change the debut discount. The effect may only appear in the long run, according to the author.
Sections 1.5 and 4.3

For researchers

  • Test other measures: carbon intensity, renewable energy, sustainability committees, signs of greenwashing, heat waves, droughts and floods.
  • Also study debuts that close below the offer price.
Conclusion

The climate already enters the debut price, but through its human side: the more people hit by disasters, the larger the discount investors ask for.

12 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • Only 145 firms with a carbon score. Because the score is voluntary and rare among debutants, the full model uses 145 of the 880 debuts. With little data, it is hard to prove the score's effect. · according to the author
  • Long-term effects are left out. Environmental rules and temperature changes may act gradually and not show up in the first-day price. · according to the author
  • Association, not cause. The model shows that the discount and people affected move together, holding other variables constant; it does not prove that one causes the other. · our note
  • Only debuts with a discount. In the sample, all closed the first day above the offer price (the smallest discount was 0.07%). Debuts that fall are left for future studies. · Table 3 and conclusion

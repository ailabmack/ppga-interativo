Master’s dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2021

On its own, no feature of cash explained companies’ debt

A company that generates more cash, with fewer ups and downs and on the rise, should be able to owe more. The dissertation tested this idea in listed companies from six Latin American countries, from 2011 to 2019. Each feature on its own passed no test; only some combinations did, many of them in periods of crisis. Explained in 12 screens.

03 What is measured

Three features of cash, each read through three indicators

Cash does not come ready-made in the balance sheet. 04 What is measured

Debt was measured with six yardsticks, from the narrowest to the broadest

All of them divide something by the company’s assets. What changes is what goes on top: only loans, which charge interest, or also bills that do not, such as suppliers and wages. Net debt subtracts the cash on hand; the dissertation notes that its logic, net debt over cash generation, is the most widely used today in Brazil and abroad.

09 The unexpected

Net debt, the market’s favourite yardstick, did not show up in any result

Of the 13 results, 10 came from the two broadest yardsticks, which add up everything the company owes, including bills without interest. Only one came from the yardstick that counts loans alone. Tap a bar:

Equations in brackets in Table 9; our count. In the conclusion, the author sums up: of the six debt models, five were significant.

10 The twist

For the author, in crises companies owe more because cash runs short, not because it is plentiful

The bet was that plentiful cash makes room for debt. Reading the tests that passed, the author concludes that much of the debt came the other way round: in a crisis, cash shrinks and the company borrows to get through the squeeze. Choose an indicator:

11 So what?

Forecasting cash, and crises, weighs on deciding how much to owe

For the author, the growth and the size of cash are key pieces in the decision to take on debt, and the region’s instability changes the reason for owing.

For finance managers

  • The less cash swings, the more predictability managers have to take on debt in the market. (author)
  • The ability to anticipate economic movements weighs on capital structure decisions. (author)

For lenders and analysts

  • In crises, companies tend to increase debt out of a need for cash at that moment, not because of beneficial effects of their activity. (author)

Our reading

  • No feature of cash, on its own, explained debt, and with size and collateral in the model almost nothing passed (screens 07 and 08).
  • The result depends on the yardstick: net debt and the one that counts only loans barely show up (screen 09).
12 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • Very different countries. Although neighbours, the countries are in different economic situations, and Venezuela had no data. · according to the author
  • The past to measure the future. The growth that matters is future growth, but the model uses past data; and the indicators may not be the most precise. · according to the author
  • No effect size. The printed outputs repeat the same table, and the number of companies is not reported: the page shows only which tests passed. · our observation
  • Many tests. There were 60 test cells; with so many tests, some pass by chance, and the 13 results are scattered with no single pattern. · our observation
  • Goes together, not cause. The models show an association between cash and debt, not that one causes the other. · our observation

Next steps suggested by the author: broaden the data (more years and more countries) and look for new indicators that measure the size, swings and growth of cash more precisely.

Is the sustainability report a shop window or a mirror?

Large companies publish every year how much they trained their employees. The dissertation read 25 of these reports and listened to the people who write them: education is always a priority, but education for sustainability barely shows up, and the report rarely helps the company think about itself. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The yardstick · GRI Standard

The standard asks for hours, not for what was learned

The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) standard is the most widely used for writing these reports: according to a figure cited in the dissertation, 74% of the world’s 250 largest companies follow it. In the training and education chapter, it requires three things:

404-1Average hours of training per employee, by gender and job category.
404-2Programs to upgrade skills and for career transition (the only descriptive item).
404-3Percentage of employees receiving regular performance reviews.
not askedWhat the training is for, which topics it covers, whether it worked and whether it relates to the company’s sustainability.

Table 3 and section 6.1 of the dissertation (GRI 2020). Companies may report more voluntarily; the standard does not encourage it.

03 The concept · social learning

Learning for sustainability means revisiting assumptions together

The dissertation draws on social learning for sustainability: people learn together, from experience, and the core mechanism is reflecting, monitoring and evaluating. Its five elements:

  1. Participation and interaction: bringing the parties together so everyone speaks.
  2. Understanding the system, with partnerships and joint work.
  3. Collaborative learning platforms, in person or online.
  4. Deciding and planning with the diversity of those who live the problem.
  5. Reflection, evaluation and monitoring of the learning itself.

Mezirow’s (1991) three types of reflection, used in the dissertation. The examples are ours.

04 Method · qualitative research

25 reports, the standard and two conversations

The author, who has prepared sustainability reports for more than ten years, looked at the same chapter from three sides: what the standard asks, what the companies wrote and what the people who write the reports say. Tap each step.

07 The unexpected · focus group

The report shows less than the company does

In the focus group, education-for-sustainability activities came up at two companies whose reports did not mention them. And one participant described how a whole program vanished from the report:

“We launched an education program, an online platform, a sustainability e-learning course […] it had gamification, interactions, prizes. […] I sat down with the HR team: ‘Look, here are these hours. Add them to your main indicator and publish it in the report.’ […] Looking back, I don’t know if adding those hours to that indicator was enough. I don’t think so.”Participant S3, focus group (section 6.3.3)

The following year, the company opened a dedicated section on education for sustainability in its report.

08 Why · barriers

Whoever reads the report decides what goes in it

For the focus group, the people who actually read the report are investors. In the one-hour interview with the GRI specialist, investors were mentioned 10 times; unions, environmentalists and other audiences, at most 2. Tap a barrier.

Mentions in the interview with the GRI specialist, the author’s count (section 6.3.1).

09 The finding · a 2-hour focus group

Two hours of conversation changed how they saw the chapter

The group began by treating the chapter as accountability. Through questions and exchange, it reached a consensus that the chapter deserves reflection and should also show what is not going well. For the author, the focus group itself worked as a space for critical reflection.

Two of the strongest statements at the end came from people whose companies reported no education-for-sustainability practices in any of the five years (section 6.3.4).

11 So what?

Using the report to learn, not only to account

As the GRI specialist put it: “It is not the report itself that matters, but the transformation that the exercise of reporting brings about.”

Companies

Hold conversation circles like the focus group during the reporting cycle; ask what each training is for and what result is expected; have the courage to report what is not going well too. · according to the author

HR and leaders

Join the sustainability conversation, give a voice to all levels and not just managers, and bring the topic into existing training, such as health and safety or compliance. · according to the author and participants

GRI and standard setters

Recommend using the references the standard itself lists and cross-checking data across chapters (hours of diversity training next to diversity in leadership roles, for example). · according to the author and the GRI specialist interview

Report readers

Be wary of training hours on their own: the same number can hide very different kinds of training. It is worth asking what changed afterwards. · our reading

12 Limits · credits

What this research does not say

  • What the companies actually do. The analysis covers what was reported; the focus group itself showed there are more practices than the reports tell. · according to the author
  • That it holds for all companies. These are five large multinationals, chosen because they are recognized for sustainability, and a single chapter of their reports. · our note
  • Whether the conversation changed anything later. The author suggests going back to the companies to see whether the focus group had any effect on later practices and reports. · according to the author
  • What came after 2020. The reports go up to 2020, when the pandemic changed training; the author suggests studying the following years and conducting long-term and intervention research. · according to the author
  • How much each company learned. The four groups on screen 05 are our reading of the dissertation’s tables, not a grade given by the author. · our note

Master’s dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2018

Assets that can be pledged help a firm take on debt, but they count for less the older it is

To lend, a bank wants something it can take if the firm does not pay: inventory, machinery, buildings, receivables. The dissertation followed 198 Brazilian companies from 2010 to 2017. Among listed firms, more pledgeable assets came with more debt, but that effect shrank with the firm’s age. And older firms did not owe less, as theory expected. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The topic

To lend, the bank wants an asset it can take if the firm does not pay

That asset is the collateral. If the firm does not pay, the lender keeps it. That is why firms with more assets to offer usually get more debt.

In the firms studied, out of every R$ 100 of assets, about R$ 58 were assets that could serve as collateral, and about R$ 32 came from debt with banks and other lenders. Test yourself: what counts in the dissertation’s collateral measure?

Source: Chart 1 and Table 5 (averages for 2010 to 2017). Collateral = (inventory + net fixed assets + receivables) ÷ total assets. Debt = (short- + long-term debt) ÷ total assets. The rest of the right-hand bar combines the owners’ capital and other obligations, such as suppliers.

03 The theory

A firm changes its source of money over its life

Like people, firms are born, grow, mature and may decline. At each stage, money comes from a different place: the owner, investors, banks or the firm’s own cash.

The dissertation starts from there: a mature firm generates cash and would need less debt. But it also builds up pledgeable assets and a track record with banks. Tap a stage.

04 The hypotheses

Three bets on age, collateral and debt

The dissertation combined two ideas: the life cycle (a mature firm pays its way with its own cash) and the trade-off view (debt has a tax advantage, and pledged assets make the lender feel safer).

05 Who was in

Of 336 companies, 198 remained, two thirds of them listed on the stock exchange

The balance sheets came from S&P’s Capital IQ database, from 2010 to 2017. The sample included non-financial firms, both listed (with shares on the exchange) and privately held, from sectors such as manufacturing, energy, health, consumer goods and technology.

06 How age is measured

On the study’s scale, the early years of a firm’s life count more than the later ones

Age is the current year minus the founding year, measured as a logarithm. In practice, going from 5 to 10 years counts the same as going from 50 to 100. Doubling the age always adds the same step.

Firms in the sample were typically about 48 years old. Listed firms ranged from 2 to 147 years; private ones from 14 to 134. Move the age.

Source: Chart 1 and Table 5. The table reports the logarithm (mean, minimum and maximum); converting back to years is our calculation. The “typical age” is the mean of the logarithm converted to years.

08 The result

In the full model, collateral came with more debt, and so did age

With the other factors accounted for (profit, size, growth and intangible assets), and comparing each firm with itself over the years, the signs changed. Only the collateral bet was supported, and only among listed firms and in the full sample.

Source: Table 9 (fixed-effects panel with robust errors). p = the chance of a result like this appearing by chance; below 0.05 counts as an effect. The models explain 8% to 12% of the variation in debt (R² from 0.08 to 0.12). Verdicts as in the dissertation.

09 The finding

The older the firm, the less collateral adds to its debt

The model has a term that combines collateral and age, and it came out negative. In practice, the “push” of collateral on debt depends on age: strong in young firms, close to zero around 60 to 75 years.

The dissertation expected the opposite (collateral counting more in older firms) and suggests an explanation: over time, the quality of those assets may worsen, and the lender does not feel any safer.

Our calculation with Table 9: effect of 10 more points of collateral = 10 × (collateral coefficient + combined-term coefficient × log of age). The dissertation did not test whether this combined effect differs from zero at each age. For private firms, neither term was significant.

10 The unexpected

Age came with more debt, mainly in firms with little collateral

Life-cycle theory bet that older firms would owe less. Among listed firms, the age coefficient came out positive (0.13, p 0.004). For the dissertation, it signals credibility: a mature firm has a track record and the bank trusts it more.

Guess: two listed firms double in age. One has 20% of its assets as collateral; the other, 80%. In which one does debt rise more?

Our calculation with Table 9 (listed firms): effect of doubling age = 0.69 × (0.1341 − 0.2145 × collateral). With the combined term in the model, the age coefficient alone applies to a firm with no collateral; at the average collateral (58%), the calculation is close to zero. The combined effect was not tested.

11 Other factors

In all three groups, more profitable firms owed less, and firms that sold more owed more

Profit and size behaved as theory expects, among private firms, listed firms and the full sample. A profitable firm funds itself with its own cash; a large firm has easier access to credit.

12 So what?

Collateral opens the door to credit, but not forever

For young firms

  • It was in these firms that pledged assets came with the most debt: about 4 extra points for every 10 points of collateral at age 10. (screen 09, our calculation)
  • High profit came with less debt in every group. (screen 11)

For lenders

  • In older firms, assets counted for little: the dissertation suggests looking at their quality over time. (dissertation)
  • Age and track record seem to lend a firm credibility, especially when it has few assets. (screen 10, our reading)

For other emerging economies

  • Differences between listed and private firms can guide financing decisions with banks and in capital markets. (dissertation)

For researchers

  • Expand the sample of private firms and include other emerging economies, with each country’s economic factors. (dissertation)
  • Split by sector and use a dynamic panel. (dissertation)

What this research does not say

  • Few private firms. There were 66 (only 10 in 2017); the dissertation links the lack of effect in this group to the small sample. · according to the dissertation
  • Explains little. The models explain 8% to 12% of the variation in debt; much of what drives debt was left out. · our observation
  • Combined effect not tested. The curves on screens 09 and 10 are calculations with the coefficients; the dissertation did not test the effect at each age or collateral level. · our observation
  • Ageing and the passing years move together. Since each firm is compared with itself, its age rises one year with each calendar year; the model does not separate ageing from other changes between 2010 and 2017. · our observation
  • Association is not causation. The models show what moved together; they do not prove that buying machinery would bring more credit. · our observation

Credit ratings barely explained how much debt Brazilian firms took on

Agencies such as S&P give firms a grade, from AAA to D, on how likely they are to repay what they owe. Studies in the US say firms keep that grade in mind when they decide to borrow. The dissertation tested the idea on 74 Brazilian firms from 2000 to 2016: of the 36 measures tied to the rating, only one passed the usual test, and none of the four hypotheses held. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The topic

A credit rating is a report card on how likely a firm is to repay what it owes

The three big agencies (S&P, Fitch and Moody’s) use letters: AAA is minimal risk; D is debt already unpaid. From BBB up, a firm is investment grade; below it, speculative grade.

The + or − sign says whether the firm sits at the top or the bottom of the letter. The border matters: in regulated markets, pension funds, insurers and banks are required to invest in investment-grade firms. Tap a letter.

Source: sections 1.1 and 2.1.5 and Chart 4 (matching the S&P, Fitch and Moody’s scales). The dissertation uses S&P’s long-term rating.

03 The theory

A firm on the edge of moving up or down a notch would avoid taking on more debt

Changing rating changes the cost of money. So, according to studies of US firms (Kisgen, 2006), a firm with a + or − on its rating, close to changing notch, issues less debt: to avoid falling, or to manage to climb.

The study tags each firm, each year, with three yes-or-no flags. Pick a rating and see how it enters the measure.

Source: sections 1.1, 2.1.5.6 to 2.1.5.8 and Table 3 (variables POM, PLUS and MINUS). The text for each rating is our illustration of the theory.

04 What is measured

The question is whether, in a given year, the firm leaned towards debt or towards owners’ money

The measure is net debt issuance: how much debenture debt grew, minus how much owners’ equity grew, over total assets. Positive: the firm leaned towards debt. Negative: towards equity.

A debenture is a debt security that the firm sells to investors in the market. Move the two changes in a firm with R$ 100 of assets.

Source: Table 3 (formula) and Table 7 (averages: 674 Brazilian balance sheets; nearly 12,000 US ones, from Kemper and Rao, 2013). The simulated firm is illustrative. On average, Brazilian firms came out slightly negative: equity grew a little more than debt.

05 The hypotheses

Four bets on when the rating would matter most

The dissertation repeated in Brazil the tests of a study of US firms (Kemper and Rao, 2013), which in turn revisited Kisgen’s. Each bet says in which firms the rating should move debt the most.

06 Who was included

74 firms rated by S&P; a third of the balance sheets came from utilities

The study included non-financial firms listed on the Brazilian stock exchange (B3) that issued debentures and were rated by S&P. Data came from the Capital IQ and Bloomberg databases.

07 First look

Within investment grade, the lower the rating, the more debt; below the border, the pattern fades

The average of three firm characteristics by rating. Investment-grade firms owed less, earned more and were larger. In speculative grade, the averages jump from one notch to the next.

Watch the ratings with few balance sheets (pale bars): an average of 1 to 7 balance sheets is fragile. Switch the characteristic.

08 The comparison

Brazilian firms owed less than US firms: 21% of their capital, against 45%

The dissertation set Brazilian averages next to those of the US study it repeats. Profit and Tobin’s Q were similar; debt was not.

The dissertation’s explanation: in the US there is a large market for selling debt securities; in Brazil, firms depend more on banks. Tap a measure.

09 The result

Of the 36 measures tied to the rating, only one passed the usual bar

Across 18 regressions, the rating entered in 36 ways: having a + or −, having only +, having only −, alone or combined with the need for outside money, average market debt or growth prospects. Each square is one of them.

At the usual bar (p below 0.05), one passed. Loosening it to 0.10, three more. But with 36 attempts, chance also hits a few: draw and see.

Source: Tables 10 to 14 (p in brackets). Our count. The draw assumes 36 independent tests with no effect; it is our yardstick for chance, which the dissertation does not use.

10 The scoreboard

None of the four bets held, as in the study of US firms

The dissertation concludes that one cannot claim that credit ratings determine Brazilian firms’ debt. Kemper and Rao (2013) did not confirm any of the four in the US either.

Source: Table 15 and section 4.5. The explanations on each row are the dissertation’s.

11 What mattered

What went with debt was how indebted the firm already was, in all 18 models

Apart from the rating, leverage came out positive and clear in every model. Average market debt came out clear, but in the opposite direction to the one expected. Profit and size barely showed up.

12 So what?

In Brazil, the rating describes risk but did not seem to steer debt

For those who decide on debt

  • In the sample, being close to a rating change did not change how much a firm issued. (screens 09 and 10)
  • The dissertation notes there are other ways to look after the rating: mergers, asset sales, operational cuts. (dissertation)

For lenders

  • Investment-grade firms owed less and earned more, on average. (screen 07)
  • Three in four balance sheets were speculative grade, most of them in the BB letter, just below the border. (screen 01)

For the Brazilian market

  • The bond market is small, and firms depend on banks to fill that role. (dissertation)

For researchers

  • Test operational measures (expenses, EV/EBITDA), net debt over EBITDA and the maturity of issues. (dissertation)
  • Use other agencies’ ratings. (dissertation)

What this research does not say

  • One rating, one agency. Only S&P’s long-term rating; short-term ratings and other agencies were left out. · according to the dissertation
  • Small groups. Some tests used few balance sheets: 85 in the B letter, 70 in industrials and only 21 on the speculative side of the border. · our observation
  • Many tests, one hit. With 36 measures, one passing at 5% is what chance tends to produce. · our reading
  • Similar measures. Net issuance and leverage share similar components by construction, which helps explain the strong link between them. · according to the dissertation
  • Not finding is not proving absence. The lack of effect holds for this sample and these measures. · our observation

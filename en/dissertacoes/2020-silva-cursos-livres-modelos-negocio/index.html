Three short-course schools: the one that grows is the one that decides fast

Short vocational courses teach a trade in days or weeks, to people with little schooling who need an income. The dissertation compared a beauty school, a maintenance school and a health school through the business-model tripod: how each one creates, configures and captures value. The one that is growing launches courses almost overnight, charges a little less than its rivals and makes the most of its partnerships.

Author: Tiago Leite da Silva Advisor: Prof. Dr. Dimária Silva e Meirelles Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The problem

Short courses are the widest door into vocational education, and few people go through it

Brazil has three levels of vocational education. The short course is not regulated by the Ministry of Education (MEC), accepts any level of schooling and is offered by private schools, NGOs, churches and unions. So, in principle, it can change its syllabus as fast as the job market.

03 The research lens

A business model stands on three legs: creating, configuring and capturing value

The dissertation uses Meirelles's (2019) tripod. Creating value means spotting an opportunity and turning it into something customers want. Configuring means arranging resources, staff and partners to deliver it. Capturing (“appropriating”) means turning it into revenue. The three turn as a cycle.

    04 How the study looked

    Three schools, three long conversations and one question: faced with each dilemma, which side did they pick?

    The author chose depth over numbers: one person from each school's management was interviewed with 34 open questions, and the stories were read looking for dilemmas (“do we do this or that?”) and the side chosen. Schools and people appear without names.

    06 Creating value

    For a short-course student, value means leaving with work, and soon

    All three schools teach hands-on and change the syllabus when the market asks. The difference lies in speed and focus: the Beauty School has one-day courses and treats placing its students as the main goal. Students leave hired by salons or already serving their first clients through an app.

    For the author, other schools look at what matters to them (board approval, well-edited materials); students want income and a job.

    07 Configuring value

    In the lean school, whoever listens to the student also sells and teaches

    At the Beauty School, the teachers themselves answer the phone, sell the course and teach it, earning 30% of the course plus a 10% commission, and the team says how much it wants to earn to set the target. What students ask for reaches the decision-maker at once. At the other two, a new course goes through management, hiring specialists and preparing materials.

    Tables 7, 10, 13 and 16 and section 6.4.2. All three have partners; only the Beauty School makes the most of them (it recommends its best students to salons, trains with suppliers' equipment and promotes their brands).

    08 Capturing value · the surprise

    The school that delivers most charges a little less than its rivals

    You might expect whoever delivers more to charge more. The Beauty School does the opposite: it prices slightly below the market. For the author, it understands its students' situation: women without income or changing careers, who compare prices before deciding. With everything else similar, the lower price tips the choice.

    To lower the price without losing money, it goes straight to practice, with no “padding”. The Health Institute, by contrast, wants to be “the best school” in its area, not the cheapest.

    09 The finding

    Across the tripod's eight pillars, the Beauty School is ahead, and only it plans to open a new branch

    The author compared the schools pillar by pillar (Tables 15 to 17), calling this a “pseudo-scale”. We turned the wording into three heights. The Beauty School had firm plans to open another branch in 2020; according to the author, the other two have seen revenue fall year after year.

    10 What the study concludes

    Five practices keep value turning: the more the school creates, the more it can earn

    For the author, the Beauty School lives Meirelles's value cycle: creating, configuring and capturing feed one another. The first course comes out fast, like a startup's minimum viable product; polished materials and new partnerships come on the next turn.

    11 So what?

    A short-course school is worth the job its students get

    School owners and managersThe author recommends a flexible structure, launching courses without delay, focusing on getting students into work, pricing for people who compare prices, and partnerships used to the full (commercial, teaching and job placement).
    Workforce training bodiesOUR READING Public programmes and NGOs that buy or run courses could ask how many students left with work, not only how many graduated.
    ResearchThe author suggests studying other schools and sectors and building a scale to measure how much each school creates, configures and captures value.

    What the study does not say

    • Three schools do not represent the sector. The results cannot be generalised. (author)
    • One voice per school, and different voices. The interviewees differ in role, schooling and tenure, and the schools are in different niches; this may shape the answers. (author)
    • Revenue reported, not measured. Growth and decline come from what the schools said; the dissertation has no revenue series. (our note)
    • Students were not measured. How many students leave with a job at each school was not measured. (our note)

Master's dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

Family-run baby shops watch the market by talking, with no system, and change their stores based on what they hear

The author interviewed managers of six small and mid-sized baby shops in different regions of Brazil. None has a system to gather and analyse information about the market and competitors; it arrives through trade fairs, suppliers and Sebrae and is discussed in meetings. This information is what led the shops to grow, change their service and plan to sell online: changes to the business model.

02 The market studied

Baby products sell to newcomers, and the customer changes with every generation

Childcare retail means baby shops for children aged 0 to 3: dummies, bottles, clothes, toys, strollers, the whole nursery. The dissertation notes that the sector is highly competitive and that today's customer compares prices on the phone, wants convenience and buys through several channels.

03 The question

Does knowing what the market is doing help a shop change the way it makes money?

The dissertation brings two ideas together. Competitive intelligence is the process of gathering, analysing and spreading information about competitors and the market to make better decisions. A business model is the logic of how a company creates value for customers, organises its activities to deliver it and keeps part of it (profit).

Tap a step. Our summary of the dissertation's literature review (sections 2.1 and 2.2: Gomes and Braga, SCIP, Teece, Osterwalder and Pigneur).

04 How the study looked

Six conversations with the people who run the shops: half founders, half successors

Between September and October 2019, the author visited six family-run baby shops in person, each at least 8 years old and with a physical store, in cities in different regions. He used a script of 15 questions; the first interview served as a test. In two shops, two managers answered together.

05 What the interviews show

Six steps: from looking for information to the customer who recognises the shop

The author marked the key passages in the interviews, grouped similar ones into 17 ideas (units of meaning) and the ideas into six categories. They follow the study's aims: adopting competitive intelligence, using it in decisions, changing the business model with it and, finally, creating, configuring and capturing value.

06 Aims 1 and 2 · sources

The trade fair is the source that comes up most: five of the six shops mention it

When they explain how they learn about the market, the managers talk about trade fairs and conferences, Sebrae (the Brazilian support service for small businesses), suppliers, a market-research firm for toys and baby goods (NPD), websites, visits to competitors and conversations with customers, staff and shopkeepers in other cities. For the author, the sources are both formal and informal.

07 Aim 1 · how they adopt it

None of the six has a system: information is weighed by the manager's experience and settled in meetings

Everyone considers information important (“Information is everything”, said two shops). But no shop has a formal process or software to analyse it, and each looks for information at its own pace, from every six months to every day. Decisions come out of board meetings, meetings with supervisors or talks between partners. For the author, this is informal competitive intelligence, “based on each manager's sensitivity”.

Quotes from units US2 (processing), US8 (frequency) and US5 (decision), section 4.1.4, categories CT01 and CT02. The three-line summary is our reading of the quotes.

08 Aim 3 · the business model

Watching competitors, the shops got bigger, more comfortable and better stocked

Asked what had changed in their business, all six managers describe changes made because of what they saw or heard: play areas, bigger and fuller stores, a new store in a mall, merging furniture and baby goods. Tap each shop.

Unit US10, section 4.1.4 (category CT03). The floor plan shows only the case with figures in the dissertation (E6: from 300 to 1,000 m²), drawn to scale by area; the other changes have no measurements.

09 The change everyone mentions

In 2019, all six shops talked about selling online, but only one said it had already started

Customers walk in with a phone in hand, “dictating the price and the number of instalments”, because a competitor already sells online. The managers see marketplaces (virtual malls with many stores) and omnichannel, which links the physical store and the internet, such as buying online and picking up in store. The dissertation highlights this move as one of the main changes to the business models.

10 Aim 4 · the answer

Information shows up in all three legs of the business model: service, supplier partnerships and local reputation

The author concludes that there are signs that competitive intelligence helped develop these shops' business models: it helps create value, configure the activities that deliver it and capture it, when customers recognise the shop. Tap each leg.

11 So what?

The small shop already uses competitive intelligence, even without a method

Shop ownersThe author says the findings can help managers in this and other sectors who want to use competitive intelligence in their decisions. Our reading: writing down what is heard at fairs and from suppliers and reviewing it at scheduled meetings would already be a first step.
Small-business support bodiesSebrae comes up in the interviews as a source of trends, consulting and even an innovation programme that, according to one shop, changed the business. For firms with little structure, this support is part of their radar.
SuppliersSupplier partnerships come up in all six quotes about configuring value: they bring information, train sales staff and help offer exclusive products.

What the research does not say

  • Six shops. Small and mid-sized, family-run; the findings are typical of them and do not apply to the whole sector. (author)
  • Signs, not a measured effect. The dissertation does not compare shops that use information with shops that do not, nor does it measure sales or profit. (our observation)
  • What they say. The changes and their causes come from the managers' own accounts, without documents or data from the shops. (our observation)
  • A 2019 snapshot. The interviews took place before the pandemic changed retail. (our observation)

Next steps suggested by the author: interview more retailers, compare small and mid-sized shops with the large baby-store chains and run quantitative studies that measure the categories.

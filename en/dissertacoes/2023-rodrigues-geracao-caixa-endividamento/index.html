Do firms that generate more cash borrow more?

The dissertation followed 757 listed companies from six Latin American countries, from 2017 to 2021, to see whether the size, the instability and the growth of their cash change how much they owe, and whether Covid-19 shook that up. In 12 screens.

02 The problem

Owing money has a good side and a bad side for a company

Interest on debt lowers the tax bill: that is the good side. But the more a company owes, the higher the risk of not being able to pay: that is the bad side. According to trade-off theory, there is a dose of debt that leaves the company at its most valuable point. Drag and look for that point.

03 The question

Two theories, opposite predictions

Think of someone with a high salary. Does the bank trust them and lend more? Or do they not even need a loan, because they pay cash? For companies, both answers have a theory behind them. Switch the theory and watch the arrows flip.

Table (Quadro) 4 of the dissertation. The earlier studies gathered in Quadro 5 found both directions, and cash growth had hardly been tested. The dissertation's hypotheses follow trade-off theory.

04 Measuring cash

A company's cash can be read in three ways

Following Carleton and Silberman (1977), the dissertation looks at how big cash generation is, how much it swings and how much it grows. As a stand-in for cash, it uses operating profit (before interest and taxes) and the value the stock market gives the company. Pick an attribute.

05 Measuring debt

Debt was measured with four yardsticks

Gross is everything the company owes; net subtracts the cash on hand, which works like debt in reverse. Total adds short and long term; long term keeps only what falls due after one year. Tap a block.

Quadro 6 and Table 2 (averages for 2017–2019 and 2020–2021, as a fraction of assets). The averages barely moved with the pandemic. The net long-term average is negative: on average, cash exceeded long-term debt.

06 Who was studied

757 companies from six countries, almost 4 in 10 from Brazil

Six countries hold 92% of the companies listed on Latin American stock exchanges. Banks and insurers were left out, and so were firms with negative equity (owing more than they had). The balance sheets came from the S&P Capital IQ database.

Table 1 (listed) and section 3.1 (final sample, without negative equity). The six sample counts add up to 757 (checked); in Mexico and Colombia, the final sample has more companies than Table 1 lists, which the dissertation does not explain. Tap a bar.

07 The shock

Covid knocked down the region's GDP, but average debt barely moved

In 2020, Latin America's economy shrank 6.6%, twice the global drop. The dissertation split 2017–2019 (before) from 2020–2021 (pandemic) and compared them. Pick a period and see the averages.

Figure 1 (World Bank data) and Table 2. In the table, the right column is the difference between periods; highlighted, the rows that changed the most. GDP and inflation are averages of the six countries, in %; returns and debt, in % of assets.

08 The results · place your bet

The dissertation bet on trade-off theory

Each company was compared with itself over the years (fixed effects), accounting for size, fixed assets, sales, GDP, inflation and interest rates. There were 36 regressions: 3 indicators × 4 yardsticks × 3 periods. Before you look, bet: were the hypotheses confirmed?

12 In practice · limits · credits

What changes for those who decide how much to owe

Finance directors

The author recommends considering cash generation and its three attributes when deciding how much debt to carry, especially in crises. The results show the answer depends on which debt one looks at.

Credit analysts

Our reading: gross debt and net debt (cash subtracted) told opposite stories for the same companies. A single yardstick can mislead.

In times of crisis

In the pandemic, the interest rate stopped explaining debt. For the author, in a crisis companies borrow to keep the business alive, often with no alternative.

Researchers

Suggested next steps: more years after the pandemic and analysis by sector, since each industry manages debt in its own way.

What this research does not say

  • Little pandemic time. The post-Covid period has only two years (2020 and 2021), and companies were not split by sector. · according to the author
  • Yardsticks with odd averages. In Table 2, gross total debt has a lower average than long-term debt and reaches negative values, which the formulas in Quadro 6 do not allow. That is why this page shows only the direction of the results, not their size. · our note
  • Many tests. With 108 results at 10%, about 1 in 10 would come out "significant" by chance. There were 55 (45 at 5%): well above chance, but each block on its own calls for caution. · our count
  • Cash by proxy. Cash was measured through operating profit and market value, not the cash flow itself. The three indicators gave different results from one another. · our note

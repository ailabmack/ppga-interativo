How national culture weighs on the decision to invest in clinical trials

Before a new medicine reaches the pharmacy, it is tested on people, often by contracted research centres. The dissertation heard from four of these Brazilian centres about one investment decision each and asks: does the country's way of doing things help or hinder these decisions? 05 Method · sections 4 and 5.2

Four research centres opened up one decision each

It is a multiple case study: a few cases, looked at closely and then compared. The author looked for Brazilian-owned clinical research centres that had made a strategic investment in the previous four years.

09 Proposition 1 · section 5.8.1 and Table 20

Wanting to do better sparked all four decisions

For the author, three cultural practices make managers feel the gap between where the company is and where it should be, a discomfort theory calls cognitive dissonance. That discomfort is what triggers the investment decision, and more strongly when all three appear together. Switch the practices on or pick a centre.

10 Proposition 2 · section 5.8.1 and Table 21

At the regulators, the country's culture turns into waiting and uncertainty

On the other side, the author points to two practices that, applied by the bodies that approve trials, make the environment uncertain: institutional collectivism (the collective protected by rules and institutions) and power distance (those on top are not accountable to those below). The result is slow approval and a lost chance to launch first. Send a protocol and pick a centre.

12 So what?

The culture that sparks the decision inside the company can also hold it back outside

For the author, decision theory gains from looking at national culture to understand where the push to act comes from, and culture theory gains from looking at industry rules and at how decision-makers think.

For research centre managers

  • Encourage a culture that values performance, looking ahead and acting firmly, which generates the push to grow. (author)
  • Plan for the wait for approvals and imports, which delayed decisions in the cases. (our reading)

For regulators

  • Get closer to research centres and drug companies to understand their needs. (author)
  • Make rules and processes more efficient, for a more stable regulatory environment. (author)

For those who study decisions and culture

  • The GLOBE traits can serve as a lens to study decisions case by case. (author)
  • Culture still needs to be studied in multinationals' decisions and in the value they create in their home countries. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Four centres. The propositions are tentative, to be confirmed in studies with more cases. · according to the author
  • One voice per centre. Each decision was told by one person; the regulators were not heard, and the delay is the view of those who wait. · our note
  • Culture read, not measured. The traits were identified in what people said; the GLOBE scores are country scores from another study, not the companies'. · our note
  • No comparison. There are no decisions from other countries, nor decisions without these traits, to compare; the link between culture and decision is a reading of the cases. · our note

Do people who work at the till and the counter innovate too?

The dissertation surveyed 147 employees, almost all of them front-line staff, at a supermarket chain and a shoe-store chain in Foz do Iguaçu, Brazil: how they absorb what is new, how they learn and how innovative they see themselves. Explained in 10 screens.

02 The question · three measures per person

Absorbing the new, learning and innovating: does learning sit in the middle?

The idea of “absorptive capacity” was born for companies: recognising the value of outside knowledge, assimilating it and using it. Few studies measured it in each person, and almost none looked beyond knowing to feeling and doing. Pick a link:

07 The unexpected · inside innovating

Nearly eight in ten find it exciting to be original, yet more than half distrust new inventions

In the same group of 147 people, most say they like new ideas, and more than half also say they wait for others to try them first. See the statements, then what weighs in the innovating score the model builds:

Table 4 (agree, n = 147) and Table 15 (weight of each part in the innovating measure, 0 to 1). Our reading: the “willingness to try” statements are about caution, and agreeing with them signals more caution; it is the part that moves least with the others.

10 So what?

In the store, those who absorb the new well also see themselves as more innovative, and part of that runs through how they learn

The author concludes that learning, with its three ways, is part of the path between absorbing and innovating. The suggestions below are our reading of the results.

For store managers

  • Sharing what one knows with colleagues was among the most accepted statements (78.2%): short sharing huddles in the store build on that.
  • More than half wait to see others use something before trying it: showing someone using the new thing helps.

For those who train teams

  • Training is not just passing on information: attitude (feeling) and practice (doing) also count in the learning score.
  • Doing had the lowest agreement: hands-on practice is worth more than explanation alone.

For research

  • Use projective techniques (talking about a colleague, not oneself), as the author suggests.
  • Test the path through learning with a dedicated test of the indirect effect (our note).

What this research does not say

  • The researcher did not hand out the questionnaires. By agreement with the chains, two of their employees ran the collection. · according to the author
  • Answers may have been cautious. Out of fear, people may not have answered fully frankly; the real scores may be lower, without changing the structure of the model. · according to the author
  • Two chains, one city. 147 answers from 11 stores in Foz do Iguaçu; they do not represent Brazilian retail. · our note
  • The path through learning was not tested on its own, and moving together is not cause. The dissertation compares the direct link before and after; each person rated themselves on all three measures. · our note

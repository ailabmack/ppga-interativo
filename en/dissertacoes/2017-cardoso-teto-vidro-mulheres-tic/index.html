Working among men did not change what women in tech think of the glass ceiling

The glass ceiling is the invisible barrier that stalls women's rise in companies. The research surveyed 233 women at two technology multinationals. Being on a mostly male team, in a technical area or having studied science or engineering made no difference to their answers, and most believe the glass can be broken through.

02 The problem

Technology recruits where there are few women

Technology companies hire mostly people with science and engineering degrees, courses where men are the majority. The literature suggests that living in a male environment reinforces in women the idea that they can rise less. The dissertation's question: does living among a male majority, at university and at work, change women's attitude towards the glass ceiling?

03 The concept

There are four ways to react to the glass ceiling

The dissertation uses a model by Australian researchers (Smith, Crittenden and Caputi, 2012). Two attitudes are more favourable to a career, because the woman keeps pursuing promotion; two are less favourable, because she stops pursuing it.

Keeps pursuing promotionDoes not pursue promotion Sees the barrier Does not see it

The 2 × 2 grid is our arrangement of the model's definitions (section 2.4). In acceptance, the woman does not seek promotion because she is satisfied or prefers another goal, such as family; the definition does not say whether she sees the ceiling. The phrases in quotes are our summary.

07 What it found

Almost all believe women can overcome the barrier

The resilience statements drew 79% to 94% agreement. In the denial ones, which state that the barrier exists, most disagreed: 80% do not think it will take decades for equality at the top. Resignation drew more disagreement, and acceptance was split, with family weighing in: 57% think women prefer a balance between family and work to a high salary.

08 The finding

None of the three male environments changed the attitudes

Women on male-majority teams, in technical areas or with science and engineering degrees answered like the others. Together, the three variables explain practically nothing of the four attitudes, and none of the hypotheses was confirmed.

09 The unexpected

What split the answers was job level, not environment

Among personal and career traits, job level changed denial and resignation, and education changed resignation. Being a mother was borderline for acceptance. Age, time in the role and being in a stable relationship made no difference.

10 Possible explanations

The barrier exists, even when women say it can be crossed

11 So what?

Listen to women before assuming what they think

Technology companiesThe author proposes using the questionnaire to diagnose what female employees think about their chances of promotion.
HR and diversityThe optimism of the answers does not erase the barriers: for the author, the discussion about equality and prejudice in the sector is still needed.
LeadersJob level split the answers; it is worth also listening to those who have not reached management, not only those who have risen. (our reading)
ResearchersTest the 18 statements that remained, hear more directors and executives, other sectors and men too.

What the research does not say

  • Two companies. They are women from two multinationals with diversity programmes; other companies may differ. (author)
  • A fragile yardstick. Only 18 of the 38 statements held up, and even they fell slightly short of the usual consistency criterion. (author)
  • Few at the top. Only 16 of the 233 are directors or executives. (author)
  • Opinion, not career path. The research measures what women think about women, not whether they were promoted. (our observation)
  • No men. Only women answered; there is no comparison with men's views. (author)

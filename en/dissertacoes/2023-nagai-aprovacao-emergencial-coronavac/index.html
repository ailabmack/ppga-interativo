What weighed when Anvisa cleared CoronaVac

On 17 January 2021, in the middle of the pandemic, the board of Anvisa, Brazil’s health regulator, authorised emergency use of the CoronaVac vaccine. The dissertation interviewed five of the agency’s top managers and asks what, in the context of that crisis, changed the way the decision was made. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The problem

The vaccine was one of many urgent decisions at Anvisa

With more than a thousand deaths a day in Brazil, the agency that approves medicines and controls ports and airports had to decide everything at once: ventilators, masks, hand sanitiser, intubation drugs, borders. The managers interviewed compare the moment to a war. Tap the numbers from the first 500 days:

03 The concept

Authorising is the last step of a strategic decision

A classic study (Mintzberg and colleagues, 1976) looked at 25 major decisions and always found three phases: identifying the problem, developing solutions and selecting one. This research looks only at the last step, authorisation: when those who studied the case lack the power to commit the organisation and need sign-off from those who have it. Tap a step:

Phases and routines of the Mintzberg, Raisinghani and Théorêt (1976) model, as the dissertation summarises them (sections 2.1 and 2.2). Step height shows order only.

04 How it works

In the emergency, the decision moved up from the technical area to the collegiate board

In the ordinary routine, the registration of a biological medicine is authorised by the technical area (the general management for medicines); the collegiate board, the agency’s top body, only steps in when there is disagreement. For the first Covid vaccines, an emergency authorisation was created and the decision moved to the board, in a public session. Compare:

Ordinary routine: Figure 4 and section 2.3. Emergency: section 2.3.2 (resolution RDC 348/2020) and statements by E4 and E5 (sections 4.1.4 and 4.1.3). The agency’s deadlines are those set in the rules; the 7 working days and the 11 people are accounts given in interviews.

05 How the research looked

Five top managers told, in about five hours, what it was like

Six people directly involved in the authorisation were invited and five agreed: three from the collegiate board (then or earlier) and two from the technical registration area. The video interviews took place from September to November 2022. Together with rules, minutes and the agency’s report, they made up 20 documents, in which the author tagged 407 passages by theme, with the help of software (ATLAS.ti). Tap a cube:

06 How the research looked

Four lenses to see the context of a decision

A recent review (Elbanna, Thanos and Jansen, 2020) groups what surrounds a strategic decision into four contextual factors: who decides, the decision itself, the outside environment and the organisation. The author sorted the interview passages by these lenses and their parts. Pick a lens:

10 The unexpected

Haste had a limit: the vaccine could jump the queue, but the lab could not be rushed

More people, daily meetings and top priority cut the wait, but the safety and efficacy flow stayed the same, and the time lab tests take does not change. And someone paid the price: the requests left in the queue. Drag the control:

11 So what?

In a crisis, deciding fast depends on united leadership, focus and people who are looked after

The author’s conclusion: the steps that guarantee safety and efficacy stayed the same; what changed was creating and legitimising processes, allocating people and prioritising. And those who decide are still people, with fear, fatigue and families.

For regulatory agencies

  • Keep what worked in the crisis (leaner flows, trust in qualified companies) with rules that guarantee safety. (managers interviewed)
  • Have an emergency manual: in the pandemic it had to be created on the spot. (author)

For those leading in a crisis

  • Align the top team, decide what comes first and put the right people at each stage. (author)
  • Explain decisions in public when there is no certainty, and look after the health of those doing the work. (author)

For researchers

  • Listen to the civil servants who lived through the same process. (author)
  • Check whether the same lenses weigh the same in decisions outside a crisis and in other sectors. (author)

What this research does not say

  • Top level only. Directors and managers were heard; the views of civil servants and companies were left out. · according to the author
  • One case, five people. The case study serves to discuss theory, not to generalise to other agencies or decisions. · author and our observation
  • Memory, not records. The interviews took place 20 to 22 months after the approval, relying on the memory of those who decided. · our observation
  • Counting passages is not measuring weight. The number of passages shows what was talked about most, not how much each factor mattered. · our observation

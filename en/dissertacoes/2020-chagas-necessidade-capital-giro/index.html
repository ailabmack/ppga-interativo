What makes a company need more money to keep running?

Before customers pay, a company has already paid its suppliers and filled its stockroom. That gap has to be financed: it is the working capital requirement. The dissertation measured this requirement in five ways for 206 companies listed on the Brazilian stock exchange (B3), from 2008 to 2018, and tested ten factors that might explain it. 02 The idea

On average, a company finances 104 days of operations before it gets paid

Add the days goods sit in inventory and the days until the customer pays; subtract the days of credit the supplier gives. Across the 206 companies, from 2008 to 2018, the average result was 104 days.

During those days, the money comes from cash, from the owners or from loans. The average hides a lot of variation: values ranged from −951 to 971 days. When the number is negative, the supplier is financing the operation.

Mean, minimum and maximum from Table 4 (requirement in days = inventory days + collection days − payment days). Our simplified calculation for the amount: treats the days as days of sales (annual sales ÷ 365 × days). The negative example (30 + 20 − 80) is illustrative.

03 How the study measured

The same need, measured five ways

Companies differ: a manufacturer holds a lot of inventory, a retailer barely sells on credit. So the author did not stick to a single measure. Pick a yardstick and see which balance-sheet accounts go into it.

Formulas from section 3.4.1 (equations 1 to 8). Block heights are illustrative. Four yardsticks divide the result by the year's net revenue, to compare companies of different sizes; the days yardstick uses average periods.

05 What was tested

Ten factors, each with a guess before the test

Inspired by a study of US companies (Hill, Kelly and Highfield, 2010), the author chose ten factors and wrote down, before running the numbers, whether each should increase ▲ or decrease ▼ the working capital requirement.

Table 2 and section 3.4.2. Factors use the previous year's data (except sales growth), to see the effect of a decision on the following year.

06 The finding

The answer depends on which yardstick you use

No factor moved all five yardsticks. Rising sales and operating cash flow pushed some up and another down. Five of the nine factors measured in the main model left no trace.

Model

Each company against itself (fixed effects): compares a company's own years and removes what is fixed about it, such as its line of business. The statistical tests chose this model. All pooled: compares all companies and years at once.

07 The unexpected

Selling more lengthens the cycle and shrinks the broad yardstick

In the main model, sales growth came with more days to finance and a requirement above the sector's, but with the broad yardstick (all current items) lower. With operating cash flow, the yardstick that includes cash fell.

08 Market power and size

Companies with a bigger slice of sector sales have fewer days to finance

Market power is the company's share of its sector's sales. Bigger players negotiate better terms with customers and suppliers, the author notes. On the days yardstick, the relationship was negative, as he expected.

Size (market value) showed up on one yardstick only, the broad one, and upwards: larger companies keep more current assets relative to sales, in both models.

Our calculation. Starts from the 104-day average (Table 4) and applies the market-power coefficient on the days yardstick: −164.73 days per 100% share in the main model (only at 10%, Table 9) and −119.40 with all pooled (at 1%, Table 8). Average share: 11%. Shows association, not causation.

09 All pooled × each against itself

High governance came with 96 more days, but only with all companies pooled

The guess was that good governance would lower the requirement. With all companies pooled, those in the Novo Mercado and Level 2 segments had, on the contrary, about 96 more days to finance. In the each-company-against-itself model this factor cannot be measured, because it barely changes from year to year.

Three other factors only show up with all companies pooled. Compare the two models on the days yardstick:

10 The size of the answer

The ten factors explain at most a quarter of the story

R² shows how much of the variation in each yardstick the factors can explain. It ranged from 4% to 26%, in line with similar studies cited by the author. The rest depends on other things.

The simplest yardstick, the firm one, responded the least: in the main model, the set of factors had no statistical effect on it.

Overall R² from Table 9 and R² from Table 8. Joint F test (Prob > F) in the main model: 0.1518 on the firm yardstick (no effect); 0.0000 to 0.0289 on the others.

11 In practice, limits and credits

Purchasing, production, sales and finance jointly decide working capital

Finance managers

Look at the requirement through more than one yardstick: what improves the cycle can worsen current-asset slack. The author argues for broad decisions that weigh the consequences.

Sales targets

Growing sales came with more days to finance. For the author, aggressive targets call for thinking at the same time about the mix of cash and credit sales and the cost of financing those days.

Smaller players in a sector

Companies with a smaller share of sector sales had more days to finance (a weak signal in the main model). Our reading: negotiating terms with suppliers matters more for them.

Researchers

The debt measures the author built and B3 governance remain leads: they appear only with all companies pooled and call for further testing.

What this research does not say

  • Only Brazilian listed companies. Private companies, other countries and quarterly data may show something else. · according to the author
  • Association, not causation. The models show what moves together, and explain at most a quarter of the variation. · our note
  • Weak signals and model-dependent results. Market power is only significant at 10% in the main model; governance and long-term debt appear only when all are pooled. · our note
  • Averages pulled by extremes. Days to finance range from −951 to 971; the 104-day mean does not describe a typical company. · our note

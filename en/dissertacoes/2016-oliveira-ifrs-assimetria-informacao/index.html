Master’s dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2016

After international accounting standards became mandatory, the “toll” for trading shares fell

Anyone buying a share knows less about the company than those who run it. That gap shows up in the distance between the price buyers offer and the price sellers ask. The dissertation followed 48 large companies in Brazil’s Ibovespa index from 2005 to 2014 and asked whether three ways of opening up the books shorten that distance. Only one clearly did. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The topic

Between the price a buyer offers and the price a seller asks there is a gap: the spread

On the exchange, the bid is the most a buyer will pay; the ask, the least a seller will accept. Anyone who wants to trade right away pays that difference, like a toll.

When traders suspect the other side knows more, the gap widens. That is why the dissertation uses the spread as a measure of information asymmetry: how much less outsiders know than insiders. Switch the scenario.

03 The theory

In theory, clearer accounts pull a chain that ends in more investment

Investors who do not know what a company is hiding protect themselves: they pay less for the share. A company that sells shares at a discount pays more for its shareholders’ money.

Tap each link. The dissertation measured the links in red; the others come from the theory it reviews.

Source: sections 1.1, 2.3, 2.7 and 2.11 (Akerlof, 1970; Leuz and Verrecchia, 2000; Easley and O’Hara, 2004).

04 What was tested

Three ways for a company to open its books to investors

One came by law: international accounting standards (IFRS), mandatory in Brazil from the 2010 financial statements. The other two are the company’s choice: joining a governance tier of the Brazilian exchange or having shares traded in New York.

For each one, the dissertation bet that the spread would shrink. Pick one.

Source: sections 1.1, 1.3, 2.9 and 2.10, Box 1 (BM&FBovespa, 2015; CVM, 2014) and Box 3.

05 How it is measured

You can estimate the spread just by watching the daily back-and-forth of prices

With no news, a trade closes sometimes at the bid, sometimes at the ask, and the price zigzags up and down. Richard Roll (1984) showed that this back-and-forth reveals the size of the spread.

The dissertation’s calculation splits the returns of odd and even days, measures how much they move in opposite directions (covariance) and turns that into a spread: 2 × √(−covariance). See the dissertation’s example or draw a year of trades.

Source: section 2.11, equations 7 and 8, Figure 1 and Table 1 (the dissertation’s hypothetical example). The draw is our own simulation: the “true” price stays put and each trade closes, at random, at the bid or the ask. 07 First look

On average, the toll was 1.41% of the share price

But it varied a lot: from less than a tenth of a point to over 7%. The companies were large (average assets of R$ 33 billion), nearly all profitable, with about half their shares in the market’s hands.

09 The result

With everything in one model, the IFRS era took 0.65 points off the spread

The regression compares companies and years while accounting for size, free float, risk and profit at once. In the IFRS years the spread was 0.65 percentage points lower. Being in a governance tier went the other way: 0.22 points higher. Having an ADR made no clear difference.

11 The scoreboard

Two of the seven bets held; the governance one came out the other way

The surprise: companies in the governance tiers, which promise rules beyond the law, had a slightly larger spread, not a smaller one. For the dissertation, the market may not perceive or recognise a benefit from belonging to these segments.

The other four had the expected sign, but an effect that cannot be told apart from zero. Tap a row.

12 So what?

The transparency that came by law was the one that showed up in prices

For companies

  • The gain came from a rule set by government and regulators; the dissertation recommends also valuing voluntary transparency. (dissertation)
  • Cheaper capital lowers the rate used to assess projects, so more projects become worthwhile. (dissertation, section 1.1)

For regulators

  • In the mandatory-IFRS years, large companies’ spread was smaller, even after accounting for size, risk and profit. (screen 09)

For the exchange and investors

  • Belonging to the Novo Mercado, N1 or N2 did not go with a smaller spread in this sample. (screens 09 and 11)
  • Riskier shares had a larger spread. (screen 09)

For researchers

  • Test other asymmetry measures, such as PIN, and how detailed reports are. (dissertation)
  • Include regulators’ enforcement, other countries and all listed companies. (dissertation)

What this research does not say

  • The cost of capital was not measured. The spread measures one of its components; the link to the cost of shareholders’ money comes from theory. · according to the dissertation
  • “IFRS” means “2010 onward”. All the companies switched together, so the model cannot separate the standard from other market changes between the two periods. · our reading
  • Large companies only. 48 Ibovespa companies; smaller, less-traded ones were left out. · according to the dissertation
  • Model assumptions. Residuals did not follow the normal curve and there was autocorrelation; standard errors were corrected for unequal variance. · according to the dissertation
  • Association, not proof of cause. The study shows that spread and transparency moved together in this sample. · our note

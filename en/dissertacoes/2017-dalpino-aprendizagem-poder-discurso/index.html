Learning from colleagues is also a contest over who has the floor

For five months, a researcher sat in the room of five analysts at a technology multinational and wrote down what they said to one another. They learn all the time by talking, but in those conversations they also test their strength: whoever knows more gains a voice, and whoever has a voice decides what the group will learn. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The question

Much of what people learn at work never goes through a course

Companies usually look after courses and programmes, which can be planned and measured. But much learning happens in a chat with a colleague, a doubt settled in the corridor. The dissertation wants to understand this everyday learning and, within it, the power struggles between colleagues of the same rank. Click the five traits of informal learning (Marsick and Volpe, 1999) to see each one in the observed room.

Formalcourses, corporate programmes, indicators; usually prescriptive
Informal and collaborativeconversations, help between colleagues, quick meetings; barely studied in Brazil, the author says

The five traits come from Marsick and Volpe (1999), cited in the introduction. The examples were chosen by us from the dissertation’s scenes. In a 2006–2016 search (ANPAD events, the Brazilian thesis library BDTD and journals ranked A1 to B2 by CAPES, the federal agency for graduate education), the author found no Brazilian study combining informal learning, collaborative learning and power relations.

03 How the research looked

Five months sitting in the room, listening and taking notes

It was non-participant observation: the researcher did not work with the group or join the conversations. The researcher went three mornings a week, 9 a.m. to noon, from December 2016 to April 2017, and noted every conversation sparked by a project problem. Sometimes the researcher started an informal chat to understand a choice. The team supports customer service (the call centre): it anticipates problems and prepares material and training for the agents at each product launch.

5analysts observed, same level, no fixed boss

Illustrative calendar: the dissertation says “three times a week”, not which days. Each project has a coordinator who changes every 2 to 4 months (a “circular organisation chart”).

    The notes were then read through French Discourse Analysis (Maingueneau): beyond what is said, what matters is where each person speaks from, to whom and on whose behalf (screen 5).

    05 The reading method

    The conversations are read as theatre scenes

    The author organises the observations into five scenes, each with the elements of a play. The question is not only “what was said” but what role each person takes on when speaking. One clue is the words that point to who is speaking, where and when (“I”, “we”, “here”, “now”), which Discourse Analysis calls deixis.

    S2“I talk to everyone, actually, the company encourages this practice of talking and constantly swapping ideas, I even think that’s why we are what we are (...) we know that we learn and grow better through dialogue, through constant conversation.”

    S2’s words in an informal chat with the researcher (scene 1, section 4.1). Translated by us.

    06 Scene 1 · discursive practices

    In what they say, it is “the company” that asks for collaboration

    All five say they learn by talking with colleagues. But when explaining why, they almost always cite a third party: “the company asks”, “they want it”. The author calls this third party a hyper-enunciator: a collective voice that makes the sentence unquestionable. And the same voice separates what counts from what does not.         08 Scenes 3 and 4 · symbolic power

        To push an idea through without a boss, S1 went looking for “votes”

        S1 wanted to create an online support community, inspired by those in the US, but “people aren’t buying the idea”. With no hierarchy to impose it, the way out was to win over one colleague at a time. The author reads this as political, campaign-style discourse: “that’s one vote I already have”.         09 Scene 4 · uses of power

        With no rank above the others, power comes from knowing and persuading

        The dissertation uses two ways of exercising power (Warschauer, 2017). In domination, whoever is higher on the org chart is in charge. In influence, someone changes decisions, and even what others learn, without formal authority, and knowledge is one of its strongest sources. In this group, with no fixed boss, almost everything ran through influence. And power was not only negative: S1’s and S5’s influence opened new learning for everyone.

        10 Scene 5 · field and habitus

        Technical things are learned in courses; persuading only by trying and listening

        For the analysts, collaborating became a condition for surviving in the job: “the specialist no longer has a place here”. Drawing on Bourdieu, the author calls this the habitus of collaboration: a way of acting that the place teaches and demands. And what is learned in the conversations is not only the project’s content but also the invisible power relations. Pick a voice.

        11 The central result

        To learn and hold their ground in the group, they speak like a scientist, a teacher or a politician

        The author’s conclusion: collaboration starts from practical needs of the work, but in the conversations the analysts seek symbolic power, and this contest decides what can and should be learned there. To do so, they borrow three ways of speaking. Guess which one each sentence draws on.

        12 So what?

        Anyone who wants to foster informal learning needs to see who holds the floor

        For the author, power relations in conversations are usually invisible to management, but they reveal who the key people are and how learning between colleagues can be boosted or blocked.

        For people managers

        • Knowing how power shows up in collaboration helps identify the key people and ways to boost informal learning. (author)
        • Our reading: spaces like the glass table and the quick meetings appear as the stage of learning; whoever decides the agenda also decides what is learned.

        For people who work in teams

        • The analysts themselves say persuading and negotiating are learned day to day, and that technical knowledge alone is not enough. (quotes)
        • Our reading: noticing when the phrase “the company wants” closes a discussion helps separate the company’s rule from the speaker’s own interest.

        For researchers

        • Repeat the study over a longer period, with ethnography. (author)
        • Use Discourse Analysis in sectors beyond technology and look at power at group and organisational level. (author)

        What this research does not say

        • Five people, one team. It studies a single group in one technology company; it does not show how things are elsewhere. · according to the author
        • Only what was said in the room. WhatsApp, Skype and internal-app messages, documents such as the job description and the physical space were left out, for lack of permission or by choice of method. · according to the author
        • Notes, not recordings. The quotes come from the researcher’s notes; in one conversation on the way to the coffee area, some words could not be recorded. · according to the author
        • Interpretation, not measurement. The scenes and kinds of discourse are the author’s reading; the dissertation neither counts utterances nor measures how much each person learned. · our note
        • Scoreboard and heights. The scoreboard on screen 8, the floor plan on screen 4 and the blocks on screen 9 were put together by us to explain the scenes. · our note

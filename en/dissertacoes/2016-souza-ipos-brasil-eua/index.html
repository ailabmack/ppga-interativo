What happens to shares after their stock market debut?

Between 2006 and 2011, 104 Brazilian and 149 American companies went public. The research followed their shares from the first trading day to three years later and compared each one with its own market's index. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The topic

Going public means selling pieces of the company on the stock exchange for the first time

This debut is called an IPO, an initial public offering of shares. The research looks at two questions: how much the share rises on the very first day, and whether, in the following years, it earns more or less than the market as a whole. Tap each term.

03 The data

Seven in ten debuts happened in 2006 and 2007

The research gathered the debuts from 2006 to 2011 on the Brazilian exchange and on the NYSE and Nasdaq, the two big US exchanges. The two boom years hold most of them. In Brazil, 2007 alone had 54 debuts, more than half the sample.

Compustat database (plus Economatica for Brazil). 18 Brazilian companies were dropped (122 → 104), almost all because of mergers, takeovers or delisting, and 19 American ones (168 → 149), because of missing or inconsistent data. Tarpon was dropped for rising 105.21% on its debut, an extreme case.

04 The two markets

In Brazil, it is the big companies that make it to the exchange

The average Brazilian offering was R$ 956.5 million; the American one, US$ 258.6 million. For the author, in a younger market with less trading, like Brazil's, going public is limited to big companies. In the US, many small and mid-sized companies debut, especially on the Nasdaq.

05 How to measure

A share only did well if it rose more than the market over the same period

Rising 20% in a year when the market rose 40% means falling behind. That is why the research compares each share with its market's index: the Ibovespa in Brazil and the S&P 500 in the US. The difference is the abnormal return. Move both sliders.

illustration Made-up numbers. The formula is equation 3: (1 + share return) ÷ (1 + index return) − 1, with prices adjusted for dividends and splits.

And for a whole country?

The research does the same calculation with the average of all the debuts in each country: the Wealth Relative. It divides the wealth of someone who put equal money into each debut by that of someone who put it into the index. Above 1, the debuts won; below 1, they lost.

06 The first day · Tables 3 to 7

On debut day, US shares rose five times as much

It is like a ticket sold below what the public would pay: on the first day, the price jumps. This jump is called underpricing. It averaged 3.33% in Brazil and 16.6% in the US. And it changes with the market's mood. Choose the phase.

08 The finding · Chart 9 · 253 debuts

Over three years, Brazilian debuts lost to the index and American ones won

In the first 90 days, the two countries move together, close to 1. From the first year on, the paths split. In the US, someone who put equal money into each debut ended up with 31% more wealth than in the index. In Brazil, with 8% less. Drag the time.

Time since the debut

The author reads Brazil as long-run value destruction, in line with classic studies, and the US as the opposite. Another of the dissertation's yardsticks, the average abnormal return of each share, gives −10.5% in year 1 and −13.1% in year 2 in Brazil, but +2.0% in year 3; in the US, +22.1%, +28.5% and +35.1% (Table 3). From month 3 to year 3, the difference between the countries passed the test.

10 The unexpected · Tables 5 and 6

Companies that debuted in Brazil after the 2008 crisis beat the index

The author separated the debuts before and after the collapse of Lehman Brothers. Before, 78 in Brazil and 116 in the US; after, 26 and 33. Bet before you look: after the crisis, did Brazilian debuts end up below the Ibovespa, level with it, or above?

For the author, Brazil left the recession early (public spending, tax cuts, credit), and abundant global money sought out the country, which ended up overvaluing its shares. In 2009, the Ibovespa rose 74% and the S&P 500, 21.6%. This is his explanation, not a test.

11 Debut and long run · Tables 10 and 11

In Brazil, a big jump on debut day did not guarantee a good third year

The author sees an inverse relationship in Brazil: shares that debuted high lost steam later. The debuts were split in half by the first-day jump. In year 3, the half that rose least on debut was 22.7% above the Ibovespa; the half that rose most, 18.7% below. In the US, the two halves ended up similar.

12 In practice · limits · credits

What the comparison teaches investors, companies going public and regulators

Investors

Between 2006 and 2011, buying at the debut in Brazil earned little on the first day and, on average, fell behind the Ibovespa over three years, with shares going in very different directions. This describes that period; it promises nothing about the next one.

Companies thinking of going public

Timing matters. In the 2006–2007 boom the debut jump was bigger; in the crisis, smaller. The author links this to windows of opportunity: companies and banks take advantage of optimistic phases to sell shares.

Exchange and regulators

The author suggests that the stricter US governance rules (the 2002 Sarbanes-Oxley Act) may have improved the profile of companies going public there, and links the Brazilian result to more information asymmetry and more conflicts of interest in a younger market. These are hypotheses, not tests.

Researchers

Suggested next steps: measure whether the Sarbanes-Oxley Act really changed the performance of American debuts and how much each country's economic scenario weighs on the outcome of debuts.

What this research does not say

  • A short, turbulent period. Debuts from 2006 to 2011, with a boom and a global crisis in between; 2008 had only 13 debuts in both countries. · from the author's data
  • Averages with a lot of spread. Each share weighs the same, and the year-3 standard deviation exceeds 90 points in both countries: a few extreme cases move the averages a lot. · our note
  • Comparison with the general index. Each share is measured against the Ibovespa or the S&P 500, not against similar companies in sector and size. And companies that disappeared through mergers, takeovers or delisting were left out. · our note
  • The causes are hypotheses. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act, information asymmetry and the economic scenario appear as the author's explanations; the research compares averages and does not test causes. It also does not deduct brokerage costs or taxes. · our note

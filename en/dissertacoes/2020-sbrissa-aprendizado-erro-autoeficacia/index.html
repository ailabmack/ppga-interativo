Who learns from their own mistakes at work?

With 242 people who have a boss, the dissertation shows that confidence in one’s own ability and an open, approachable superior go hand in hand with learning from mistakes. Safety within the team, which many point to as key, made no difference over and above them. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem

Mistakes are inevitable; learning from them is not

No strategy eliminates every mistake, and an organisation that handles mistakes openly learns, innovates and changes better. But learning from them is not automatic: it depends on many things. Before seeing the data, make your guess. After a mistake, what helps a person most to learn from it?

The four options are the four ideas the dissertation brings together in a single model (sections 1 and 2; Frese and Keith, 2015; Rybowiak and colleagues, 1999). Your guess stays in your browser and comes back on screen 8.

08 The finding

Self-confidence weighs most; the approachable boss comes next; the safe team, almost nothing

People who believe they can solve problems and handle the unexpected are the ones who most say they learn from their own mistakes. An open, available and approachable superior also counts, less so. Switch the scale:

Together, the three explain 14.2% of the differences between people in learning from mistakes (R²); the rest comes from outside the model.

Table 10. f² = how much R² would fall without that arrow (0.02 small, 0.15 medium, 0.35 large). For the author, an R² of 14% is medium in the behavioural sciences and similar to that of the study the model drew on (Ye, Wang and Li, 2019). A link is not a cause: everything comes from a questionnaire answered once.

10 What it found · the safe team

Resources and an approachable boss build safety in the team

Psychological safety is the belief that one can take risks in the team, ask for help and discuss difficult problems. It grows where the team has information, help from specialists and training and where the boss is approachable. Move the sliders:

11 What it found · the checks

Run three different ways, the links barely change

The dissertation also ran the model with the type of company as a control and with the bet that self-confidence would change the weight of the safe team (H4). That bet was not confirmed. Switch the model:

12 So what?

Learning from mistakes runs through the person and whoever leads them

For the author, the results contradict the idea that the environment matters most: it pays to invest in each person’s development and in open leaders.

For HR and development

  • Invest in self-development, self-knowledge and people’s confidence in their own ability, for instance with role models to observe (Bandura). (author)
  • Encourage informal learning, on the job itself. (author)

For those who lead

  • Be open, available and approachable; accept mistakes as part of the job, talk about them honestly and help fix them. (author)
  • Assess this style when selecting and training leaders. (author)

For the organisation

  • Review rewards and punishments so they do not push people to hide mistakes. (author)
  • Our reading: information, help and training do not appear linked directly to learning, but they underpin team safety (screen 10).

What this study does not say

  • Who answered. 242 people from many companies, by convenience; it does not represent all workers, and the controls do not remove every bias. · according to the author
  • Only one side. Only those being led answered; the bosses’ view was left out. · according to the author
  • Belief, not outcome. The scale measures what people believe they learn from mistakes, not actual mistakes or what changed afterwards. · according to the author
  • A link is not a cause. A questionnaire answered once shows what goes together; the three factors explain 14% of learning from mistakes. · our note
  • “My team”. The safety statements are about the team; for people without a team or peers, they may not fit. · according to the author

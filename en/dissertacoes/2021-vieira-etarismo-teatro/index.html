In the theatre, age weighs in silence: fewer roles, veiled prejudice and nobody living on acting alone

Ten actors and actresses aged 45 to 73, with at least 20 years on stage, told what changes as you grow older in the theatre. Prejudice shows up more veiled than open, roles grow scarce, even more so for women, and all of them needed another source of income.

Author: Ubiratan Negrão Vieira Advisor: Prof. Dr. Darcy Mitiko Mori Hanashiro Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The problem

Brazil is ageing, and nobody had studied the older actor in the theatre

With more older people there will be more older workers, and prejudice against them is already well known in companies. The author, an actor himself, wanted to know how this plays out on stage. The question: what challenges do older actors face in the theatre because of their age?

Articles on ageism in the theatre in three academic databases (Spell, Scielo and Scopus, 2014–2019)0
Articles on film and advertising that served as the starting point (Web of Science and Google Scholar)6

The search found only news stories and interviews on the topic in the theatre, not studies. So the dissertation starts from what is known about film and advertising (section 2.6).

03 How the research looked

Ten lives on stage, from 20 to 52 years of career

The author talked with actors and actresses over 45, with at least 20 years in theatre and a professional licence. The interviews followed a guide of 14 open questions, were recorded with consent, in person or online, in the middle of the pandemic, and were analysed by categories. In the dissertation each person has a fictitious name; here, only a number (E01 to E10).

04 The research map

Six challenges; three of them, together, are ageism

Ageism is prejudice linked to age. The dissertation treats it in three layers: the ready-made idea about the group (stereotype), the hostile feeling (prejudice) and the act that takes opportunities away (discrimination). The interviews brought three more challenges that are not prejudice but weigh just as much: the body, the roles and the money.

Height of each block: how many items the dissertation lists under that category in its summary table (Appendix A, our count). It is not how often the topic came up. Tap a block.

They know it exists, but only 3 of 10 say they were discriminated against

09 Theatre, TV and film

In the theatre, wrinkles weigh less; the body weighs more

All the interviewees mentioned losing physical vigour. For the author, it weighs more in the theatre, which is live, with no editing or stunt doubles. The demand for a young face, with botox and procedures, is greater on TV and in film, and the theatre shows the actor as they are. Switch the lens:

Order, not measurement

Height = order in the author's reading (sections 4.3.3 and 4.3.4), with no figures in the dissertation. For the face, he puts TV and film together above the theatre.

10 What people live on

None of the ten lives on acting alone

The author found nobody who worked only as a stage actor. Everyone spoke about supplementary income, and it almost always comes from the arts themselves: teaching, directing, writing, producing. Only one person has a second profession outside the arts. Tap an activity:

11 The finding

Everything flows into plan B

In the author's reading, the challenges add up: ready-made ideas, prejudice and discrimination, plus the loss of vigour and the lack of roles, push older actors towards another source of income. And he points to a way out: if those who hire valued experience, which everyone mentioned, there would be more roles.

12 So what?

The experience everyone mentions could turn into roles

Directors and castingThe author asks them to look at the older actor from another angle: experience can shorten the path in rehearsal and improve the result.
Unions and governmentThe interviewees mentioned quotas for older actors. The author suggests the research could feed this discussion with unions and public bodies.
AudienceFor the author, audiences could enjoy more shows with older actors, for their stage presence and their relationship with the audience.
Those who actEveryone built a plan B. Some prepared early for a parallel career, seeing what happened to colleagues; in the accounts, the lack of a plan B is linked to falling ill.

What the research does not say

  • Ten people, one viewpoint. These are the accounts of those who act; directors, producers and casting staff were not heard, and what they think appears through the actors' voices. (our note)
  • No count of quotes. The dissertation does not say how many mentioned each topic, except in a few cases (experience, plan B and physical vigour: everyone; discrimination suffered: three). (our note)
  • A researcher from the field. The author is an actor and reached the interviewees through his life in the profession, which opened doors and also shapes the reading. (our note)
  • Pandemic. Many actors did not feel safe talking in person, which delayed the interviews. (author)

Next step suggested by the author: studying whether older actors and actresses are paid differently in the theatre, which did not come up in the accounts.

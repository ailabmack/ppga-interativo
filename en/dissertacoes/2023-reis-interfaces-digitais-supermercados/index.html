What makes a technology catch on at the supermarket

The author visited 47 stores, 35 websites and 24 apps of São Paulo supermarkets and interviewed three executives from the sector. The finding: a digital interface only takes hold when it saves the customer time, effort or money and also brings the store efficiency or sales, and customers still have to lose their fear of making mistakes. 02 The problem · food retail

A giant sector that lives on cents cannot get technology wrong

According to the Brazilian supermarket association (ABRAS), cited in the dissertation, food retail had revenues of R$ 611.2 billion in 2021, 7.03% of GDP. But the average net profit was 2.9%. Drag the value of a shopping trip and see how much is left for the supermarket:

With so little slack, every machine, app or kiosk has to pay for itself. Hence the questions behind the research: how do you create value with digital innovations? Why do some catch on and others not? In a Web of Science search (2000 to 2021), the author found only 7 articles on digital touchpoints in retail, and none answered this.

05 Result · websites and apps

Outside the store, the phone has become the flyer of offers

From the websites and apps, only the functions that bring customers to the physical store remained: 5 on websites and 9 in apps. Buying online and picking up in store and activating offers on the phone to use at the till lead the way. And the weekly flyer of offers shows up in 3 of the 9 app functions.

What sells the most?

[...] you think of promotions and app push notifications, personalised discounts, something like that, but it's the flyer, the good old flyer, physical, actual paper [...]VAR02, in charge of digitalisation at a retailer

That is why chains send the same flyer through every channel: e-mail, WhatsApp and app notifications.

10 The unexpected · who foots the bill

Click and collect costs the store efficiency, and yet it is among the most offered

For the supermarket, in-store pickup takes space, staff and an accurate inventory, and it is not clear that it sells more. By the logic of value, few should offer it. Guess: how many do?

What the store pays so customers can leave quickly

  • A reserved area, often in place of parking spaces, and an employee on hand (VAR01).
  • Orders picked in the morning and, where online reaches 20% of revenue, overnight (VAR02).
  • An accurate inventory in the system and managers who do not “cut” items from orders: today we fill 97, 98% of the items, we want to get to 99.5 (VAR02).
11 The finding · the author's model

Value is a cycle that customers and supermarkets turn together

The supermarket only gains when customers adopt the interface, and customers only adopt it when they gain. Both learn from use and start the loop again with new expectations. This is what the author calls multilevel, interdependent and cyclical value. Walk around the cycle:

12 Takeaways

Before buying a technology, a supermarket should ask what the customer gains

The author suggests mapping each step of the shopping journey by the customer's time, effort and benefit: where value is lost, there is an opportunity to innovate. And judging technology by more than cost savings.

For supermarkets

  • Favour interfaces that save customers time or effort and bring efficiency or sales.
  • Have the interface in quantity, in many stores, and always working.
  • Teach customers how to use it: fear and embarrassment of making mistakes weigh in.

For technology makers

  • Design easy, safe interfaces that prevent mistakes.
  • Use the customer's phone instead of in-store devices: software scales at lower cost than machines.
  • Fix what burdens operations (like the robot's battery) so the numbers add up.

For research

  • A model of how digital interfaces create value in food retail, seen as a process.
  • Next steps: other cities, the size and format of each chain, the post-purchase phase and the customer as the focus.

What this research does not say

  • Whether it holds for all of Brazil. It is an exploratory study, with São Paulo stores, three interviews and a single supplier. · according to the author
  • How many customers use each interface. The percentages count whether the chain offers the interface, not how many people use it. · our observation
  • How much time or money each one saves. The dials are the author's reading from trying the interfaces and what the executives said, without measurement. · our observation
  • The customers' opinion. Those who spoke were executives; the author suggests studying customers themselves. · according to the author

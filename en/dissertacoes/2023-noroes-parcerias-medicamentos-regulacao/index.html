The rules of the partnerships that make medicines for Brazil’s public health system

In Brazil, private companies teach public laboratories how to make medicines that the public health system (SUS) buys. These are the Productive Development Partnerships (PDPs). The dissertation heard six people from four companies that took part in them and asks how the country’s rules help or hinder these partnerships. Explained in 11 screens.

02 The problem

In a PDP, the private company hands the medicine’s know-how to a public laboratory

The Ministry of Health picks medicines that are strategic for the SUS. A private company that knows how to make them transfers the technology to a public laboratory, and the SUS buys the partnership’s product during the transfer. The goal is wider access, lower prices and less reliance on imports. Tap each part of the diagram:

04 The medicine’s path

A new medicine takes over a decade; a copy is approved in about a year

A new medicine goes through clinical trials in people, in ever larger phases. Once the patent expires, a copy (generic or “similar”) does not repeat those trials: it only needs bioequivalence, which shows in healthy volunteers that the body absorbs it like the original. Switch the scale:

05 How the research looked

Six people from four private companies told what it was like

Two pharmaceutical laboratories and two CROs (companies that run clinical research for others), all in Brazil’s Southeast and with years of PDP experience. The video interviews lasted about 1.5 hours each and were analysed in four themes: why they joined, what helped or hindered, the effect of the rules and whether they would do it again. 06 What it found

Each company joined for its own reason, and the social mission mattered to most

An expiring patent, an invitation from the government, a client’s request, a niche with few competitors. Yet all four cited the impact on society as a positive point and, in most interviews, as one of the reasons to join. Pick a company and read the quote:

    09 The finding

    The law got clearer, but when procedures are not followed, companies leave

    Written rules reduce uncertainty. But the public sector has the power to decide case by case, and the companies reported procedures and deadlines that were not met. This creates legal insecurity and raises the transaction cost, the cost of negotiating, monitoring and enforcing a deal. See the domino effect:

      Chain built by us from the conclusions (section 6) and Laboratory B’s quotes. For the author, suing the State has an “insurmountable” cost, so leaving becomes the usual response. It is an explanation proposed from six interviews, not a measured effect.

      10 The unexpected

      The shortcut that made the partnerships viable also moved them away from innovation

      Because of patents, the SUS has mostly run PDPs for copies, approved through bioequivalence. It is simpler and faster but, for the author, the focus shifted from innovation to replication. And whoever arrived before the rules paid the price: CRO A ran the country’s first study of “similar” medicines when ANVISA still had no guide for biosimilars. Compare:

      11 So what?

      Keeping the partnerships depends on the public side following the agreed procedures

      The author’s conclusion: PDP regulation has improved a lot since the start, but some procedures are still leading partnerships to end.

      For the Ministry of Health

      • Manage the execution of the partnerships and their procedures better, if it wants to keep the model. (author)
      • Our reading: the weak point raised is not a lack of rules but the public side meeting deadlines and procedures.

      For laboratories and CROs

      • Know the risks and transaction costs before choosing the partnership and while in it. (author)
      • The laboratories pointed to the public partner’s infrastructure as the biggest bottleneck in technology transfer.

      For researchers

      • Measure how proactive the SUS is in the partnerships and how that relates to PDP rules. (author)
      • Study how ANVISA’s manufacturing rules shape technology transfer. (author)

      What this research does not say

      • One side only. Six people from four private companies were heard; public laboratories, the Ministry and ANVISA did not give their side. · our note
      • Few cases, one region. Companies chosen by convenience and referral, all in the Southeast: the findings are clues, not a picture of every PDP. · author and our note
      • Partnerships in general. At the interviewees’ request, no specific partnership was analysed, and the deadlines quoted are averages. · according to the author
      • Out of scope. Technology transfer to the public laboratory, cited as the longest phase, and coordination across alliances were left for future studies. · according to the author

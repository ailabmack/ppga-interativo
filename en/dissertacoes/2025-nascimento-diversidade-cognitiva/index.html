Does thinking differently help teams decide better?

A survey of 257 professionals on cross-functional project teams examined how diversity in ways of thinking relates to teamwork, creativity, communication and the quality of decisions. Explained in 13 screens.

Author: Tatiane Teixeira Neri do Nascimento Advisor: Prof. Dr. Fellipe Silva Martins Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The starting point

One meeting, five minds

In a cross-functional team, people from different areas work together on a temporary project and make decisions that matter to the organization. Each of them sees the same problem in a different way.

03 The concept

The difference that doesn't show in the photo

Diversity has two levels. On the surface are the differences you can see, such as job role, education, gender and ethnicity. Below it lie values, beliefs and ways of thinking: that is where cognitive diversity, the focus of the research, lives.

    The four bottom layers are the four questions of the scale used to measure cognitive diversity (Van der Vegt and Janssen, 2003). The iceberg is a metaphor. Source: Sections 2.3, 2.4 and 3.1.

    04 What was measured

    Five pieces, one question

    The research set out to learn how cognitive diversity relates to decision quality, and what role three everyday team processes play. Click a card to see how each piece was measured.

    All questions were answered on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Altogether there are 91 statements about the team. Source: Section 3.1 and Table 3.

    10 The unexpected

    Talking well is not enough

    Of the three team processes, only creativity has a direct bridge to the decision. Once the other pieces are taken into account, communication and teamwork add nothing to decision quality. Try crossing.

    This does not mean communication and teamwork don't matter: they are strongly linked to each other and to creativity (screen 8). What does not appear is a direct link from them to the decision. Source: Table 31 and Section 5.

    11 How much is explained

    Decisions depend on much more

    Each glass shows how much of the differences between teams the model manages to explain. Communication is nearly half full. Decision quality sits at 13.5%: most of it comes from factors the research did not measure.

    Measure used: R² (Table 30). The possibly missing factors are pointed out by the author herself in the limitations (Section 8).

    12 Why it matters

    So what?

    For the author, managing cognitive diversity goes beyond hiring different people: you need to create conditions for differences to turn into ideas, and for ideas to turn into good decisions.

    For managers and project leaders

    Make room for people to disagree without fear. Use structured brainstorming and role rotation, and put several alternatives on the table before deciding. Communication is there to explore points of view, not to reach a quick consensus.

    The author also suggests dosing diversity according to the project phase: encourage it while searching for ideas and align during execution.

    Implications from the dissertation · the suggestion about phases was not tested

    For HR and team assessment

    Looking only at the end result hides the path. Intermediate indicators, such as creativity, collective learning and the number of alternatives considered, help show whether the team is making the most of its differences.

    Implications from the dissertation

    For people who work in teams

    Thinking differently from the group is a resource. In the study, creativity was the only direct bridge between team processes and the decision: a different perspective carries more weight when it becomes a concrete proposal.

    Our reading

    For society

    Valuing different ways of thinking is a matter of inclusion and also of competitiveness: organizations open to a variety of reasoning styles, experiences and worldviews tend to be more innovative.

    13 Limits and credits

    What this research does not say

    • It shows associations, not cause and effect. The data were collected at a single point in time, and each person answered about all the pieces, which may inflate some links.
    • Diversity was measured with 4 questions. The concept still has no consensus definition, and a short measure may leave nuances out.
    • The sample does not represent the whole country. It was a convenience sample, with 80.2% of responses from São Paulo and 46.7% from the services sector.
    • The model explains little of the decision (13.5%). Centralized decision-making, psychological safety and remote work are among the pieces that may be missing.
    • Indirect paths were not tested. This page shows only the direct links. Testing routes such as diversity → teamwork → creativity → decision is one of the suggested next steps.

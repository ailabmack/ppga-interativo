In large listed companies, having an owner with half of the voting shares came with lower profit and, in most models, with more debt

The dissertation followed 39 companies in the IBrX 50 index, from 2010 to 2017, and tested six pieces of governance (who owns, who sits on the board, who chairs it) in 14 models. The piece that showed up most was the majority owner: lower profit in every performance model. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The topic

Governance is the set of rules that protects the money of those who do not run the company

People who invest in a listed company rarely run it. Corporate governance was born to prevent abuse by those in charge. But who that “someone in charge” is changes from one country to another.

In Brazil, the law allows shares without voting rights (preferred shares). So a controlling shareholder can be in charge with a small part of total capital, and the main conflict becomes one between them and the smaller partners.

Source: chapter 1 (context) and section 2.2 of the dissertation, based on Andrade and Rossetti (2004) and Silveira et al. (2004).

03 The context

The stock exchange created good-governance labels, and companies join if they want to

Brazil’s corporate law does not require many good practices. So the stock exchange (now B3) created listing segments with stricter rules than the law. Joining is voluntary; the idea is that more investor protection lowers perceived risk and raises share value.

08 The finding

With a majority owner, profit was lower on every yardstick

In companies where someone held 50% or more of the voting shares (almost half the cases), the return was 6 to 10 percentage points lower, in all six performance models. For the author, when the owner is in command, management tends to be less professional.

6 of 6 performance models with a negative sign (p between 0.009 and 0.033)

Theory expected this, but the literature is not unanimous: some authors cited think a strong owner monitors better and improves results.

09 Hypothesis 1 · debt

Majority owners and independent directors came with more debt

The majority owner came with more debt in 4 of the 6 debt models with reliable data; the share of independent directors, in 3. For the author, debt can act as a brake on opportunism, and independent directors, more technical, help find credit through their contacts.

10 The unexpected

When the CEO also chaired the board, profit was slightly higher, contrary to what was expected

According to agency theory, the person who runs the company should not chair the body that oversees them: the CEO comes to control what the board gets to know. The author expected lower profit in these cases.

11 So what?

Who owns and who oversees goes along with how a company funds itself and how much it earns

The dissertation concludes that some governance pieces are related to debt and performance: the majority owner, the share of independent directors and CEO duality. The other three (women on the board, board size and stock options) barely showed up.

For investors

  • In the 39 companies, concentrated control came with lower returns. (screen 08)
  • It is worth looking at who controls the company and how much of the voting capital they hold. (our reading)

For boards and managers

  • Knowing whether governance moves debt helps find the debt level with the most tax benefit. (author)
  • Independent directors can open doors to credit through their network. (author)

For the market and regulators

  • Good practices, especially those protecting minority shareholders, help develop the capital market and the economy. (author, with La Porta et al.)

For researchers

  • Repeat the analysis with dynamic panels, which may reveal what static ones missed. (author)
  • A governance variable may weigh differently in each country: studying the Brazilian case matters. (author)
Association is not cause. The dissertation shows what went together in these 39 companies. It cannot say that removing the majority owner would raise profit, or that adding independent directors would make a company borrow more. This page is not investment advice.
12 Limits and credits

What this research does not say

  • Large companies only. These are 39 IBrX 50 companies, the most traded; smaller and private companies may behave differently. · our observation
  • Static panels. The author suggests redoing it with dynamic panels, which take the previous year into account and may show other relationships. · according to the author
  • Many tests, a 10% threshold. With 14 models and 6 pieces, some isolated signs may appear by chance; what repeats, like the majority owner, counts more. · our observation
  • A yardstick with a problem. For the total-liabilities yardstick, the descriptive table shows a mean of 0.13 with a standard deviation of 2.96 and a maximum of 0.72, which does not add up; the author already calls it the “weakest”. We left those two models hatched and out of the counts. · our observation
  • Signs and text that diverge. We followed each table’s z and confidence interval. So we counted the majority owner in Table 6 (p 0.036), which the text says lost significance, and read duality in Table 7 as negative. · our observation

Uncertain rules lead drug-testing partnerships to build their own controls

To test new medicines in people, drug companies hire specialised firms. In Brazil these partnerships live with slow approval, unclear rules and shifting decisions. The dissertation asks: does the shape of public rules change how the companies agree on and oversee the work? In 12 screens.

Author: Ji Yoon Lee Sanches Advisor: Prof. Dr. Walter Bataglia Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The setting · sections 6.2 and 8

Brazil leads drug trials in Latin America

Everyone knows that getting a trial approved here is slow. So why do drug companies keep bringing trials to Brazil?

Clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov in October 2015 (Table 2 of the dissertation). According to the author, Brazil was also the first country in the region to have clinical research companies, from the 1980s.

03 The rules of the game · section 2.1.1

On the World Bank’s ruler, Brazil’s regulation sits in the middle and keeps slipping

The dissertation starts from an idea in institutional economics: formal rules (laws, regulations, agencies) exist to reduce uncertainty for those doing business. When they fail, each company has to protect itself.

One thermometer for these rules is the World Bank’s regulatory quality indicator: the government’s ability to make rules that help the private sector develop.

04 Who does what · section 6.1

The drug company pays for the trial and a specialised firm does part of the work

Before reaching the pharmacy, a medicine is tested in people, in four phases. For a long time the drug company did everything itself. Today it hires CROs (Contract Research Organisations). It is an alliance: two independent companies working together for years.

~30%of trial time saved with a CRO

> US$ 20 bnCRO market, almost a third of research spending

Figures cited in the dissertation (Gomes et al., 2012; PhRMA, 2015). “Full package” and “only some tasks” are the two hiring models (one-stop-shop and functional) described by Hujistee and Schipper (2011); the third mode shows the old way.

05 How it looked · sections 7 and 8

Of 20 organisations invited, 7 opened the door

The author spoke with companies on both sides of the alliance and with those around them: industry associations and the body that gives ethical approval. The interviews were open-ended, recorded and read with categories set in advance (rules: clarity, agencies, timing; alliance: uncertainty, contract, controls).

Each company and body appears by the code or acronym used in the dissertation; the people interviewed are not identified here. ANVISA, the health regulator, was approached several times and did not give an interview. After reading each case, the author crossed the rule categories with the alliance categories in 28 comparison grids.

06 How long it takes · sections 6.2 and 8

Each side sees a different clock for approving a trial

In Brazil a trial goes through three doors: the local ethics committee (CEP), the national ethics commission (CONEP) and ANVISA, the health regulator. The rules promise a few months. The companies tell another story. Tap the markers.

07 The unexpected · section 8.6

For the ethics body, double review is the exception and part of the delay comes from the researchers themselves

Every organisation heard criticised the rules, except one: CONEP. For it, nearly all projects are decided by the local committee alone; the double local-and-national review is the exception.

What CONEP defends

Protecting participants: many have difficulty reading and, in Brazil, seeking redress in court is hard. That is why the rule requires access to the medicine after the study for as long as it helps.

What the companies see

Demands that change from one project to the next and unclear post-study access rules. One association sums it up: “Is it slow? It is. Will I give post-study access? I don’t want to take the risk. So Brazil is out.”

Out of every 100 projects, according to CONEP

96 to 98 decided by the local committee alone   2 to 4 also go through CONEP (multi-country trials, genetics, Indigenous peoples…)

Where the pending issues are, according to CONEP
90% in the consent form

“Many consent forms look as if they were written by lawyers.” The form is “a document from the investigator to the participant” and needs plain language. Average time at CONEP, it says: 0.9 month.

Figures given by CONEP’s representative in the interview; the dissertation does not check them against another source. The companies also named the consent form as the main source of returned submissions.

08 Propositions 1 and 2 · section 8.8

Slow, shifting rules leave the partnership unsure whether the study will reach the end

The biggest uncertainty reported is not the partner: it is the study itself. Multi-country trials have a global deadline for recruiting patients. If approval here is late, the trial closes without Brazil, or the sponsor cancels the contract. Switch on the flaws the companies pointed out.

illustration of the propositions
Alliance uncertainty

The dissertation proposes that bureaucratic and ineffective rules (proposition 1) and unclear and unstable rules (proposition 2) increase alliance uncertainty. These are propositions to be tested in future research, not a measurement: the meter only lights up with the flaws switched on, to show the logic. The quotes are from the companies and associations, in Tables 6 and 7.

09 Propositions 3 and 4 · Table 5

Of 17 controls between the companies, 9 stay outside the contract

To make up for the rules’ failings, companies tighten the contract (reports, meetings, requirements about the team) and create controls that are not even written down (training the partner’s team, visiting the sites, tracking turnover). “The contract is just a formalisation of everything that was discussed,” says IF-A.

Types as in Table 5 of the dissertation; the count by type is ours. “Both” are controls that sometimes appear in the contract and sometimes outside it (audits and amendments to the umbrella contract).

10 The IF-B case · sections 8.2 and 9

With high uncertainty, the partner becomes almost part of the house

Between buying a service on the market and doing everything in-house, an alliance sits in the middle. The author concludes that, with high uncertainty, some controls come “very close” to a hierarchy, when the company does not bring the task back in-house.

IF-B’s oversight meetings with the CRO in one year

Set in the contract: every 15 days in Brazil, monthly in Latin America and every three months worldwide. In one year, 40 meetings (our count).

11 Conflicts · section 8.7.2

In a dispute nobody goes to court: it climbs step by step

Every contract provides for court action, but none of the four companies reported a lawsuit. Going to court would bring “more burden than benefit” (IF-A): it exposes the company and takes time. At IF-B, a local problem climbs up to the two directors in charge of the global contract. Climb the ladder.

12 So what?

The contract alone does not hold the partnership together when public rules fail

For the author, the Brazilian case shows that when formal rules do not reduce uncertainty, companies create mechanisms to compensate for the failures, inside and outside the contract, resting on trust between the parties.

For alliance managers

  • Know the rule environment the partnership will operate in and plan controls for it, inside and outside the contract. (author)
  • Prioritise the partner’s qualifications and experience and the quality of the relationship. (author)

For public bodies

  • Talk more with those being regulated, to see their real needs and complaints. (author)
  • Make reviews uniform and standardised, in ethics and at ANVISA, to make the environment more stable. (author)

For researchers

  • Measure the uncertainty created by the rules and test whether it leads to more controls, and which ones. (author)
  • Compare countries with different rules (common law × civil law, regulatory quality). (author)

What this research does not say

  • Few cases. Seven organisations of different kinds; the companies vary in size and reach. It cannot be generalised. · according to the author
  • Propositions, not proof. The four links between rules and controls are hypotheses to be tested with measurement. · according to the author
  • Without ANVISA. The health regulator did not give an interview; the public view came only from ethics (CONEP). · author and our note
  • Timings told by those speaking. The months each organisation cites are accounts, not a survey of cases; and the dissertation dates from 2016, before later rule changes. · our note

In Brazilian companies with a credit rating, the trade-off theory of debt passed part of the test

Debt saves taxes but raises the risk of failing. The trade-off theory says each company seeks the balance point between the two. With 60 Brazilian companies with a credit rating, from 2010 to 2017, the dissertation tests nine company traits in two mirrors: how much they owe and what rating they get. The author concludes the theory holds; in the tables, 3 of the 18 tests came out in the predicted direction, 3 the opposite way and 12 with no clear effect.

02 The theory

Owing pays off until the risk of failing weighs more than the tax saved

Interest on debt is deducted from profit before tax: the more a company owes, the less tax it pays. But too much debt squeezes cash and brings bankruptcy closer, which is costly (lawyers, customers and suppliers who walk away, higher interest).

The static trade-off theory says there is an optimal level of debt, where the next real of tax saved is cancelled out by the next real of expected bankruptcy cost. Move the debt.

03 The thermometer

The credit rating works as a thermometer of the risk of failing

No one knows in advance a company's chance of failing. The dissertation uses the credit rating given by S&P (from AAA to D) as a measure of that chance, set from outside, by the market.

The ratings were grouped into three bands: the higher the number, the worse the rating and the higher the risk. Tap each band.

04 The questions

The same nine traits, tested in two mirrors: debt and rating

If the theory holds, those with more to gain in taxes and less to lose in bankruptcy should owe more and, by choosing to owe more, accept a worse rating.

H1 · The bigger the tax benefit, the higher the debt and the chance of bankruptcy.
H2 · The lower the cost of a bankruptcy, the higher the debt and the chance of bankruptcy.

One distinctive choice: debt does not enter the rating model. For the author, by choosing how much to owe a company is already choosing the rating it will get.

Source: section 2.1.3, Charts 1 and 3 and the “expected sign” column of Tables 6 and 7.

05 How the study looked

Of 219 Brazilian companies with a rating, 60 remained, followed for eight years

The starting point was every Brazilian company with an S&P rating between 2010 and 2017. Banks and other financial firms, which follow a different logic of debt, were dropped, as were companies whose rating did not change over the period or that lacked data.

With each company's data year by year (Capital IQ), the author ran two regressions: one explains how much the company owes (debt ÷ debt plus equity); the other, a logistic regression, explains which rating band it is in.

06 Mirror 1 · how much they owe

Larger companies and those that spent less on research owed more, as the theory predicts

Of the nine traits, three had a clear effect on debt. Size and research and development went in the predicted direction. Swings in results went the opposite way: those with the most unstable profit owed more.

The other six, including the tax rate, could not be told apart from chance. The model explains 23% of the differences in debt between companies (R² 0.23).

07 Mirror 2 · what rating they get

In the rating, three traits mattered, and two of them the opposite way

Companies with more unstable results had worse ratings, as the theory predicts. But those worth more on the stock market than on the balance sheet and those that spent more on research had better ratings, when the dissertation expected worse ones.

Size and fixed assets, which the theory links to a better rating, had no clear effect on the rating.

08 The finding

Of the 18 tests, 3 came out as the theory predicted, 3 the opposite way and 12 with no clear effect

Nine traits × two mirrors = 18 tests. Tap each tile to see what it says.

The author's reading. The dissertation concludes that H1 and H2 are confirmed: debt would be the lever with which a company chooses the chance of bankruptcy it accepts, and larger companies with more fixed assets and more stable results would have less risk of failing. It acknowledges that the theory fails for market value, expenses, research and age. The count above is ours, made on Tables 6 and 7 with the 10% cutoff the dissertation uses.
09 The unexpected

Those with the most unstable profit owed more, and the rating picked up the risk

Swings in results (how much operating profit varied over the past five years) had a clear effect in both mirrors (the only other such trait was research and development). By the theory, a company with unstable cash runs more risk and should owe less. Among the 60 companies, it owed more.

The dissertation recalls studies that had already seen this: companies with a higher cost of bankruptcy end up with riskier debt structures. Switch the company.

10 What did not show up

Tax, the centerpiece of the theory, made no difference in either mirror

The most direct measure of the gain from debt is the rate a company actually pays: income tax and social contribution ÷ profit before tax. On average, the 60 companies paid 21%, below the 34% ceiling.

By the theory, a company paying a higher rate gains more by deducting interest and should owe more. In both regressions, the tax rate could not be told apart from chance. See each mirror.

Source: Table 4 (average effective rate 0.2138), section 4.1 (34% ceiling), Tables 6 and 7. The dissertation lists as a limitation not having studied interest on equity, another Brazilian tax deduction that competes with debt.

11 So what?

Size and profit stability count; tax, in these data, did not show up

For finance directors

  • Larger companies owed more; those investing more in research, less. (screen 06)
  • Unstable profit came with a worse rating. (screen 09)

For lenders

  • Companies with unstable profit owed more, not less: risk piles up where the theory does not expect it. (screen 09)

For tax specialists

  • The effective tax rate explained neither debt nor rating. (screen 10)
  • Interest on equity was left out. (dissertation)

For researchers

  • Expand the sample in companies and years, include other rating agencies, privately held companies and other countries. (dissertation)

What this research does not say

  • Few companies. In Brazil, few companies have a credit rating; with 60, small effects can go unnoticed. · according to the dissertation
  • Rating in three bands. No company was near default, so the rating model in practice compares the two top bands. · our note
  • Association, not cause. The regressions show what moves together; they do not say whether debt came before the risk or after. · our note
  • Direction only. The dissertation does not translate the coefficients into effect sizes; neither does this page. · our note

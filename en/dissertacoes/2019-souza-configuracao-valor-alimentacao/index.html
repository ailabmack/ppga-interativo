Master's dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2019

New food businesses build their operation on the way, one dilemma at a time

The dissertation heard the founders of four small food businesses in São Paulo, 2 to 3 years old: baking mixes, donuts, healthy frozen meals and fine sweets. None started with everything planned. On a base of value proposition and customers, each set up nine activities, from the menu to delivery, and kept changing them after every stumble.

Author: Mariana Nicodemo de Souza Advisor: Prof. Dr. Dimária Silva e Meirelles Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

02 The question

Textbooks draw the firm as a line; a starting business lives in a cycle

The classic value chain lines activities up in a row: buy, make, promote, sell, deliver. It describes a finished firm, looking back. The author asks: how does a firm that is still being born set these activities up?

Her answer: not in a row, but in a trial-and-error cycle, where each settled dilemma changes the way things are done. To see this, she used Activity Theory, which looks at who does the work, with what, what for and under which rules.

04 The method · narrative analysis

Every business story hides dilemmas and the way out that was found

The author read the interviews three times and cut them into events. In each, she looked for the dilemma (the “oppositions”) and the outcome, written as an “if… then…” (the “syllogisms”). Do the same with an excerpt from firm A, the one selling jars of baking mix.

05 Activity Theory · Figures 7 to 10

Every activity has seven parts, and the same task is put together in different ways

In Activity Theory, doing something involves who does it, the tools, the aim, the outcome, the rules, the community around it and the division of labour. When a part does not fit, a contradiction arises, and that is what makes the activity change. Compare production in the four firms.

07 Breaks and outcomes

Every change in the operation came from a stumble

In Activity Theory, contradictions are the engine of change. In the four kitchens they show up as “breaks”: something stops working and the activity is redone. Tap the cards.

08 The unexpected · competitor analysis

For someone selling something new, the competitor also teaches the customer

One of the dilemmas in Chart 75 is “competition as harmful × competition as beneficial”. At firm A, whose product nobody knew in Brazil, competitors were welcome: they helped build the habit. See how each firm looks at its rivals.

09 Financial and accounting management

Keeping track of cash is one of the hardest tasks, and here only the two firms required to do it did it

Of the nine activities, it was the only one the dissertation did not find working in all four firms. The two already registered as microenterprises (ME) use management software and send their invoices to an accountant. The two still registered as MEI (Brazil's simplest sole-trader status) do not keep proper control, for lack of time. Tap each one.

Dilemma from Chart 75: “firm's legal category × mandatory controls”. With four firms, this is a pattern to watch, not a rule.

10 Future planning

The plans are to grow, but slowly and with their own money

They all want to change something: customer, menu, sales channel, kitchen. And they all weigh the same dilemma: grow bit by bit, with what the business earns, or invest heavily (with loans, a shop, more structure). The dissertation records caution, wariness of outside money and worry about Brazil's economy.

11 So what?

A starting firm's business model is never finished: it is redone with every dilemma

People opening a businessOUR READING The four kitchens' stumbles (the first big order, the delivery that ruined the sweets, the untracked cash) appear as part of the road. Knowing they will come helps to test small before investing.
People who support small firmsOUR READING Financial control was the weakest activity, above all among MEIs. Practical support on that front, not only with the initial plan, seems to be missing.
ResearchFor the author, this is the first time Activity Theory is used on its own as a strategy lens for business models. She suggests applying the model and the method to firms in other trades and to large, established firms.

What the research does not say

  • It does not generalize. The conclusions do not hold for all small or new firms, in food or in other trades. (author)
  • Only founders spoke. Staff, customers and partners were not heard; everything is the account of the person in charge. (method)
  • Accounts, not numbers. The dissertation did not measure the firms' sales, costs or profit. (our note)
  • Those still open. All four firms were running; we do not know how firms that closed early set up their operation. (our note)

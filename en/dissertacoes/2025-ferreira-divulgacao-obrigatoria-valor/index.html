Does requiring firms to disclose sustainable practices change their value?

Between 2016 and 2022, seven emerging countries, Brazil among them, began to require listed firms to report what they do for the environment, for people and for good governance (ESG). The research followed 3,175 firms in 11 countries from 2014 to 2023 to see what the law did to their value. Explained in 12 screens.

02 What the theories say

Reporting what a firm does costs money and also informs

Disclosing ESG practices can be the firm's choice or a legal requirement. The difference matters.

VoluntaryThe firm chooses what to report and when. Without a standard, most reports cannot be audited, and trust falls.
MandatoryEveryone reports, to the same standard, which makes comparing and monitoring possible. But the rule brings new costs: processes, people, reports.

06 Try it · how to measure value

Two yardsticks for firm value

The research measures value in two ways. The market-to-book ratio (MTB) says how many times over the market pays for book equity. The total shareholder return (TSR) adds how much the share rose in the year and the dividends paid.

07 The method

Instead of levels, the research compares how much each group changed

The method is called difference-in-differences (DID). It treats the law as a natural experiment: it compares the change in firm value in the countries that made disclosure mandatory with the change in the countries that did not.

illustration Made-up numbers to show the logic. In the research, each country has its own "after" (the year of its law), and the model controls for what is specific to each country and each year. The method's assumption: without the law, both groups would have followed the same trend.

09 The main result

With the law, value rose on both yardsticks

In all four models, firms in the countries that made disclosure mandatory gained value relative to firms in the countries that did not, always significant at 1%. The research hypothesis was confirmed.

11 Robustness test

Matching brought the groups closer, but did not make them equal

Firms in the two groups were different. To compare like with like, the author used propensity score matching (PSM): each observation from the countries with a law was paired with the most similar one from the other group, without repeats.

12 In practice · limits · credits

What changes for those who invest, disclose or regulate

Investors

Knowing that mandatory disclosure came with more highly valued shares helps decide where to put money.

Firms

Knowing that mandatory disclosure is associated with more value helps decide how much capital to put into sustainability reporting.

Regulators and governments

Helps assess these rules, calibrate incentives and penalties and attract foreign investment to emerging countries.

Researchers

Next steps: the IFRS S1 and S2 standards from 2026 and specific measures for each ESG dimension (emissions, staff turnover, green patents).

What this research does not say

  • Few countries meet the criterion. Only 11 emerging countries had at least five firms with an S&P credit rating. · according to the author
  • The law explains little of value. The model accounts for 1.6% of the differences in the ratio and 6.6% in return: value depends on many other things. · our note (Table 7)
  • The method's assumption is not tested in the text. The dissertation does not show whether the two groups followed the same trend before the laws, nor how the effect evolves year by year. Brazil and Chile have only two years after the law. · our note
  • Different laws, one event. "Comply or explain", phased rules and rules only for the 1,000 largest firms all count as the same change. · our note

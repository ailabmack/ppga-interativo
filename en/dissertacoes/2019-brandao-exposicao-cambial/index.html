With a free-form yardstick, almost every company in six Latin American countries seems to feel the dollar, but only lightly

When the dollar goes up or down, does a company's share price follow? The dissertation measures this in five ways for 540 companies in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico and Peru, from 2000 to 2018. The fixed-shape yardsticks (straight lines and curves) find the effect in 11% to 51% of companies, depending on the country; the free-form yardstick, in over 90%, with much smaller shocks.

02 What is measured

Exposure is how much the share moves when the dollar moves

An exporter sells in dollars: an expensive dollar means more local currency in the till. An importer buys in dollars: an expensive dollar means higher costs. The market knows this and the share price reacts. The research measures that reaction with one number, the exposure coefficient.

Exchange-rate riskBelongs to the currency: the unexpected ups and downs of the exchange rate.
Exchange-rate exposureBelongs to the company: how much its value changes with those ups and downs (Adler and Dumas, 1984).
Illustration · made-up companies

In the dissertation's reading, a positive coefficient means the share tends to rise when the dollar rises; negative, that it tends to fall. A coefficient of 0.5: a dollar 10% more expensive, a share 5% above what the market as a whole would explain.
03 The puzzle

In theory the dollar matters; in the data, it was hard to see

In 1990, a study of 287 US multinationals barely found any exchange-rate exposure. Researchers called it a puzzle. One explanation, recalled in the dissertation: companies protect themselves, and protection erases the effect in the numbers.

Tap the three shields that a study of 1,150 companies in 16 countries (Bartram, Brown and Minton, 2010) identified.

Figures from Bartram, Brown and Minton (2010), cited in the dissertation: the three protections together cut exposure by 70% on average; pass-through and operational protection, 10% to 15%; derivatives and foreign-currency debt, about 40%. 04 Appendix A · 73 articles

From almost 14,000 articles on currency hedging, one question remained: how to measure exposure?

Before the tests, the author searched three article databases from 2008 to 2018. He read 73 in full. Exchange-rate exposure was the central topic of 18 of them, and the studies did not agree on how to measure it.

06 The method

The same cloud of points, five ways to draw the relationship

Each point is a day: the currency moved this much, the share (net of the market) moved that much. The fixed yardsticks impose a shape: a line, a curve, or one line per range of movement. The free-form yardstick fits a little line in each stretch, looking only at neighboring days.

The dissertation's novelty, compared with the Aysun and Guldi (2011) study it follows: letting the data choose the width of that neighborhood, testing each width on days left out (cross-validation).

08 Comparison with Aysun and Guldi (2011)

With the same yardsticks, the original study found far fewer exposed companies

Aysun and Guldi studied 2,584 companies in six countries, from 1995 to 2006. Three countries repeat here: Brazil, Chile and Mexico. Guess: how many Brazilian companies did the free-form yardstick find exposed in that study?

For the author, two possible reasons: the period studied here had much more volatile exchange rates, with these countries' currencies losing value, and the window chosen by the data makes the free-form yardstick more sensitive. The dissertation does not test which one weighs more.

10 The unexpected

The direction of the effect, up or down, also depends on the yardstick

By the straight-line yardstick, on average, shares in Chile, Mexico and Peru fall when the dollar rises. And by the free-form yardstick?

The author's reading: the region's economies export a lot, and a weaker local currency raises what exporters receive (OECD and IDB reports cited in the dissertation). The dissertation does not separate exporters and importers to test this.

Average signed coefficients (Table 10). With the free-form yardstick, Mexico stays at 0.0001: positive, but practically zero.

12 So what?

Before deciding how to protect against the dollar, it helps to know the yardstick changes the answer

For finance managers

  • Exposure is the starting point for choosing protections: derivatives, foreign-currency debt, exporting and importing. (dissertation)
  • Measure with more than one yardstick before deciding. (our reading)

For investors

  • By the fixed yardsticks, the average sign changes from country to country; by the free-form one, it is positive and small everywhere. (screens 09 and 10)
  • Averages for 2000 to 2018 do not say what each share will do. This is not investment advice. (our note)

For researchers

  • Use the measure to look for what causes exposure: derivatives use, foreign sales, foreign-currency debt, degree of internationalization. (dissertation)
  • Study who controls the firm and crises in emerging markets. (dissertation)

For methods teachers

  • Same data, five yardsticks, different answers: an example of how the shape of a model changes the conclusion. (our reading)
  • Check whether each printed test follows from the numbers next to it. (screen 11)

What this research does not say

  • No referee between the yardsticks. The usual criteria for choosing between models (Akaike, Bayes) were not applied to the free-form yardstick. · according to the dissertation
  • Few stock markets in the region. Latin America has few developed capital markets, and the samples are small; in Peru, the index used has 3 companies. · according to the dissertation
  • Little to compare with. Few studies publish the degree of exposure, and year-by-year exchange-rate volatility was not measured. · according to the dissertation
  • The free-form yardstick's test. The printed test values do not follow from the coefficient and the standard error; by the division, 21 of 538 companies would pass at 1%. · our arithmetic, screen 11
  • Association, not cause. The coefficient shows that share and dollar move together; it does not say why nor separate exporters from importers. · our note

Did Covid make banks lend less?

During the pandemic, governments cut interest rates and released money so that credit would reach firms and households. But banks did not always follow the script. The study followed 1,153 banks in 65 countries, from 2016 to 2023, to see where lending slowed down. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem

In a crisis, the bank stands between the government and those who need credit

The bank takes money from savers and lends it to those who need it. During Covid, governments used banks to keep money flowing: lower interest rates, more liquidity, loan guarantees. But uncertainty also makes banks afraid of not being paid back.

03 The context

Covid killed proportionally more in lower-income countries

Rich countries recorded more cases. But for every 100 cases, deaths were three times more frequent in low-income countries. Emerging economies have fewer resources to protect themselves from a health crisis, and that is what led the study to separate developed and emerging countries.

World Health Organization data up to December 2024, cited in section 2.1.1; World Bank income groups. In the study, "developed" means high income and "emerging" groups the other three.

07 The method

The study compares how much each group changed after 2020

The method is called difference-in-differences (DID). Countries with Covid cases per million inhabitants at or above the median form the high-contagion group; the rest form the comparison group. The same logic compares emerging and developed countries.

illustration Made-up numbers to show the logic. The method’s premise: without Covid, both groups would have followed the same trend. In the real model, the study also holds constant the banks’ size, cash and capital, GDP growth and the quality of each country’s government.

08 Robustness test

Matching made the groups more alike

High-contagion banks were different from the others. To compare like with like, the study redid the analysis with propensity score matching (PSM): for each observation in the high-contagion group, it picked the most similar one in the comparison group, without repeats.

12 In practice · limits · credits

What changes for those who regulate, lend or invest

Governments and regulators

Low rates and easy money are not enough if banks pull back. Knowing where lending slows most (the hardest-hit and emerging countries) helps tailor measures to each country.

Banks

Spotting periods of greater uncertainty early, when they come from outside shocks, helps adjust capital before the crisis.

Investors

The different reactions of banks in each group of countries can be taken into account when diversifying portfolio risk.

Researchers

Suggested next steps: check whether who owns the bank changes its reaction, and look at other credit channels, such as receivables funds, peer-to-peer loans and bonds.

What this study does not say

  • Only listed banks. Privately held banks were left out for lack of data. · according to the author
  • Direction, not size. The coefficients in Table 5 are too large for the scale of the measure (a mean of 0.3% and a standard deviation of 9.6 points), so the page shows only which way lending went and how reliably. · our note
  • The model explains little. 3.1% of the differences in loan growth (0.8% with matching): lending depends on much more. · our note
  • The group is the country, not the bank. Every bank in a high-contagion country is in the same group, and the US has 37% of the banks. The dissertation does not show whether the groups followed the same trend before 2020. · our note

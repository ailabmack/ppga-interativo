Does knowing how to use technology help people work better?

At a Brazilian public financial institution, 356 employees in technology and in social programmes rated their own digital competence, how useful and easy they find technology, and their own performance. The dissertation measures how the three are linked and where gaps by age, gender and department appear. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The problem

Skill, usefulness and performance are three different things

Almost every study of digital competence was done in schools and universities. The author takes the question into a company and tests an idea that, as far as the author found, no one had tested: part of the effect of competence on performance would run through use of technology. 04 How the research looked

356 employees from two very different departments

The research took place at a Brazilian public financial institution, which is not named. In March and April 2025, an anonymous questionnaire went to the Information Technology department, where a lot of digital competence is expected, and to Social Programmes, which serves recipients of government programmes across the country. The HR department was also invited, but few answered and it was left out.

07 What it found · the model

All four hypotheses were confirmed

With structural equation modelling, the author tested the links all at once. People who rate their digital competence higher also find technology more useful and easy and report higher performance. The weakest link is between use and performance. Tap an arrow:

Standardised coefficients (β) of the final model, Table 35 and Figure 7 (SmartPLS 4, 10,000 resamples). Thickness ∝ β. R² = how much of the differences between people the model explains.

08 The finding

Most of the effect goes straight to performance

Digital competence is linked to performance along two paths. The direct one is worth 0.412. The one through use of technology is worth 0.066: it is significant, but it is about one seventh of the total. Before seeing the name statistics gives this, choose:

10 What it found · the digital divide

Men and IT staff rate themselves slightly higher

The dissertation did not look at access to technology but at the ability to use it, the so-called second-order digital divide. On average, men rated themselves above women, and IT above Social Programmes. Before you look, a guess: in which area was the gap between men and women largest?

11 What it found · age

Only the group aged 60 or over scored lower, and narrowly

The hypothesis was that younger people would have more digital competence. Across the groups up to age 59, the means barely change. The group aged 60 or over, with 33 people, scored below those aged 40 to 49 and 50 to 59, right at the edge of significance. Tap a group or switch the view:

12 So what?

Train those with less digital skill

For the author, the results support training programmes aimed at those with less command of technology, which also look after engagement and confidence in using it, in line with SDG 10 on reducing inequalities.

For the company

  • The adapted questionnaire can serve as a diagnosis to guide action, the author says.
  • The lowest scores were in creating content (2.37) and solving technical problems (2.79); the highest, in communicating (3.24) and staying safe (3.19).

For those who look after people

  • Training is not just teaching the tool: the author asks for attention to confidence in use.
  • Self-assessments can mislead: according to studies the author cites, men tend to overestimate and women to underestimate what they know, and stereotypes weigh on older workers.

For society

  • The research looked at the divide in the ability to use technology, not in access to it, and found it among colleagues at the same company.
  • Social Programmes serves recipients through digital channels: the competence of those who serve reaches the public.

What this research does not say

  • One company, two departments. And ages concentrated in mid-career; other sectors and organisations may differ. · according to the author
  • It is all self-assessment. Competence and performance were reported by the people themselves; the author suggests adding company indicators and peer review. · according to the author
  • A single point in time. It shows what goes together, not what causes what; the author suggests following people over time. · according to the author
  • "Use" is an opinion about technology. The measure asks whether it is useful and easy, not how much people use it. · our observation
  • Gender and department overlap. IT is 62% men and Social Programmes 60% women; each comparison looks at one factor at a time. · our observation, Table 17

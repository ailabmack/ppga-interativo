Top managers see age discrimination, but few act against it

The study talked to 16 top managers at pharmaceutical companies, people who hire, promote and dismiss. Almost all of them notice unwritten rules about the right age for each job. Most live with those rules, and even those who say “age doesn’t matter” prize the traits of the young. Explained in 11 screens.

Author: Alessandra Helena Valente Miyazaki Advisor: Prof. Darcy Mitiko Mori Hanashiro Program: Graduate Program in Business Administration (PPGA) · Mackenzie Presbyterian University

03 How the study looked · 16 interviews

The author heard 16 managers who report straight to the president

These were long conversations, mostly in each manager’s office, guided by an open script: who the older worker is, whether it is worth having one, in which areas age matters and how each manager reacts. 04 The unexpected

The managers were much younger than theory predicted

According to career timetable theory (Lawrence, 1988), top managers should average 52 to 53.5 years of age. The author found no manager over 55. The average among those who gave their age was 45.9. Switch the layers on to compare:

11 So what? Limits and credits

If top managers don’t move, who will fight age discrimination?

What the study suggests

  • For companies: put age on the diversity agenda, alongside gender and race, and review practices that discard older people on cost grounds, such as voluntary layoffs and compulsory retirement.
  • For HR: look at its own recruiting (who reaches the interviews) and create training for older staff too.
  • For top managers: the conversation already made a difference: four said they will pay more attention to the issue, and one HR head decided to take it to the board meeting.
this age question, the way you asked me, I had never thought about it.E3 · translated by us

The agenda against age discrimination is the social contribution the author argues for (section 6); the first two items are our reading of the findings.

What this study does not say

  • Perception, not measurement. The study did not measure discrimination in the companies; not noticing it does not mean it is absent. · per the author
  • One sector, 16 people. Managers chosen by access, in the pharmaceutical sector; other sectors and other levels may differ. · per the author
  • Cases sought out. The author sought more people at two companies that stood out (one very ageist, one denying everything). · section 4.3.2
  • A sensitive topic. Age discrimination is punishable by law, and managers might be cautious; three only said some things with the recorder off. · sections 4.2 and 6

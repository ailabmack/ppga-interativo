Profit, growth and cash explained only a slice of what the shares returned

Do companies that make more profit, grow more and hold more cash give more return to their shareholders? The dissertation tested the question on 609 US companies, from 2011 to 2014. All three went along with the return, but together they explained 8% to 13% of it, and which one weighed most changed from year to year. Explained in 12 screens.

02 The idea

Three questions about a company: does it make profit, grow, hold cash?

Earlier studies showed that profitability, growth and liquidity are three different sides of a company’s performance, and that companies doing well on all three return more to shareholders, in the US, India, Australia and Turkey.

The dissertation asks how much these three explain of shareholder return. Each is measured by several figures taken from the balance sheet and income statement. Tap a card to see them.

Source: sections 2 and 3.1 and Table 1. Studies cited by the dissertation: Hamann et al. (2013), Türel, Türel and Needles (2012) and Hofer and Carton (2006). The plain-language names of the figures are ours.

03 Who was included

Of 6,579 US companies, 609 had every figure in all four years

The balance sheets and income statements came from the Compustat database, 2011 to 2014. Only companies with all 15 of the study’s figures in every year stayed in, so the same group was compared each time.

05 How the research looked

The model groups the figures into three pillars and gives each a weight

The technique, called structural equation modelling, does two things at once: it sees how closely each figure moves with its pillar (the loading, from 0 to 1) and how much each pillar weighs on the share return (the weight).

One model per year. Thick line: larger weight or loading; dashed: a weight that did not pass the test. Tap a pillar to see its loadings and quality scores.

Source: Figure 2 (SmartPLS diagrams), Table 3 and Table 4. The test is the dissertation’s: resampling (bootstrap) with 1,000 draws and a t-value above 1.96.

06 What it found

All three pulled the return up, but only growth passed the test in all four years

All 12 weights (3 pillars × 4 years) came out positive. Eight passed the dissertation’s test. Profitability vanished in 2012 and 2013; liquidity in 2011 and 2013.

Source: Table 3 and Table 11 (bootstrap t-values). Standardised weight (β): how far the return moves, in standard deviations, when the pillar moves one standard deviation. Our count.

07 The unexpected

The pillar that weighed most changed from one year to the next

If profit, growth and cash were a fixed recipe for returns, the same ingredient would lead every year. That is not what happened.

Source: Table 3 (the largest β each year). Striped bars: weights that did not pass the test. Our reading: in 2014 the three weights were close (0.20, 0.17 and 0.15), and the dissertation does not test whether one is larger than another.

08 The finding

Together, the three explained 8% to 13% of the return: the rest was down to other things

R² says how much of the ups and downs of the return, from one company to another, the model manages to track. For the dissertation, the three pillars have a small relationship with the return: a model with only three dimensions leaves out important variables.

This research is not investment advice: it describes the past of 609 companies, without simulating buying or selling shares.

Source: Table 3 (R² of shareholder return). The introduction mentions 10% to 14% and the conclusion 8% to 12%; we follow the table.

09 First check

Taking company size into account barely changed the picture

Large companies might return differently from small ones. The dissertation added revenue to the model, as a measure of size, to see whether the pillars kept their weight.

10 Second check

One year’s figures did not foretell the next year’s return

Perhaps the market took time to react: 2011 profit would show up in the 2012 return. The dissertation tested this and found no stable relationship. R² fell and the weights switched sign from one year to the next.

12 So what?

Profit, growth and cash count, but they do not tell the whole story

For those who run companies

  • All three pillars went along with the return, and growth was there in all four years. (screen 06)
  • No single indicator sums up the value created for shareholders; the dissertation argues for measuring performance on several dimensions at once. (dissertation)

For investors

  • Share returns depend heavily on expectations about the future, not only on balance-sheet figures. (dissertation)
  • One year’s figures did not foretell the next year’s return in this sample. (screen 10)
  • This is not investment advice.

For researchers

  • Expand the model with other financial measures and with strategic and operational variables. (dissertation)
  • Compare the three-pillar model with Hofer and Carton’s full model. (dissertation)

What this research does not say

  • Only three dimensions. Efficiency, leverage and other measures were left out. · according to the dissertation
  • Only US-listed companies with complete data. 609 of 6,579; it does not automatically apply to private companies or other countries. · according to the dissertation
  • Return is expectation. Share returns reflect what investors expect of the future. · according to the dissertation
  • Some figures barely tracked their pillar. Operating cash flow growth had loadings from 0.05 to 0.45; return on invested capital was near zero in 2011 and 2012; and 2014 liquidity fell short of the minimum reliability. · Figure 2 and Table 4; our reading
  • Moving together is not causing. The weights show association within the same year, not that profit causes the return. · our observation

Master's dissertation · PPGA Mackenzie · 2020

Multinational branches run dozens of diversity initiatives, but half reach inclusion for no group at all

Eleven diversity managers from 8 branches went through, one initiative at a time, what their companies do for women, Black people, people with disabilities and LGBT+ people. Programs run 42 to 80 initiatives, nearly the same from company to company, and most stop at recruiting and talks. Real inclusion shows up in only a few.

02 The question

Diversity is the party. Where does inclusion come in?

For 30 years multinationals have talked about diversity management: what a company does to attract, keep and develop people from groups that are usually left out. In the interviews, every manager talked a lot about inclusion. But what counts as inclusion? One of them answered with an image that circulates in the field:

A lot of people say that “diversity is the party and inclusion is asking someone to dance”. I think it's a bit backwards. I think diversity is the party, but inclusion is when you put together the guest list.E3, translated by us

The dissertation uses this conversation to ask: what do Brazilian branches of multinationals actually do, and how much of it is inclusion?

04 How the study looked

Eight branches, eleven managers and a list of 106 initiatives

The author chose branches of multinationals with diversity initiatives known in the media and from different industries. Each company got the name of a Greek letter. He used the ethnographic interview: instead of testing hypotheses, stepping into the world of people who know the subject from the inside, with questions that describe (“how did it start?”), organize (“what kinds of initiatives are there?”) and compare (“what's the difference between X and Y?”).

06 Study 2 · 8 branches

Programs run 42 to 80 initiatives, and those higher up have more

The author expected that, as a company climbed, it would swap simple initiatives for more mature ones. That's not what he saw: old initiatives remain in use for the less served groups, and new ones pile on. Only Capa, with the longest program, sits one step below Lambda.

07 Study 2 · 106 initiatives

Companies in different industries do almost the same things

No manager knew what the others had answered, and the branches range from animal health to consulting. Even so, the lists look very alike. On average, 77% of a branch's initiatives also existed in at least four others. The author calls this mimicry: copying what the market already considers right.

Who spreads the same recipes? Managers named more than 50 consultancies, colleges, NGOs and company networks specialized in each group.

08 Study 2 · average of the 8 branches

Plenty of publicity and talks, little care for those already hired

The most adopted kinds of initiative speak to the outside: communication, job ads, awareness events. The least adopted ones touch the structure: retaining, promoting, selecting without bias, giving people a voice and holding leaders accountable. For the author, programs mainly serve the company's image and its employer brand.

      09 The finding

      Half of the branches reach inclusion for none of the four groups

      The author rated each group within each branch. Only Gama and Lambda treat all four on the same step. Iota and Capa have three different steps at once. And Alfa, Beta, Delta and Épsilon don't reach step 3, the first that includes, for any group.

      12 So what?

      Asking who is left out matters more than adding initiatives

      Use the list as a mirrorThe author suggests using the 106-initiative questionnaire as a check-up, to see which groups are falling behind within the same company.
      After hiringHiring without retaining or promoting becomes an endless cycle. Retention, careers, reviews of appraisals and diversity goals tied to executive bonuses are missing.
      Who benefits?For the author, every policy should face the question “which people gain from this and which are left out?”, without treating a group as if everyone in it were the same.
      A voice for those affectedInstead of only bringing in outside experts, make room for people in these groups to propose initiatives, and expect everyone else to do their part.

      What the study doesn't say

      • Only one side. Managers were heard, not the people the initiatives are meant to benefit. (author)
      • Showcase companies. Branches were chosen for having well-known programs; the overall picture of multinationals in Brazil may be worse. (author)
      • A snapshot. Interviews are from 2019 and 2020, and many programs were recent. (author)
      • The yardstick is the author's. He assigned the step of each initiative, group and branch, using the staircase he proposed; eight cases don't allow generalization. (our note)

      Next steps suggested by the author: repeat the study in Brazilian companies, follow the same branches over time and apply the initiative list as a survey to many more managers.
